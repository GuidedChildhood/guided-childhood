# Mechanism, not mandate, how we use weak evidence without becoming a fear merchant

**Date:** 18 August 2026. **For:** Justin, the content strategy, and a durable guardrail.
British English, no dashes anywhere. Held to the Odgers test. This is a position, and a self audit.

## The question that prompted this

Are we guilty of using Jonathan Haidt's tactics, scaring people, telling them how bad platforms
like Roblox are, when our whole ethos is that we educate rather than ban. Honest answer, partly, and
the cybertrauma series is where it showed. This document fixes it, turns the fix into the sharpest
thing we have said, and leaves a test so it does not happen again.

## The honest self audit

You can disagree with every one of Haidt's conclusions and still copy his method without noticing.
His method is not the ban. His method is the order of operations. Lead with the scariest single
child. Let the reader feel the alarm before they meet the evidence. Name a platform as the villain.
Then, only then, offer the response. By the time the evidence arrives, the emotion has already
decided.

Our cybertrauma series landed its conclusions in the right place, every post finished on
preparation and an askable adult, not a ban. But the register borrowed Haidt's engine. "A seven
year old traumatised." "Squid Game." "Things inside Roblox." The hook did the frightening, and the
education arrived late. And we never once said out loud the two sentences that separate us from him.
Sixteen children is not a basis for policy. And we are not here to demonise the platform. This
document says both, plainly, and threads them back through the work.

## The insight, the rule that actually separates us

Here is the principle, and it is genuinely sharp, because it lets us act on weak evidence without
ever becoming fear merchants.

**The strength of evidence you need scales with the cost and the reversibility of your response.**

- **Weak, emerging evidence** (a mechanism seen in sixteen children, a small qualitative study, an
  association that explains a fraction of a percent) licenses only **low regret responses**. Teach
  it. Prepare the child. Train the safeguarding staff to recognise it. Commission the replication.
  Every one of those is cheap, reversible, rights neutral, and useful even if the harm turns out to
  be rare. You lose nothing by being wrong.
- **Strong, causal, replicated evidence** is what you need before a **high regret response**. A
  ban. Criminalisation. Walling a platform off. Those are expensive, rights affecting, evasion
  prone, and actively harmful if the evidence was thin. You lose a great deal by being wrong.

Haidt's error is not that he cares. It is that he reaches for the high regret response on the
strength of the low regret evidence. Maximal restrictive policy, built on findings that are mostly
correlational and modest in size. He inverts the rule. That is why his critics, Odgers, Orben,
Przybylski, keep landing the same blow, and it is why his approach remains, to us, unconvincing,
even though he named a real worry.

We invert nothing. We match the response to the evidence. And here is the part that makes education
not just defensible but correct.

**Education is the only response that is safe to be wrong about.** If the harm turns out to be big,
we prepared the child for it. If the harm turns out to be small, we still handed them judgement they
will use for the rest of their lives. There is no version of the future in which teaching a child to
understand the machine was the wrong call. A ban has a wrong version, the eight in ten who climb
over it, the child pushed underground, the lifeline cut. Education has none. That asymmetry is the
whole company.

## Can you make policy on low numbers

This is the real question underneath Justin's, and the honest answer is a distinction, not a yes or
a no.

You cannot make **restrictive** policy on low numbers. Sixteen children cannot justify a ban, a
criminal offence, or a platform wall, and anyone who tries is doing the thing we criticise.

You can, and should, make **preparatory** policy on low numbers. A mechanism shown in sixteen
children is a perfectly good reason to teach a child what they might meet, to train a teacher to
recognise a trauma response, and to fund the bigger study. Knibbs herself asks for exactly this
proportion, recognise it, train for it, commission replication at scale. She does not ask for a ban.
Neither do we. That is the tell that her work and ours belong together, and neither belongs with the
prohibition camp.

So the line is not "act" versus "do not act." It is "prepare" versus "prohibit." Weak evidence is a
mandate to prepare and a prohibition on prohibiting.

## On the platforms, Roblox included

Roblox has real problems. Leaky age gates, litigation, content a young child should not meet. We can
say that, because it is true. What we must never do is what the ban camp does with it, turn the
platform into a monster and imply the answer is to keep the child away from it. Two reasons.

First, contact is inevitable. Every child will meet these platforms, at a friend's house, on a
sibling's device, at sixteen when the delay lifts. A childhood spent walled off from the machine
produces a sixteen year old who meets all of it at once with no habits and no judgement. The
platform being imperfect is the argument for preparation, not against contact.

Second, demonising the platform teaches the child nothing, and makes it forbidden fruit. The child
who is taught how the machine works, why it serves them the next thing, and how to step back from
the pull, is safer inside Roblox than the child who was simply told Roblox is bad. Our quarrel is
never with the platform. It is with the idea that a gate is a substitute for a prepared child.

## What this means for the work, threaded through

Every research led piece, this series and the next, must pass through this frame:

- **Use the finding to teach, never to frighten.** The study is a reason to prepare a child, not a
  reason to scare a parent. If the post frightens more than it equips, it failed, whatever its
  conclusion.
- **Say the numbers out loud.** If a study is sixteen children, say sixteen children, and say what
  that can and cannot support. Transparency about weak evidence is the opposite of Haidt, and it is
  our credibility.
- **Never make a platform the villain.** Name its real faults, then turn immediately to the child
  and the habit. The villain framing is his, not ours.
- **Match the ask to the evidence.** Weak evidence, prepare. Strong evidence, and only then,
  consider a stronger response. Never a ban off a mechanism.
- **Keep Haidt as the honest contrast.** He named the worry, and he reaches too far. We share the
  worry, and we respond in proportion. That contrast, stated fairly, is our position in one move.

## The pre publish test, are we becoming Haidt

Run this on every research led post before it ships. Any yes is a rewrite.

1. Does the hook frighten before it informs, or make a parent feel alarm before evidence?
2. Is a platform cast as the villain, rather than named honestly and then set aside for the child?
3. Does the piece imply the answer is less contact, a ban, a wall, rather than more preparation?
4. Have we hidden or skated over how weak or small the evidence is?
5. Does the reader finish more frightened, or more capable? (If more frightened, it failed.)

The Haidt test sits on top of the hidden thread and the Odgers test, never instead of them. The
hidden thread decides what the read is for. The Odgers test decides whether the evidence holds. This
test decides whether we used it like ourselves or like him.

## The one line

We use the evidence to prepare a child, never to frighten a parent or forbid a platform. Mechanism
informs education. Only strong evidence informs prohibition. That is the whole difference, and it is
the whole company.
