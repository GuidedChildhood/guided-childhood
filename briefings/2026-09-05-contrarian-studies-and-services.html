60 Second Summary

The first real evaluations of Australia's under 16 ban are in, and the model the UK is copying moved platform use from about 86% of under 16s to about 81% in three months, left daily use statistically unchanged, and pushed it out of parents' sight: the share of parents who did not know their child was on social media rose ten points. Use went up among 16 year olds, the cliff edge our passport exists to remove. Meanwhile the strongest new science keeps moving our way: risk lives in feeds, patterns and vulnerable children, not average hours, and a child's own social media effect flips within six months for half of adolescents, which is the research case for tracking patterns over time rather than delivering one verdict. Two discipline notes from the Skeptic: nobody can yet say the ban failed any more than it worked, because the headline numbers on both sides are weak, and several famous statistics in this debate are folklore. The single most important fact for the business is quieter than any of it: statutory RSHE online safety teaching became compulsory in every English school on 1 September 2026 with no delivery budget attached, in the same window that US settlement money started earmarking funds for digital literacy. A mandated buyer and a funding precedent arrived in our launch window. Every finding in this briefing lands on a service we have already built.

Key Findings

Australia's ban barely moved use, and its first measurable effect was less parental visibility

Reliability 8/10

The regulator's own three month evaluation (803 children aged 10 to 15) found platform use fell from about 86% to 81% and daily use from about 60% to 58%, while the share of parents unaware their child used social media rose ten percentage points. The independent BMJ evaluation found the same shape: 85.9% before, 81% after, minimal change offline, and increased use among 16 year olds. 37% of children simply changed their profile birthdate. Use did not stop, it relocated and went quiet.

Supported: Child, Academic, Historian, Economist · Challenged: Skeptic (early, no control group; do not say "failed")
Sources: S1, S2, S4

Statutory RSHE online safety teaching became compulsory in England on 1 September 2026, with no delivery budget

Reliability 9/10

The updated RSHE statutory guidance is mandatory in English schools from the start of this term, and the Lords debated the missing delivery funding in March 2026. In the same window, US state settlements with Meta began earmarking funds for digital literacy programmes over ten years. A legally mandated buyer with no budget line, plus the first funded precedent for our category, both landed inside our launch window. Only preparation products can invoice the mandate.

Supported: Economist, Historian · Challenged: none
Sources: S24, S25

Risk lives in the pathway, the feed and the vulnerable child, not in average hours

Reliability 8/10

OxWell (32,102 UK pupils, Nature Mental Health, August 2026): 34.5% saw self harm content in the past month, and 66.2% of those met it through an algorithmic feed rather than searching, so exposure is a delivery pathway problem, not an hours problem. Fassi and colleagues (Nature Human Behaviour, 3,340 UK adolescents): teens with diagnosed internalising conditions report social comparison at 48%, roughly double their peers, so how a child uses is the signal. The biggest deactivation trial ever run (over 35,000 adults) found tiny average gains that concentrated in specific subgroups. Averages hide the story every time.

Supported: Clinician, Academic, Economist · Challenged: Skeptic (cross sectional, direction unproven)
Sources: S8, S9, S10

The cliff edge is real: prohibition until a birthday produces a worse arrival

Reliability 8/10

Three lenses converged on this from different literatures. The BMJ evaluation found use increased among 16 year olds after Australia's ban. The graduated driver licensing literature found teens who delay licensing to 18 to skip restrictions crash worst, while Queensland's graduated system cut crashes 13.1% a year in a state where the licence age never moved: the graduation mechanism does the work, not the age line. And the forbidden fruit literature shows restrictive age labels increase attraction from about age nine. Children denied practice arrive at the legal age with more appetite and no skills. This is the passport argument stated by the evidence itself.

Supported: Academic, Historian, Child · Challenged: Skeptic (16+ rise is one survey wave; watch replication)
Sources: S2, S26, S27, S28

Ban compliance is a coordination problem: only 27% comply, and 52% stay because their friends stayed

Reliability 7/10

Bursztyn, Duckworth, Sunstein and colleagues (NBER, April 2026) studied 239 Australian teens: about 27% of banned 14 to 15 year olds complied, over half of non compliers cited friends still being there, and the teens who did comply reported more boredom and difficulty keeping up with friendships. An individual family's rule fails for the same reason: the cost of leaving is personal while the group remains. The strongest answer is cohort level change, a class or a year group moving through stages together.

Supported: Child, Economist · Challenged: Skeptic (working paper, n of 239, not yet peer reviewed)
Sources: S3

Surveillance style monitoring backfires; open, joint, conversational approaches are what hold

Reliability 7/10

Meta's own unsealed research (Project MYST) found parental supervision tools barely matter and life stress drives compulsive use. MIT Technology Review called monitoring apps a failed paradigm in August 2026, with about 1 in 5 teens feeling surveilled and controlling monitoring predicting hiding. An analysis of 75 commercial parental control apps found most overly restrictive and privacy invasive, and the research field is moving to autonomy based approaches. Meanwhile Ofcom finds 41% of children have tried to bypass controls and vendor grade efficacy collapses through the teen years. The market keeps selling watching; the evidence keeps rewarding talking.

Supported: Clinician, Child, Economist · Challenged: Skeptic (efficacy decay figures are vendor grade, directional only)
Sources: S11, S12, S13, S23

A child's social media effect is not stable: it flips within six months for half of adolescents

Reliability 8/10

The strongest within person design available (297 adolescents, 126 momentary surveys per wave, two waves six months apart, Child Development 2025) found individual effects of social media on wellbeing, self esteem and friendship closeness stayed stable for only 32 to 51% of adolescents. A one off reading of whether social media is good or bad for a particular child expires. Separately, Odgers' group found morning low mood predicts same day passive phone use rather than the reverse, so mood often leads and phones follow.

Supported: Academic, Clinician · Challenged: none material (design is the field's best)
Sources: S14, S15

Enforced, bounded friction delivers where platform bans and paper policies do not

Reliability 7/10

Norway's enforced school day phone restrictions (477 middle schools, now peer reviewed in the Journal of Human Resources) cut girls' specialist mental healthcare visits sharply, reduced bullying in both sexes and lifted low SES girls' grades. A two week trial blocking mobile internet on the device (calls and texts kept) improved attention, mental health and wellbeing. Yet England's SMART Schools study found restrictive school policies changed nothing, because they did not reduce total daily use, and managing phones costs an average UK school over 100 staff hours a week. The pattern: friction that is enforced, bounded and environmental works; rules on paper and platform level bans do not.

Supported: Academic, Clinician · Challenged: Skeptic (Norwegian timing recalled by principals; 2010 to 2018 classrooms are not 2026 TikTok)
Sources: S16, S17, S18, S19

Children asked for feature level fixes and feared exclusion; they got a ban anyway

Reliability 6/10

In the UK Government's own consultation (116,211 responses, about 14,000 from children), 62% of responding children said restricting high risk features would make them safer, and 72% worried about exclusion if restrictions came in. The ban was announced three weeks after it closed. ReachOut found parents rank social media their number one concern while young people rank it 24th among their own, below cost of living, study stress and loneliness, and 57% of 12 to 15 year olds say they use platforms to cope with worries. The gap between what adults fear and what children report is itself a finding, and removal is also removal of a coping tool.

Supported: Child · Challenged: Skeptic (consultation self selected; ReachOut not peer reviewed)
Sources: S5, S6

The ban misses the cohort where use is growing fastest: preschool and primary

Reliability 8/10

Ofcom's 2026 children's media report: 40% of UK under 13s have a social media profile, 37% of parents of 3 to 5 year olds say their child uses at least one social media app (up from 29% in 2023), and 19% of 3 to 5s use it independently. The Centre for Social Justice estimates around 800,000 UK under 5s on social media. A Spring 2027 teen ban does not touch any of this. The only instrument that reaches a four year old is a parent, which is exactly where Stages 1 and 2 sit, and where almost nobody else's product or policy does.

Supported: Child, Clinician · Challenged: none (regulator data, though parent report)
Sources: S7, S20

One in five US adolescents now takes mental health advice from AI chatbots, and most tell nobody

Reliability 7/10

RAND (JAMA, June 2026): 19.2% of US 12 to 21 year olds use AI chatbots for mental health advice, and 63.3% of those tell no one. Problematic chatbot use has begun appearing as a named presentation in child psychiatry clinics. While the ban debate looks backwards at social media, the next pathway is already open, unsupervised and invisible to parents. Our child app's zero AI rule, with every model call on the parent's side, is no longer a quiet engineering choice, it is a safety position with evidence behind it.

Supported: Clinician · Challenged: Skeptic (US sample; UK prevalence unmeasured)
Sources: S21, S22

Both camps' flagship numbers are contested, and several famous statistics are folklore

Reliability 8/10

Ferguson's null meta analysis is peer reviewed and under formal audit; the audit lives on an advocacy blog. The specification curve behind the famous potatoes comparison was criticised in a Royal Statistical Society journal for averaging away real subgroup effects. Part of the rise in measured teen distress may be measurement artefact (prevalence inflation, items drifting in meaning between cohorts). And three widely repeated numbers will get any post killed: teens do not spend 9 hours a day on social media (that was 2015 total entertainment media; Gallup puts social media at 4.8 hours), the Instagram 1 in 3 body image figure is an internal Meta slide about girls already struggling, not a prevalence estimate, and UK teen suicide base rates are too small to carry any trend claim in either direction.

Supported: Skeptic, Academic, Historian · Challenged: none (this is the discipline layer)
Sources: S29, S30, S31, S32

The scientific mainstream and the regulatory mainstream are both drifting toward graduation

Reliability 6/10

A May 2026 Frontiers consensus style paper argues bans lack evidence and may backfire, echoing the Science Media Centre and Cambridge expert reaction to the UK announcement. The EU is preparing a gradual access proposal rather than a hard ban. CAMHS pressure keeps rising (over a million active referrals, 128 day average waits) while spending grows 2% against 10% demand growth, which is why prevention keeps winning the economic argument. The centre of gravity in both science and policy is moving from the age line to the staircase. We built the staircase first.

Supported: Academic, Historian, Economist, Clinician · Challenged: Skeptic (consensus papers are position pieces, not data)

Six perspectives

Lens Panels

The Clinician

The clinic and the headlines have swapped places

Referrals passed a million with 128 day waits, yet the newest clinical evidence says mood often leads and phones follow, exposure arrives by algorithm rather than by hours, and device removal appears nowhere in the self harm evidence base. What families need is a pattern reader and a calibrated pathway, which is what we built. The next presenting problem is already AI chatbots, not feeds.

The Academic

The best designs keep answering: it depends on the child, and the month

Within person methods now exist, and they return heterogeneity, instability and subgroups: effects flip for half of adolescents in six months, vulnerable teens use differently, and the banned model's own evaluation shows use rising at the cliff edge of 16. Preparation and repeated reading of one child beat any single verdict.

The Skeptic

Nobody can yet say the ban worked or failed, and that protects us

The 4.7 million account figure has no denominator; the 70% circumvention figure is a survivorship biased advocacy survey; the VPN surges are vendor marketing confounded by adult age checks. Say "there is no evidence it delivered what parents were promised" and nothing stronger. Our own favourite numbers get the same treatment: never quote the Florida happiness study or the Sapien smartphone age data.

The Economist

The mandate arrived before the budget, and that gap is the business

England made online safety teaching compulsory this term with no delivery money, schools already burn 100 staff hours a week managing phones, CAMHS demand outruns spending five to one, and settlement money is starting to fund digital literacy. Every force pushes schools toward a cheap, ready made, evidence honest provider. That is the schools product's moment.

The Historian

After every panic, the winner is the trusted certifier

Comics got a code, games got ESRB ratings that parents still check, driving got graduation, and each hollow credential died while the behaviour anchored ones survived. Queensland cut crashes 13.1% a year without moving the age line. The durable artefact of this panic will be a verifiable, behaviour anchored credential worded as aspiration, never as restriction. That is the passport, provided the stage checks measure practice.

The Child

The ban's first effect was hiding, and nobody asked us anyway

Eighty one percent are still on, they got there by lying about a birthday rather than using a VPN, the group chats moved to less regulated places, and parents know less than before. Children asked for safer features and feared exclusion; they rank social media 24th among their own worries and most say they use it to cope. The real choice is prepared and visible versus unprepared and hidden.

So what do we do

What This Means for Guided Childhood

  • Schools Lead every school conversation with the mandate, not the ban. RSHE online safety teaching is compulsory in England from this term with no delivery budget, phones already cost a school 100 staff hours a week, and the packs are zero prep with no pupil data. The pitch writes itself: the law now requires what we hand you finished.
  • Passport The cliff edge is now our best evidenced argument. Use rises at 16 in the banned model, delayed drivers crash worst, and forbidden fruit labelling increases attraction from age nine. Keep every certificate and passport line aspiration forward ("has completed the preparation"), never restriction shaped, and let the graduation mechanism carry the story.
  • Wellbeing tracker Sell the re read, not the verdict. Individual effects flip within six months for half of adolescents, and morning mood predicts use more than the reverse. The pattern tracker's job is exactly this: repeated, gentle readings of one child over time. That is now a peer reviewed product justification.
  • DiGi Never allow or deny is what the evidence orders, not just our taste. Surveillance monitoring predicts hiding, open joint mediation holds, and children endorsed feature level, conversational fixes in the government's own consultation. DiGi's calibrated pathway is the built form of the approach the literature keeps rewarding.
  • Stages 1 and 2 Own the cohort no policy reaches. 40% of under 13s have profiles, 19% of 3 to 5s use social media independently, and the ban touches none of it. Early years content and the co watching adventures are the only instrument in the market aimed where the growth actually is. Say so in marketing.
  • Family agreement Answer the coordination problem at cohort level. Bans fail because friends stay; 52% of non compliers say so. A class or year group moving through stages together (the school to home bridge, the shared home codes) beats any individual family's rule. Build school cohort framing into the parent night materials.
  • Child app Make zero AI on the child's side a named safety promise. One in five US adolescents takes mental health advice from chatbots and most tell nobody. "No AI ever talks to your child; every model call happens on the parent's side, once, stored" is a proof path claim no big platform can copy.
  • Sleep and screen balance Keep friction environmental and bounded. What works in trials is enforced, bounded friction: protected windows, the device night rules, patterns not hours. What fails is policy on paper and platform bans. Our sleep protections and protected windows sit on the working side of that line; keep them there and cite it.
  • Scripts Write the concern gap into the scripts library. Parents rank social media first; children rank it 24th and most say they use it to cope. Scripts that open with the child's actual worry (friends, school stress, boredom) before the platform will land where lectures cannot. Commission a small set on "when the ban comes up at the dinner table".
  • Marketing Hold the Skeptic's line in every post. Never say the ban failed, never quote the 9 hours figure, the Instagram 1 in 3 slide, the Florida happiness study, the Sapien data or a teen suicide trend. The defensible sentence is: there is no evidence the ban delivered what parents were promised, and the first thing it measurably changed was how much parents know. Bans alone are not enough, and preparation does not have to wait for the verdict.
Honest limits

Assumptions and the Missing Lens

This briefing rests on

Early evaluations: the Australian evidence is three months of a multi year policy, without control groups, and could yet turn. Several load bearing figures reached us through search summaries because primary PDFs (eSafety, Ofcom, the Molly Rose briefing, NBER, the Commons Library) were blocked from this environment; the verification pass fetches what it can and the ledger below records the outcome per source. Working papers (Bursztyn, Allcott) are not yet peer reviewed. The heterogeneity thesis, risk lives in patterns and vulnerable children, is our own thesis, and we have weighted evidence consistent with it; the Skeptic lens was instructed to attack that bias and its survivors are marked. Adult samples (deactivation, abstinence, internet blocking trials) are generalised to adolescents by inference, not data.

The missing seventh lens

The Teacher. England's mandate makes classroom teachers the delivery workforce for online safety education, unfunded and untrained, and the 100 staff hours figure suggests they are already the shock absorber. A teacher lens would tell us what is actually deliverable in a Tuesday afternoon PSHE slot, what a non specialist can teach with confidence, and what makes a pack get used twice rather than once. It would directly shape the schools product and is worth running as a V3 before the autumn school pitch cycle.
