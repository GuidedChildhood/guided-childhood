# The Italian study, Haidt's framing, and where we stand

**Date:** 12 August 2026. **For:** Justin, and the comment replies and posts that follow.
British English, no dashes anywhere, held to the Odgers test (defensible to a hostile expert).

The After Babel post promotes a new Italian study (Gui, Respi, Abbiati and colleagues,
Nature Human Behaviour, August 2026) as fresh causal backing for the phones cause harm
thesis. Justin's instinct was that it looked biased and inaccurate. Here is the honest
finding, because it matters that we get this one right.

## The headline, said plainly

Do not attack the study. Attack the framing. The study itself is one of the better built
things in this whole field, and the authors are careful and heavily hedged. Haidt and
Rausch are the ones who stretch it past what it can carry. If we rubbish a solid,
peer reviewed Nature paper just because Haidt cited it, we lose the one thing that makes us
different, that we are the calm, evidence honest voice. So the move is surgical. Grant the
study its real weight, name its genuine limits fairly, and separate it hard from the three
places the After Babel post overclaims. And then the twist that actually helps us: read
straight, this study supports our position, not the ban everything one.

## 1. What the study actually is

- Gui, Respi, Abbiati et al, "Longitudinal effect of early social media use on standardized
  learning outcomes during school career," Nature Human Behaviour, 2026. DOI
  10.1038/s41562-026-02522-4. Preprint on Research Square (rs-6785728).
- 5,227 students, 28 schools, northern Italy. A 2023 questionnaire on when each child first
  opened a social media account, record linked to Italy's national INVALSI standardized
  tests at grades 2, 5, 8 and 10 in Italian, maths and English.
- Design: difference in differences plus statistical matching. Because they have test scores
  from before and after a child opened an account, they compare the change over time for
  early openers (grades 6 to 8) against late openers (grade 9 or later), after matching the
  groups on prior scores, family background and parental education.
- Finding: opening an account in grade 6 rather than grade 9 or later tracks with about 0.22
  standard deviations lower Italian and 0.18 lower maths by grade 8, widening to 0.22 Italian
  and 0.27 maths by grade 10. A dose response gradient, the earlier the account, the bigger
  the gap. And no effect at all on English.

This is a genuinely strong observational design for the question, better than the
correlational studies that make up most of the harm literature. Say so. It buys us
credibility when we then point at the real limits.

## 2. The genuine holes, held fairly

These are real, but note, the authors concede most of them themselves. That is the tell that
the study is honest and the framing is not.

1. **The exposure is a memory.** The whole thing hangs on a 15 or 16 year old correctly
   recalling the school grade they first opened an account, up to five years earlier, on a
   questionnaire built for this study with no published validation. If the memory error is
   random it only weakens the effect. If it tilts with current performance, say a struggling
   student misremembers, the result bends in an unknown direction. UK experts at the Science
   Media Centre flagged exactly this, the ad hoc instrument and the recall bias.
2. **Matching removes what you can see, not what you cannot.** Difference in differences
   cancels out any fixed difference between the groups. It does not cancel out a hidden trait
   that shows up in early adolescence and pushes a child both toward an early account and
   toward slipping grades, think ADHD traits, low conscientiousness, a home with less
   structure. The authors admit it, they cannot rule out unobserved factors like family
   dynamics or personality that express in early adolescence. This is the big one. What kind
   of home lets an eleven year old open Instagram, and is that the real driver.
3. **Only two data points before the account.** Parallel trends is the load bearing
   assumption, and they defend it with just grade 2 and grade 5. Enough to draw a straight
   line, not enough to catch a divergence that starts after grade 5 for reasons that have
   nothing to do with social media. They concede they cannot rule that out.
4. **The English result quietly breaks the simple story.** If early social media wrecked
   attention across the board, English should fall too. It does not move. The authors rescue
   it with incidental exposure, kids absorb English from the content. Plausible, but it is a
   post hoc save, offered after the fact for whichever subject happened to come up null. It
   pushes the mechanism away from a clean attention fragmentation story toward something
   messier.
5. **COVID sits inside the window.** These 2007 to 2008 cohorts hit lockdown school closures
   right in the middle of the treatment years. The authors argue it is a shared shock that
   cancels out. It only cancels if early and late adopters were pushed into screens equally,
   and if lockdown deepened the early adopters more, COVID contaminates the result. No formal
   check on that interaction is visible in the reachable materials.
6. **Multiple comparisons.** Three subjects by three age group contrasts is up to twelve
   tests, and the reachable materials do not show how or whether the p values were corrected.
   Against that, the dose response gradient is harder to explain by chance, so this one is a
   caution, not a kill.

Net on the study alone: a credible, above average brick for the claim that delaying early
access helps academic outcomes. Probable, not proven, at the stated size. Which is roughly
where the authors themselves land.

## 3. Where Haidt's framing outruns the data, the three overreaches

This is the actual target. Three specific moves in the After Babel post go past the paper.

1. **The TikTok speculation dressed as a finding.** They write that if the study were
   repeated today on Gen Alpha, they expect even larger effects. That is a guess with no data
   behind it. The study's cohort opened accounts around 2020, before the short form video
   wave. Projecting a bigger harm forward is exactly the correlation to prophecy move that
   gets the whole field in trouble. It is presented next to the real result as if it shared
   its weight. It does not.
2. **The category swap, test scores smuggled in to back a mental health claim.** After Babel
   is a mental health argument, an epidemic of teenage mental illness. This study does not
   measure mental health at all. It measures maths and reading test scores. Using an academic
   outcomes paper to reinforce the mental illness thesis is a quiet swap of one claim for
   another. They are different questions with different evidence, and the strongest skeptics,
   Odgers, Orben, Przybylski, are strongest precisely on the mental health side, where the
   population level effects are tiny.
3. **The confident causal voice over the authors' hedge.** The post leans on the pre trends
   check to read the result as good as causal, while the authors themselves say they cannot
   categorically exclude confounding and cannot rule out that differences emerged later.
   Haidt's certainty is louder than the paper's.

## 4. The wider evidence, so we are not caught one sided

The honest state of the field, two things are true at once.

- **Population wide, the average effect is small.** Orben and Przybylski (Nature Human
  Behaviour 2019), screen use explains at most about 0.4 percent of the variance in teen
  wellbeing, an effect they benchmark as smaller than wearing glasses. Vuorre and Przybylski
  (2024), 2.4 million people across 168 countries, no consistent smartphone harm signal
  globally. The National Academies (NASEM 2024) consensus, the research does not support
  social media causing mental health change at the population level.
- **But harm concentrates, and timing matters.** Orben, Blakemore and colleagues (Nature
  Communications 2022) found windows of developmental sensitivity, roughly 11 to 13 for
  girls and 14 to 15 for boys, where higher use predicts lower life satisfaction a year on.
  NASEM lists real harms, displacement of sleep and reading and time, problematic use,
  attention interference. The signal is real for a vulnerable minority, in specific windows,
  by pattern of use, not for everyone by the hour.

The Italian study fits the second bullet, not Haidt's maximal first one. An early access,
developmental window, academic displacement effect is exactly what the careful middle already
predicts. That is why it is not a threat to us. It is closer to our thesis than to his.

## 5. What this means for us, and how our service answers it

Here is the part that turns the whole thing to our advantage. Read straight, the study argues
for what we already do.

- **It is an argument for delay, and delay is our on ramp.** The paper's own policy line is
  that postponing early access may help. That is the Readiness Pathway, the staged 4 to 16
  road, aimed at the delay movement. The study hands us a reason to delay that is not fear,
  it is maths scores.
- **But the authors go further, and so do we.** They say plainly that delay alone is not
  enough, that design and digital education are complementary, not alternatives. That is our
  exact three part position. We are the education and the preparation half they say is
  missing. A ban delays the apps, we build the judgement.
- **The mechanism they land on is our balance report, not a hours counter.** Their strongest
  mediator was smartphone pervasiveness, the phone bleeding into study, meals, sleep, sport,
  the fragmentation of attention. That is pattern, not total hours. Our balance report reads
  exactly that, the shape of use, not the size of it. The study is evidence for the thing we
  built.
- **The honest voice is the differentiator.** Every other operator will either weaponise this
  study as proof the sky is falling, or ignore it. We do the rare third thing, we grant it,
  we bound it, and we show it points at preparation. That calm is the brand.

## 6. Lines Justin can actually use, defensible in public

Short, true, and they hold under a hostile reply.

- "It is a good study, and it says something quieter than the headline. Opening an account at
  eleven rather than fourteen tracked with lower maths and reading scores, and the authors are
  careful to say they cannot fully rule out that the kind of home that allows it is part of
  the story."
- "Notice it found nothing for English, which is the tell. If social media just broke
  attention, English would drop too. It did not. The picture is more about displacing reading
  and maths practice than a brain being rewired."
- "It measures test scores, not mental health. Worth holding those apart. On the mental health
  question the population evidence is genuinely weak. On the delay early, teach early
  question, this adds a brick."
- "The authors' own conclusion is delay plus better design plus digital education, together,
  not a ban on its own. That is the whole of what we do."
- "The strongest thing in it was not screen hours, it was the phone bleeding into study,
  meals and sleep. The pattern, not the clock. That is exactly what a good balance report
  should watch."

## 7. What NOT to do

- Do not call it junk science or biased. It is not, and saying so makes us look like the
  mirror image of the thing we criticise.
- Do not deny that early access can matter. The defensible position grants a real, modest,
  concentrated effect. Denying it entirely is as unscientific as overclaiming it.
- Do not fight on the mental health ground using this paper. It is not a mental health paper.
