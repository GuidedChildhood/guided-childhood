The morning before school and the after school return

What the evidence and the people in the room say about the two hardest transitions of a school day, and what the product should do at 07:30 and 15:30 so a parent gets the one move and the words, and can log what happened in one tap.

60 Second Summary

The two flashpoints are not screen problems first. They are sleep and food problems that the screen walks into. Two randomised experiments show ordinary sleep loss makes children irritable and inattentive, the UK cohort shows irregular bedtimes predicting behaviour in a dose response that reverses when bedtimes settle, and in the datasets everyone fights over, enough sleep and breakfast carry associations three to six times the size of technology use. So the morning card leads with last night and food, and the after school card leads with hunger, company and a predictable stopping point.

"After school restraint collapse" is a parenting blog term from 2016. The behaviour is real; the mechanism failed replication across 23 labs. Describe the pattern, never name a syndrome. Morning screens causing lateness or bad moods is unsupported: one small French case control is all there is, the DfE publishes no lateness figures, and the 1958 British study of 1,854 matched children found viewers no more tired than matched controls while half the teachers blamed the set.

The strongest tension in the sweep is between the brief and the build lenses. The brief wants the right minute, twice a day on school days. The trials say the minute is worth single digit points, ten prompts a week is the arm that raised opt out and helped nobody, web push reaches 5 to 8 percent of a web app's families, and what retains is the log that is the product. Build the window and the loop, default to one window per family, put the card on Home, and read one number in week four.

Children already know: 37 percent say their own screen time is too high and two thirds of teenagers have done something about it. The DfE's own survey found 41 percent of primary children at the TV and 28 percent on a device before school, and parents valuing a calm, predictable start over food. The after school hour now has a parent in it for primary children (88 percent accompanied) and nobody in it for many teenagers. In one term three statutory documents landed on this seam and the school has nothing practical to send home. Nothing the law does in 2027 touches either window. The platform mapper found most of the plumbing already built: the 07:30 and 15:30 pushes, the moments that raise a worry, the scripts, the outcomes with a time band. The build is medium and mostly words. The tenth lens, an Early Years key person added in V3, found the under fives were in the ledger all along: the transitions study is a study of one to five year olds, the lever at two is today's nap as much as last night, the daily log is statutory rather than surveillance, and from September 2026 the nursery door is the one place a professional is told to talk to parents about screens.

Key Findings

The two flashpoints are paid for the night before and at the last meal, not in the minute they blow up

Reliability 9/10

Two randomised crossover experiments show that ordinary sleep loss degrades emotion regulation, mood and attention: one hour less for four nights in 8 to 12 year olds (Vriend 2013, n 32), and 6.5 against 10 hours in bed for five nights in 14 to 17 year olds, with parents and teenagers both reporting the irritability (Baum 2014, n 50). In the UK Millennium Cohort (n 10,230) irregular bedtimes predicted behaviour difficulties in a dose response, and the effect reversed when bedtimes became regular (Kelly 2013). In the same adolescent datasets used in the screen time debate, enough sleep (β +0.07 to +0.25) and eating breakfast (β +0.12 to +0.17) carry associations several times the size of technology use (medians β −0.005 to −0.035, at most 0.4 percent of variance) (Orben and Przybylski 2019, n 355,358). In the Selby district of North Yorkshire's 2024 pupil survey (Year 6 n 829, within a county survey of 14,934), 8 percent of Year 6 had nothing to eat before school and 57 percent felt tired at school the day before, up from 47 percent in 2022. NHS guidance lists tiredness and hunger as the first two causes of a tantrum. The 1958 British television study found no difference in morning tiredness between viewers and matched controls, although half the teachers blamed the set anyway (1,854 matched viewers and controls, 1955).

"After school restraint collapse" is a description, not a diagnosis, and the mechanism behind it failed replication

Reliability 9/10

Romper traces the term to psychotherapist and former teacher Andrea Loewen Nair in 2016; a Child Mind Institute psychologist, Dr Dave Anderson, says in print that it "isn't a clinical term" but a description of a phenomenon. No peer reviewed paper names or tests it. Every explanation offered for it borrows willpower depletion, which returned d = 0.04 (95 percent CI −0.07 to 0.15) across 23 labs and 2,141 participants against an original estimate of d = 0.62 (Hagger 2016); the glucose version of the same idea failed a p curve (Vadillo 2016). The nearest physiology is a small rise in cortisol across the day at childcare, strongest under three (Vermeer 2006, r 0.18). UK parents do not use the phrase; Mumsnet says "straight on the telly", "strops", "zombified". Children melting down at 3.30 is a real observation. The label and the mechanism are not science, so the product describes the pattern and never names a syndrome.

A stopping point agreed in advance beats a countdown, and the screen is never a reward or a punishment

Reliability 8/10

In the only diary study of screen endings (28 families, 380 transitions, children 14 to 66 months, parent rated), children were less upset when the screen time had been routine rather than ad hoc, and transitions the technology ended went better than ones a parent interrupted; warned children were more upset, not less, a result the authors suspect is confounded by parents warning when they expect trouble (Hiniker 2016). Advance notice alone did not raise compliance in three noncompliant preschoolers (Wilder 2010). RCPCH tells families to agree the plan in a calm moment; NHS paediatric advice gives a heads up 30 minutes out, a short fixed sequence and the same cue phrase every time; the UK Chief Medical Officers add phones outside the bedroom at bedtime and screen free meals. The most upvoted morning rule on Mumsnet is the home made version: "TV only when shoes are on."

The minute is worth single digits; content fit and about three prompts a week carry the effect, and a daily log is the retention mechanic

Reliability 8/10

In the one direct test of how often to text parents, three a week beat one and five: five raised opt out by 58 percent and helped nobody (Cortes 2021, 3,473 families randomised). Personalising the content to the child beat generic content by 63 percent on moving up a reading level with the same ping (Doss 2018, n 794). Two microrandomised trials put timing at a few percentage points: a prompt opens the app 3.5 times more often within the hour and changes time to disengagement not at all (Bell 2023); next day engagement rose 3.9 percent, more at weekends than weekday mornings (Bidargaddi 2018). Sensor driven timing did no better than a fixed daily slot and half the users were gone in two weeks either way (Morrison 2017); a contextual nudge fell from +107 percent to +24 percent over six weeks (HeartSteps 2019). Stressed people ignore the prompt built for their stressed moment: 79 percent non engagement, with stress predicting silence (2026). What retains is the log itself: every logging measure tracked outcomes in Noom (n 11,252), and Finch holds 37 percent at day seven on a morning check in loop. The EEF reached the same conclusion from the school side: telling parents their child's days missed moved absence by 0.21 days across five terms among 71,916 pupils with below 95 percent attendance, and "further practical support and tip sharing are required".

Children already know, and most teenagers are already acting

Reliability 8/10

Ofcom's 2026 survey of 3,426 children: 37 percent of 8 to 17s say their own screen time is too high (28 percent of 8 to 9s, 44 percent of 16 to 17s, 56 percent of those with a mental health condition), and 65 percent of 13 to 17s have taken at least one step, 29 percent disabling notifications, 21 percent no longer taking a device to bed. Parents are harsher: 55 percent say their child's time is too high. In children's own words the morning is rush, shouting and being late ("My Dad shouting and rowing. Being late for anything", a boy of 11; Children's Commissioner, nearly 2,000 children, 2020) and the return home is a need to "switch off" in a bedroom, often alone (Ofcom Children's Media Lives 2026). Advice that assumes resistance will feel like being managed; advice that assumes alliance will feel like respect.

The morning is already a screen moment for a large minority of primary children, and what parents want from a calmer start is predictability, not food

Reliability 8/10

The DfE's own survey of 5,487 parents in the 750 breakfast club early adopter schools (March 2026) found that before school 41 percent of children watch television and 28 percent use a tablet, phone, computer or console; 80 percent eat breakfast. Parents who use the free club credit it with mornings that are less stressful (68 percent), easier (66 percent) and more predictable (76 percent); the reasons they give are longer working hours (53 percent), the child enjoying it (43 percent) and an easier drop off (35 percent); a smoother morning routine at home is named by 20 percent. Of the parents who do not use it, 32 percent "prefer to have more time with my child in the morning". That is the customer. The state is now paying £1 per child per day for a 30 minute calm start in about 2,750 primaries, and 64 percent of primary heads expect no attendance effect from it.

Measured, the breakfast window is about 28 minutes online a day for UK 8 to 14s, and the after school hours are spent at home with family, almost never with friends

Reliability 7/10

Ofcom's passive metering panel (701 children aged 8 to 14, 28 days each, October 2025 to March 2026; Figure 4 of the Children's Online Experiences report) puts the 05:00 to 08:59 window at 25 minutes for 8 to 9s, 28 for 10 to 12s and 32 for 13 to 14s, with the 15:00 to 20:59 block the largest of the day at 1h24 to 2h01. The figures are per visitor per day across smartphones, tablets and computers, average weekdays with weekends (3h24 against 4h07 overall) and exclude TV sets and consoles, so the school morning itself is likely lower and the TV in the kitchen is not counted. In three day diaries from 204 UK 11 to 12 year olds, 30 percent of the 3pm to 6pm window went on screens (about 54 minutes), under 1 percent of the time was with friends, about 46 percent with family or siblings and about 20 percent alone; more time alone was associated with more screen use (Haycraft 2020, cross sectional). The Economist and the Skeptic both reported that no UK daypart figure exists, which was true of the headline Ofcom reports; the metered figure lives in the online experiences report and needs the caveats attached whenever it is used.

The after school hour now has a parent in it; the 1970s latchkey worry has inverted

Reliability 8/10

In 1971, 80 percent of English 7 and 8 year olds got to school unaccompanied by an adult; by 1990 it was 9 percent; the share of junior school children accompanied by a parent rose from 14 percent to 64 percent, and escorting children consumed an estimated 904 million hours in 1990 (Hillman, Adams and Whitelegg 1990). National Travel Survey: 88 percent of 7 to 10 year olds and 31 percent of 11 to 13 year olds are usually accompanied by an adult. Only 27 percent of 5 to 7s, 22 percent of 8 to 11s and 10 percent of 12 to 14s use an after school club, so most families do the 3pm to 6pm window themselves (DfE 2025); the market prices that gap at £69.38 a week in England (Coram 2026). The 1958 researchers studied a child coming home to an empty afternoon; today's primary child comes home with a parent in the car or at the gate, which is the opening for a together model rather than a supervision gap.

Morning screens causing lateness, mood or school trouble is unsupported, so the product must not claim it

Reliability 8/10

No study of before school screen use and lateness, attendance or morning mood was found. The one study that isolates the before school slot is a French case control of 276 children aged 3.5 to 6.5 recruited after a language disorder diagnosis, with exposure from parent recall, a morning exposure odds ratio of 3.40 (1.60 to 7.23) and a combined estimate running from 1.44 to 23.95 (Collet 2019); reverse causation is live, since the child who is hard to get out of the door is the one most likely to be handed a screen. Against matched controls in 1958, television viewers were no more tired in the morning and spent the same time on homework. The DfE publishes no lateness statistics at all; late after registers close is 0.17 percent of primary sessions and 0.31 percent of secondary (FFT 2025) and 0.3 percent of penalty notices (DfE 2024/25); the DfE's £750 a day figure is about absence, not lateness. A school saying it can "instantly see which kids have watched TV before school" is a teacher report of exactly the kind the 1958 controls contradicted.

Three statutory documents landed on this seam in September 2026, and the school has nothing practical to send home

Reliability 7/10

Ofsted's toolkit for inspections from 7 September 2026 asks a primary what it does about "the fact that pupils' punctuality and attendance may depend on parents" and "how well staff teach pupils daily routines and habits", and asks every school about home school partnership on phones. The RSHE guidance in force from 1 September 2026 makes "practical steps for improving sleep, such as not using screens in the bedroom" statutory primary content, and at secondary "screen-free time before bed and removing phones from the bedroom". The mobile phones guidance is statutory from 29 June 2026, and the DfE's own parent guidance on screen use for ages 5 to 16, announced 8 June 2026 for the autumn, is not yet published. What schools currently send home about mornings is a punctuality web page: bedtime routine, kit ready the night before, a minutes lost table; none of four checked mentions screens or reaches a parent at the moment it matters. Staff time is the constraint (48.1 hours a week; Magic Breakfast cost 820 staff hours a year), so whatever we hand a school has to cost it one link, once.

Parents have invented the mechanic at home and buy charts and free locks; nobody sells the words for the thirty seconds after the child asks

Reliability 7/10

The morning is the fight parents argue about in public (Mumsnet morning TV threads run to 419, 189 and 86 replies) and after school is the one they confess to (14 and 38). The dominant home made fix is the screen as the reward for being ready. What they buy is cheap and physical for the morning, a £1.50 to £20 chart, and a free lock for the afternoon (Google Family Link, 4.6 stars from 42,000 UK ratings), and both fail at the same point: the moment the child asks and the parent has to be the no. Routine apps charge £6 to £13 a month, the one star Joon reviews are about sticker shock, and parents on Reddit report children growing bored of the pet (Choosing Therapy; a competitor puts the fade at four to eight weeks, which is a competitor's claim). 75 percent of UK parents in a commercial survey report a tantrum after a device was refused (Compare and Recycle 2025, n 1,000), and 51 percent in an undated Amazon brand survey of 2,000 parents, from around 2021, call school mornings the most stressful part of the day. At £7.99 to £12.99 a parent prices Guided Childhood against Joon and Family Link, not against a coach, unless the pitch is the words for the moment.

Everything ever regulated regulates the night; nothing touches 7.30am or 3.30pm

Reliability 7/10

The Toddlers' Truce (no television 6pm to 7pm, 1946 to 1957) ended with a minister saying bedtime was the parents' job. The 9pm watershed came out of the 1964 Television Act, with the ITA recommending that material injurious to children be excluded before 9pm and a family viewing period lasting until then (Ofcom 2014). Breakfast television's first act in 1983 was a two hour children's show across the getting ready window, and no regulator ever treated that hour as a children's hour. YouTube turned autoplay off for teens by default in 2021; the UK's under 16 package, announced 15 June 2026 and live Spring 2027 after 116,211 consultation responses, adds for 16 and 17s a default midnight to 6am curfew with notifications disabled and autoplay and personalised feeds off by default, leaves WhatsApp and Signal out and, per NPR, YouTube Kids. Nothing in any of it acts on the school run or the return. The morning is unregulated and unstudied rather than newly dangerous, and the after school moment is old and now has a parent in it.

Nudges to parents work when the content fits the child, in the one UK analogue and the best US trials, with a vendor caveat

Reliability 6/10

Ready4K's three texts a week moved parental involvement at home and school by 0.15 to 0.29 SD and child early literacy by about 0.11 SD overall and 0.31 SD for children below the median (York, Loeb and Doss 2019, 1,031 parents); a coauthor on the differentiated texts paper is at the vendor, and the numbers hold but should be labelled. The closest UK analogue is BRIC, a bedtime routine delivered by seven nights of texts to 50 first time parents of 1 to 3 year olds: 98 percent retention, routine quality improved and parental mood disturbance fell, in a non randomised pilot (Kitsaras 2022). Self directed parenting programmes with no human lose more than 90 percent before completion (Dadds 2019, n 2,967, 7.3 percent completed), and the stated reasons for leaving text programmes are "not helpful" (23 percent) and "too busy" (22 percent).

For a two to four year old the lever is today's nap as much as last night, and the only person who knows whether it happened is at the nursery door

Reliability 8/10

Half of three year olds still nap once a day and a third of four year olds (Iglowstein 2003, 493 Zurich children). In the one experiment on toddlers, a single missed nap cut 30 to 36 month olds' positive emotion responses by 34 percent and raised negative responses by 31 percent within hours (Berger 2012, n 10). WHO puts sleep need at 11 to 14 hours including naps at one to two and 10 to 13 at three to four, and the Millennium Cohort's irregular bedtimes at three predicted behaviour difficulties at seven in a dose response, reversing when bedtimes became regular (Kelly 2013, n 10,230). Across 10,085 children in 13 countries including the UK, sleep improved with every extra night a week of bedtime routine (Mindell 2015). The 5pm meltdown in a two year old is usually the nap, so a pick up card for this age asks did she sleep and did she eat before it asks anything about a screen.

Settling in is a measurable stress curve, and the goodbye that works is short, the same every day and started by the parent

Reliability 7/10

When 15 month olds started childcare, cortisol in the first hour after their mothers left ran 75 to 100 percent above home levels during the first nine days, and attachments stayed or became secure when mothers spent more days settling the child in (Ahnert 2004, n 70). A Norwegian study of 113 toddlers found an easy start while parents were present, demanding first weeks alone, and cortisol back to baseline by three months, with parents' and staff's impressions matching the hormone (2023). Watching mothers leave 6 to 18 month olds every morning for three months, separations the mother started with a repeated ritual reduced stress, more crying followed when she stayed to chat to the caregiver after the goodbye, and staff met the children who cried most with distraction and those who cried least with empathy (Klein 2010). The shape transfers to any new home routine for a two year old, a new screen rule included: the adult present first, then the ritual, then the step back, and weeks rather than a day for it to settle. Samples are small, under two and from Germany, Norway, Austria and Israel; the transfer to two to four is practice inference and is marked as such.

The nursery door is the one place in the system where a professional is now told to talk to parents about screens, kindly, every day

Reliability 8/10

From 1 September 2026 every English setting must have regard to the government's screen use guidance (EYFS 3.94), which tells providers to raise screens with parents in conversations and handovers and to keep it non judgemental; every child already has a named key person whose job includes the relationship with the parents (3.42) and a daily two way flow of information (3.95). The government's own under five guidance (Best Start in Life, March 2026) sets the first official English thresholds: avoid screens under two except shared activities, aim for no more than an hour a day at two to five, bedrooms and mealtimes screen free, none in the hour before bed. The expert group behind it found no convincing evidence that short periods of up to 30 minutes were harmful at two and over, put the acceleration of harm beyond about 1.5 hours a day at two, and said its time limits are based broadly on knowledge of child development rather than directly on evidence (EYSTAG 2026). Nine in ten three and four year olds are in a setting, the median is 22 to 24 hours a week, and childminders and private nurseries open ten hours a day against seven for schools (DfE 2025), so for many two to four year olds the pick up is at 5.30 or 6 at the end of a day longer than a school day. No equivalent conversation exists at a primary gate.

The pick up screen that stops the car seat scream is the behaviour the under five evidence measures, and the pushchair screen is talk not happening

What the lenses agree happens, minute by minute

The Two Transitions

The morning, from the Clinician's timeline with the other lenses' corrections

  1. 06:45 to 07:00, waking. Won't get up after a late night is sleep debt, and the lever was last night: phone charged outside the bedroom (the Chief Medical Officers' line), screens off an hour before sleep (RCPCH). Fixed wake time even after a late one, light on, recover the sleep tonight. Words: "Same time up, early night tonight." Not a lecture.
  2. 07:00 to 07:20, the first reach. Teenagers go back to bed with the phone; younger children ask for the TV before they are dressed. The phone that charged downstairs cannot be reached for, so that fight never starts. For primary: screen only after dressed, teeth and bag by the door, said as "when, then" in the same words every day. Children's own stress words for this hour are shouting and being late, so fewer instructions, a calmer adult, and the parent's own phone away.
  3. 07:20 to 07:45, dressing and breakfast. Food before the demand. If a screen is on, pick something with a natural end and say in advance it goes off when the episode ends; let the programme end it rather than a countdown alone. Breakfast screen free, which is the Chief Medical Officers' advice. Talk about what they watched: never discussing content was the pattern the one morning screens study flagged.
  4. 07:45 to 08:15, the door. A picture sequence by the door for under sevens and for autistic and ADHD children, and a longer runway. If the fight happens only on school days with a stomach ache or nausea, stop pushing the screen rule: it is school avoidance until shown otherwise, and the move is the school and the GP.

The return, 15:15 to 17:30

  1. 15:15 to 15:30, the gate or the door. Warm, wordless, a snack in the bag. No "how was your day" for twenty minutes: children answer "fine". For a teenager coming home to an empty house, the message is to the parent who arrives later.
  2. 15:30 to 15:50, the first twenty minutes. Describe the pattern to parents, never a syndrome. Unstructured time rather than a scheduled activity. If a screen goes on to calm a young child, that is the use most likely to cost regulation skill later, especially boys and intense temperaments, so a person or a snack before a tablet. Teenagers call this switching off and fourteen year olds call it peaceful; name it as legitimate.
  3. 15:50 to 16:30, snack, screen, homework. Decide the stopping point before the screen goes on and let the episode or a visible timer end it. Homework not in the first twenty minutes and never as the second fight of the day; for primary, short and done, or left.
  4. 16:30 to 17:30, the switch to tea. Screen free meal, the same cue phrase daily. This is the natural minute for the one tap log, and the log should carry two more facts the evidence says matter: sleep the night before and whether they had eaten, because the two week pattern tells a screen problem from a sleep and food problem.

Nine perspectives, six on the evidence and three on the business

Lens Panels

The Clinician

It is sleep and food before it is a screen

Two randomised crossover experiments (8 to 12 and 14 to 17 year olds) and a London cohort of 6,616 put the morning in the bedroom the night before: phone use in the hour before sleep raises the odds of insufficient weekday sleep by 1.82, in the dark by 2.13, and the same children reach for the phone on waking. Hunger and tiredness are the first causes of a tantrum in NHS guidance and the first words of every expert quoted on the after school collapse. So the pre emptive message at 07:20 and 15:30 is a state check followed by one low demand move, and the referral line matters: a morning fight on school days only, with a stomach ache, is school avoidance until shown otherwise.

The Academic

Predictability beats quantity

The best evidence on either transition is not about screens at all. Regular routines predict better behaviour, sleep and executive function with a dose response in 10,230 UK children, and the effect reverses when bedtimes become regular, which is the most useful number for a parent because it says the window is open. Household chaos has a small reliable association with executive function (r 0.16 across 16,480 children), and where it is directly assessed, instability matters more than mess. Screens enter as the thing that erodes the routine: they end badly when ad hoc and well when predictable, and warnings alone made children more upset in the one study that measured it.

The Skeptic

The prompt opens the door; what is behind it does the work

Three randomised arms by the Stanford group settle the design: personalised beats generic by 63 percent on the same ping, three texts a week beat one and five (five raised opt out by 58 percent), and which day helps depends on the family. Microrandomised trials put the minute at single digit points and found no change in time to disengagement. Two cards a day on school days is ten a week, the arm that failed. Restraint collapse borrows a mechanism that returned d = 0.04 across 23 labs; the morning screens risk figure is one case control with an interval from 1.44 to 23.95. The one tap log is self report at the worst minute from the engaged minority, so design the loop to learn from drop off too.

The Economist

The two moments the state has priced at zero

492,800 penalty notices in 2024/25 and 1,400 of them for lateness, against a DfE estimate of about £750 in lifetime earnings per missed secondary day. The after school gap has a price, £69.38 a week in England, but only 13 percent of local authorities have enough care for 12 to 14 year olds and only 10 percent of that age use a club, so 3pm to 6pm is unpaid parent time with a device as co provider. Every regulatory lever since 2021 acts on bedtime and the night. Willingness to pay benchmarks (Qustodio £5 to £7, Bark £8, GoHenry £4 to £10, a club at £69 a week) bracket £12.99 comfortably, if the family feels it twice a day.

The Historian

Britain has only ever managed the child's day from the evening end

The Toddlers' Truce, family viewing time, the watershed and the Dick Barton writers' code were all about tea to bed; breakfast television's first act in 1983 was a children's show across the getting ready window and no regulator blinked. The 1958 study (1,854 matched viewers and controls) found viewers no more tired in the morning than matched controls while half the teachers blamed the set; it found parental example mattered more than parental control, and recorded, as a finding rather than advice, that the set was already being used as an instrument of discipline, for punishment and reward. The after school moment changed in one way no screen panic captured: four in five 7 and 8 year olds got to school unaccompanied in 1971, fewer than one in ten by 1990, and escorting consumed 904 million hours that year. The parent is in the room now.

The Child

Feed me first, ask me later

Nearly four in ten 8 to 17s say their own screen time is too high and two thirds of teenagers have already done something about it. Children describe the morning as rush, shouting and being late; the phone is the quiet corner inside it. They describe the return home as switching off, often alone, in a house where the parent is on their own screen (36 percent of 2,000 secondary pupils had asked a parent to put the phone down and 46 percent of those said the parent took no notice, as the BBC reported the 2017 HMC survey). What helps is a person, not a rule: "it's enough if they pay attention to me afterward". And a one tap log about them, that they never see, is surveillance; filled in together it is our story.

The SaaS Operator

Build the window and the loop; do not build the clock

Web push reaches 5 to 8 percent of a web app's families, so the card has to live on Home. Sensor driven timing did no better than a fixed slot and half the users were gone in two weeks either way; a contextual nudge fell from +107 percent to +24 percent over six weeks; 79 percent of prompts went unanswered precisely when the person's stress threshold was met. What retains is the log that is the product (Noom, Finch at 37 percent day seven). Ask the parent two times at onboarding, default to one window, put the one tap moment on the card, and read one number in week four: card to moment rate, 15 percent or above. And the biggest number in the ledger is not this feature: a four day trial converts 25.5 percent against 42.5 percent for 17 to 32 days.

Parent Demand

Nobody sells the thirty seconds after the child asks

The morning is argued in public (419 reply threads), the afternoon confessed in private (14). Parents have already invented the mechanic, the screen as the reward for being ready, and they buy a £2 printable or a £15 chart for the morning and a free lock for the afternoon. Both fail at the same point: the ask. Routine apps at £6 to £13 a month lose the child in four to eight weeks. The purchase trigger is the first six weeks of autumn term and an outside voice, usually the school's note about TV before school. At £7.99 to £12.99 a parent compares us to Joon and Family Link unless the pitch is the words for the moment rather than another chart.

Schools Digital Learning

The seam is now statutory, and the school has nothing practical to send home

In one term three official documents landed on the morning and the evening: Ofsted asks a primary what it does about punctuality depending on parents, RSHE makes screens out of the bedroom statutory primary content, and the DfE is paying £1 a child a day for a 30 minute calm start because its own survey found 41 percent of children at the TV and 28 percent on a device before school. Yet the EEF's highest security attendance trial found facts to parents move absence by 0.21 days across five terms and asked for practical tips instead. A school will send one link, once, through the MIS it already has, if the DSL can confirm the logged moment never reaches the school and a pilot can show the register changing. Staff time is the only currency.

The Early Years Key Person

The handover is not a hand off, and the log already exists

The nine lenses used the under five evidence and then said there was none: the transitions study they rank highest is a study of one to five year olds, and read for its own age it says a session in the same slot, ended by the episode, beats a warning. For a two year old the when then sentence is for the parent to hold, not the child. Sleep is today's nap as much as last night, and the only person who knows whether it happened is at the door, so the pick up questions are did she sleep and did she eat before anything about a screen. The daily log the nine lenses worried was surveillance is statutory at this age: every setting must run a two way flow of information with parents every day, and parents ask for it. A log of the child's state and the parent's own day is a continuation of the handover; a log of screen minutes is the one thing nursery never does. And from September 2026 the key person is the one professional in the system told to raise screens with parents, kindly, at the door. That is the Stage 1 distribution opening, and it was not in the briefing.

Honest limits

Assumptions and the Missing Lens

This briefing rests on

UK context throughout. The only metered daypart figure (Ofcom's CPOM panel) averages weekdays with weekends and excludes smart TVs and consoles, so the school morning itself is probably lower and the kitchen television is uncounted; the DfE parent survey is of families in breakfast club schools, not all families. Almost all children's data starts at age 8; nothing in this ledger asks a child under five about the morning in their own words, so advice for nursery and reception mornings is adult inference and is labelled as such. The nudge evidence is from adult self help apps and from literacy and attendance texting programmes, not from parents of school age children around screens; the nearest UK analogue is a 50 family bedtime pilot. Web push reach of 5 to 8 percent is a single vendor's figure. The product facts (prices, the 4 percent free to paid benchmark, one founder) are as of 7 October 2026. The Skeptic's warning stands over the whole loop: a one tap log is self report at the worst minute of the day from the families who stay, so the loop must learn from drop off as much as from taps.
