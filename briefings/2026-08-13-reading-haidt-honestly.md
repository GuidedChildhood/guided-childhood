# Reading Haidt honestly, the evidence, the errors, and the better path

**Date:** 13 August 2026. **For:** Justin, and the "Louder Than The Evidence" series that follows.
British English, no dashes anywhere. Held to the Odgers test, defensible to a hostile expert.
Source of truth for the content pack at content/packs/2026-08-13-louder-than-the-evidence/.

## The posture, before anything

We are not writing a takedown of a man. That is a trap, and it is off brand. Our whole
philosophy warns against single villain framing, so making Jonathan Haidt the villain would
make us the thing we criticise. The target is the proportionality, the gap between the volume
of the claim and the weight of the evidence, using the most famous version of the claim as the
way in. Haidt is the honest, well meaning author of the loudest version, not the enemy. Credit
him fully and early, every time, then turn. That honest pivot is the signature move and it is
what makes the critique land instead of bounce.

## 1. What Haidt gets right, and it is a lot

State this plainly and first. It is true, and it earns the right to the rest.

- He named a real parental worry and made "delay the smartphone" a mainstream, low cost, low
  risk default. That is a genuine public good.
- Phones in schools are a real distraction. Phone free school policies are sensible. The
  largest study of about 4,600 schools found teachers reported fewer distractions, so the case
  is sensible rather than a proven miracle, but it is real.
- Sleep displacement is real, even if the blue light mechanism is contested (Etchells puts the
  tech attributable sleep loss at only a few minutes a night, so hold the magnitude honestly).
- The decline of unsupervised play and childhood independence is well documented and widely
  accepted. His formula, over protected in the real world and under protected online, is sharp
  and fair.
- He is transparent. He posts his data, names his critics, runs open review documents, and has
  publicly corrected at least one mislabelled figure. Even Aaron Brown, a hostile critic,
  called his integrity and transparency admirable. The dispute is about inference from
  evidence, not honesty. Say that. It makes us the fair one.

## 2. The recurring pattern, where the volume outruns the evidence

Not "lies." A consistent, decade long habit of reading small, mostly correlational associations
as a proven, population wide, brain rewiring catastrophe. Six documented threads.

1. **Correlation read as cause.** The core charge, from the most authoritative venue.
   Candice Odgers, in Nature (29 March 2024), reviewing The Anxious Generation:
   "the book's repeated suggestion that digital technologies are rewiring our children's brains
   and causing an epidemic of mental illness is not supported by science," and the blame on
   social media "might distract us from effectively responding to the real causes." She is a UC
   Irvine psychologist who has tracked thousands of 10 to 14 year olds since 2008. She is the
   single most cited counter voice.
2. **Effect size inflation.** Orben and Przybylski (Nature Human Behaviour, 2019), specification
   curve across about 355,000 adolescents, found digital technology use explains at most 0.4
   percent of the variance in wellbeing, an association they compared to wearing glasses or
   eating potatoes, and smaller than the effect of bullying. A tiny average presented as a
   civilisational emergency.
3. **The everywhere at once claim.** Haidt argues the decline hit the whole Anglosphere
   simultaneously around 2012, so a common cause. The counter is that the international picture
   is inconsistent. Caveat we must hold honestly: the strongest global null studies (Vuorre and
   Przybylski, 2.4 million people across 168 countries, 2024) measure internet and technology
   adoption broadly, not adolescent social media specifically, and Haidt's team has a peer
   reviewed rebuttal on exactly that point. So use these to show the population signal is not
   clean, not to claim they measure teen social media directly. That gap is where a hostile
   expert attacks, so do not overstep it.
4. **Selective citation.** The open collaborative Google Docs pile up supportive studies. Their
   own text concedes "we have not conducted an exhaustive search," which critics read as
   advocacy dressed as systematic review.
5. **Weak experiments.** Aaron Brown (in Reason, libertarian, so label it) counted that of about
   476 studies in the book, roughly two thirds pre date 2010, and only 22 hold data on heavy
   social media use or serious adolescent mental illness, and none on both. Stuart Ritchie adds
   that the causal experiments lean on short deprivation studies, often on college students, not
   early adolescents.
6. **Alternatives underweighted.** The 2008 financial crisis and its unequal weight on poorer
   families, changes in diagnosis and willingness to report distress, school pressure. Named by
   Odgers as under examined.

The corrected graph, the single most concrete instance: a UK teen self harm figure was
originally labelled as hospitalisations when the data were self harm episodes from primary care
records. Professor Sir Louis Appleby, the UK government's suicide prevention adviser, flagged it,
and the description was amended. Use this as the clean, undeniable example of the volume
outrunning the care.

## 3. Odgers, the anchor, and the wrong villain

Odgers is the spine of the series because her critique carries our hidden thread for us. Her
argument in three moves.

- **Reverse causation.** Struggling children use technology differently and more heavily, so the
  correlation often runs the other way, distress driving use, not use driving distress. The 2025
  registered reports in Nature Human Behaviour (Fassi et al, about 3,340 UK adolescents with
  clinical assessment) support this, teens with mental health conditions use social media more
  and are more affected by it, and the authors stress the design gives no causal evidence and
  the relationship may be bidirectional. This is our strongest recent brick because it is
  registered and UK.
- **The real drivers are upstream.** Poverty, the 2008 recession's unequal weight, childhood
  adversity, family instability, and, in her newer work, adult mental health, she notes parental
  overdose deaths more than doubled between 2011 and 2021. Her fix is to fund the adults and
  services around children, counsellors, drop in centres, digital mental health, paid for by
  taxing the platforms. This is the poverty and ACE thesis, in a Nature reviewer's mouth, not
  just ours.
- **Restriction punishes the protected.** Her sharpest line, verify the exact wording at source
  before quoting: "Instead of fixing the online world, we are punishing victims by taking away
  the places they go to spend time with friends, consume youth culture, and too often, escape
  adults who are harming them offline." And from NPR (June 2026): "We've already kicked teenagers
  out of public spaces, and now we're going to take away the spaces where they virtually gather."

## 4. The policy that backfires, held to the ban rule

We never relitigate the ban itself. We treat it as settled background and talk preparation. But
the evidence that a ban alone fails is fair game and it is strong.

- Australia's under 16 ban took effect 10 December 2025. Three months in, more than 8 in 10 teens
  were still online (Al Jazeera, August 2026), via VPNs, borrowed and fake accounts, even face
  masks to beat age checks. The age check machinery created new harm, a hack on a verification
  vendor exposed about 68,000 government IDs.
- Bans strip the protected. Schueller, Lind and Odgers (Frontiers, 2026), "We cannot ban our way
  out of a youth mental health crisis," note the banned children keep browsing anonymously,
  losing the age based protections, filters and controls, they would have had. The Trevor Project
  data shows most LGBTQ young people rely on online spaces for connection they cannot get offline.
- Driving it underground erodes the one thing that protects, the parent child conversation. A
  roughly 4,000 teen study found young people with positive parental communication had healthier
  tech relationships. This is our line, connection is the protection, in the policy evidence.

## 5. The career, stated fairly, not as accusation

Haidt's reputation is now bound to one causal claim, which raises the stakes of being right. The
Anxious Generation was a runaway bestseller. He runs an advocacy nonprofit (the TAG Movement), a
lab at NYU Stern (the Tech and Society Lab), and a paid Substack (After Babel). The thesis
underpins real law, Australia's ban, US state laws, the UK debate. Frame this as the ordinary
incentives of a public intellectual whose book, nonprofit, lab, newsletter and policy legacy all
now depend on one idea being true. That is a reason to watch for confirmation pressure. It is not
evidence of bad faith, and we say so. Do not cite any Substack revenue figure, none is verified.

## 6. The honest caveats, so we do not overclaim

- Do not lean on Ferguson as load bearing. His meta analyses point our way (near zero effects),
  but he is a contested figure and Haidt has a published re analysis. Mention once, hedged. Lean
  on Orben and Przybylski, Odgers, NASEM, and the Fassi registered reports instead.
- Do not say the Vuorre global studies measure teen social media. They measure internet and
  technology adoption. Say that precisely.
- Do not say social media is harmless. The honest middle is, population effects are small, harm
  concentrates in already vulnerable children and in developmental windows (Orben and Blakemore,
  2022, roughly 11 to 13 for girls, 14 to 15 for boys), and pattern of use beats raw hours.
- The Italian study (Gui, Respi, Abbiati, Nature Human Behaviour, August 2026) is real and well
  built and shows an early access academic effect. It is not a mental health paper. Read straight
  it argues for delay plus design plus education, our position, and its strongest mediator was
  phone pervasiveness, the pattern not the hours. See the 12 August briefing.

## 7. The through line for the series

The loudest story about our children is not the truest one. The truest one is quieter, harder to
film, and more useful. The average harm is small, the real harm is concentrated and it is
upstream, poverty and adversity and a parent's own struggle, and the answer is not a wall, it is
preparation and connection. Every post carries one brick of that. One post lands the poverty and
ACE thesis plainly (the wrong villain post). The series credits Haidt, models the good faith it
asks for, and finishes on capability, never fear.

## 8. Recent evidence to fold in, 2025 and 2026, both sides

An honest scoreboard, because the field split again this year. Hold both. This is the material to
add when we want fresh studies rather than the Italian paper alone.

**Backs Haidt, recent and defensible:**
- **Nagata et al, JAMA Network Open, 2025.** The ABCD cohort, 11,876 US children followed from age
  9 to 10 over three years, analysed within person (RI-CLPM). Within a child, more social media
  one year predicted more depressive symptoms the next, and the reverse path was not supported.
  This is the freshest strong directional result and it cuts against our own reverse causation
  line, so we hold it honestly. Caveat, observational, self reported use, small effects, one
  cohort. It shows temporal order, not manipulation.
- **May, Malouff and Meynadier, meta analysis, 2025.** 10 RCTs, 1,491 people. Reducing social
  media lowered depression, Hedges g = 0.25, small but significant after publication bias
  adjustment.
- **Braghieri, Levy and Makarin, American Economic Review, 2022.** The cleanest causal paper we
  have, the staggered Facebook college rollout raised depression and anxiety via social comparison.
  Not new, but still the anchor. College students in the 2000s, not today's under 16s.
- Lower tier, cite with care: a 2025 BMC Medicine screen time reduction RCT (young adults, not
  adolescents), the World Happiness Report 2026 chapter by Haidt and Rausch (NOT peer reviewed, an
  argument built on other studies), and Sapien Labs 2025 (cross sectional, retrospective, an
  unvalidated mental health quotient, use as corroborating scale, never as proof).

**Goes against Haidt, recent and defensible:**
- **Lemahieu et al, Scientific Reports, 2025.** Meta analysis of social media abstinence, 10
  studies, 4,674 people, no significant effect on positive affect, negative affect or life
  satisfaction. Larger than the May meta, and it undercuts the just quit and feel better story.
  Adults and abstinence, so it speaks to restriction as remedy, not developmental harm. Present it
  alongside the May meta or we are not meeting our own standard.
- **Lind, Schueller and Odgers, Frontiers, 2026.** Audited about 40 randomised restriction
  experiments and found not one included anyone under 16, the exact band being banned has zero
  experimental evidence behind it. Disclose their industry ties (YouTube advisory, Headspace,
  Ksana Health) before a critic does.
- **Goodyear et al, SMART Schools, Lancet Regional Health Europe, 2025.** 1,227 UK pupils, 30
  schools. Restrictive school phone policies showed no better wellbeing, sleep or attainment, and
  did not even cut total daily use. The best recent school ban study, a null for the in school
  mechanism.
- **Australia eSafety evaluation, 2026.** Three months into the under 16 ban, about 81 percent of
  under 16s were still on the restricted platforms, use barely moved, VPN downloads tripled.
  Implementation failure and evasion, not a mental health outcome, so do not overclaim it.
- **Tolboll et al, umbrella review, Child and Adolescent Mental Health, 2026.** 72 reviews, 2021 to
  2025. General use, weak and inconsistent. Problematic use, consistently linked to ill being.
  Their own caveat, the weak general use findings may be underpowered rather than a true null.
  Present it whole.
- **Ferguson, Current Psychology, 2026,** argues the link is an artifact of confounds. Contested
  figure, Haidt has a rebuttal, so cite once with the rebuttal named, never load bearing.
- **Jakubowski et al, npj Science of Learning, 2025,** ties the PISA test score decline to weeks of
  school closure, roughly 1 percent of an SD per week closed. The best it was COVID not phones
  anchor. Twenge 2026 (Journal of Adolescence) argues the phone side, Emily Oster's ParentData
  rebuts it (numerate blog, not peer reviewed).

**The point that matters most for us.** Directionality is genuinely split now. Fassi 2025 (UK,
registered) found distress shaping use, Nagata 2025 (ABCD, within person) found use predicting
later distress. So keep the reverse causation line hedged, "often runs both ways," never "it runs
backwards, full stop." That hedge is exactly what keeps us defensible, and post 3 already carries
it. The strongest, most honest new post we can add is the scoreboard itself, the newest study for
Haidt and the newest against him landed within months, and they still disagree, which is the whole
proportion argument in one post.
