Guided Childhood Research Briefing

The moments parents actually live in, and what arrives next

What the evidence says about after school, the morning, the holidays, the calming screen, the parent’s own stress, the new phone, the new game and the chatbot, for children aged 4 to 16, so DiGi can say the right thing at the right moment and a term early.

60 Second Summary

The hours on the clock tell you almost nothing. Across 355,000 teenagers, screen time explained under half a percent of how they felt, so the thermometer DiGi should read is sleep, mood, friends and what the screen is replacing.

The two rules with the strongest evidence are about place and people, not minutes: a device out of the bedroom overnight, even switched off, and a parent whose own phone is away for one protected slot a day.

The moment parents struggle with is the ending. Endings go best when the content stops itself, at the end of the episode or the match, agreed before play starts. A spoken countdown mid game is the weakest tool in the box.

The forecast starts earlier than the phone. The money and the first account arrive at 8 to 10, the phone lands at 11 in the UK, and the first phone shows up in sleep before it shows up anywhere else.

AI is already in most homes: over half of UK 8 to 17s and two thirds of US teens use it. The child to watch is the lonely or SEND child who says the chatbot feels like a friend.

Every panic before this one ended the same way. The parenting move that survived radio, comics, television and games was a shared room, a routine, and a grown up beside the child asking what they are watching.

Key Findings

A device in the bedroom overnight costs sleep even when it is switched off. The charging spot outside the bedroom is the single best house rule at every age.

Reliability 9/10

Across 20 studies and 125,198 children with a mean age of 14.5, using a device at bedtime roughly doubled the odds of too little sleep (OR 2.17) and nearly tripled the odds of daytime sleepiness (OR 2.72). A device merely present in the room, unused, still raised the odds of short sleep (OR 1.79). In the US ABCD cohort of 10,280 ten to fourteen year olds, a screen in the bedroom raised the risk of trouble falling or staying asleep by 27%, and leaving the ringer on overnight by 23%. The morning is mostly lost the night before.

Supported: Clinician, Skeptic, Academic, Historian · Challenged: None
Sources: Carter 2016; Nagata 2023; Livingstone and Bovill 2001

Hours are a poor thermometer. Ask what they are doing, with whom, and what it replaces, not how long.

Reliability 9/10

Across three datasets and 355,358 adolescents, digital technology use explained at most 0.4% of the variation in wellbeing, an association the authors compared to eating potatoes. In 120,115 English fifteen year olds the curve was a hill, with both very heavy use and no use at all sitting below moderate use. Sampled six times a day, 46% of teenagers felt better after scrolling, 44% felt no different and 10% felt worse, so the same hour lands differently on different children.

Supported: Historian, Skeptic, Academic · Challenged: Clinician (the 10% are the ones in clinic)
Sources: Orben and Przybylski 2019; Przybylski and Weinstein 2017; Beyens 2020

The parent’s own phone is inside the loop. A hard day pushes parents onto their phones, and phones during play predict harder behaviour later.

Reliability 8/10

In 183 couples with a child under five followed for six months, a child’s hard behaviour predicted more parent phone use during time together, with parenting stress sitting in the middle of the loop, and that phone use went with more acting out and withdrawal in the child. The loop runs both ways. Teenagers notice too: 46% of US teens say a parent is at least sometimes distracted by their phone when they are trying to talk, while only 31% of parents say it happens regularly. A rule lands when the parent visibly keeps it.

Supported: Clinician, Academic, Skeptic, Child · Challenged: None
Sources: McDaniel and Radesky 2018; Pew 2024 screen time

Using a screen to get dinner made is fine and always was. Using it as the routine answer to a child who is upset is the pattern to watch, most of all in boys and big feelers under six.

Reliability 8/10

Two thirds of US parents of under eights use screens to occupy a child so they can get things done or take a break, and that is not the pattern the research worries about. In 422 three to five year olds, using a device to calm predicted more emotional reactivity six months later in boys (r 0.20) and in high energy children (r 0.11), and the reactivity then drove more calming use. The doctors’ own rule book has moved with this: the American Academy of Pediatrics dropped its no screens under two line in 2016 and asked who is in the room instead. Keep “occupy” and “calm” as separate jobs in your head.

Supported: Clinician, Academic, Skeptic, Historian · Challenged: Skeptic (convenience sample, parent report, small effect)
Sources: Radesky 2023; Common Sense Zero to Eight 2025; Radesky 2016; AAP 2016

The first phone lands at 11 in the UK and shows up in sleep first. Plan it in Year 6, before the box is opened.

Reliability 8/10

UK smartphone ownership jumps from 56% of ten year olds to 83% of eleven year olds, and Ofcom calls starting senior school the tipping point. In 10,588 US children, twelve year olds with a phone had 1.6 times the odds of too little sleep, 1.4 times the odds of obesity and 1.3 times the odds of depression compared with those without, and among the 3,486 who had no phone at 12, getting one in the following year raised the odds of clinical level problems by 57% and of too little sleep by 50%. Association, not proof, but the direction is consistent, and the girls’ sensitivity window for social media sits at 11 to 13.

Supported: Clinician, Academic, Economist, Historian, Child · Challenged: Skeptic (families who give phones early differ in other ways)
Sources: Ofcom Children and Parents 2026; Barzilay 2025; Orben 2022

The money arrives before the phone. Nearly every UK child plays online games by 8 and half of them spend real money inside, and the games do not ask a parent.

Reliability 8/10

97% of UK 8 to 17s play games online and 53% of those spend money in them; among children who spent in the last month, 32% say they often regret it. Of the 100 highest grossing iPhone games, not one of those with loot boxes asked a parent’s consent before letting an under 18 buy them, despite the industry’s own rule, and only 23.5% said the loot boxes were there. Fortnite’s maker paid $245 million for a button layout that charged children with one accidental press. Under 14s mostly pay with gift cards, so the gift card is the lever: a fixed amount agreed in advance, never topped up mid month.

Supported: Economist, Child · Challenged: None
Sources: Ofcom Children’s Online Experiences 2026; Ofcom Children’s Online Spending 2025; Xiao and Lund 2025; FTC 2023; Gambling Commission 2025

Loving a game is not a disorder. The clinical line is three things together for months, and the rare child who crosses it has an NHS door.

Reliability 8/10

The WHO diagnoses gaming disorder only when control is lost, gaming crowds out everything else, and it carries on despite real harm, normally for a year. The best pooled estimate is 3 to 4 in 100 gamers, lower in the most careful national surveys. Followed for six years, about one in ten teenagers drifted into rising problem symptoms while the rest held steady or faded. Forty years of violent game research ended with an effect indistinguishable from zero. The NHS National Centre for Gaming Disorders takes self referrals from 13.

Supported: Clinician, Skeptic, Academic, Historian · Challenged: None
Sources: WHO ICD 11; Stevens 2021; Coyne 2020; Drummond 2020; NHS NCGD

Rules aimed only at the child barely move problem use. Rules about place and time that the whole family keeps do.

Reliability 7/10

Across 57 studies the link between any parental mediation style and children’s outcomes is small (restrictive r 0.06, co viewing r 0.09), a meta analysis of adolescent problem internet use found active and restrictive mediation both sitting at zero, and in teens over 14 strict rules tracked slightly more problem use, not less. A rapid review of 40 studies on parental control tools found they rarely do what families hope on their own. The one randomised trial that improved children’s mental health (89 families, 181 children, Cohen d 0.53) cut leisure screens for the whole household for two weeks. Use the control as a fence around an agreed plan, never instead of one.

Supported: Academic, Economist, Skeptic · Challenged: Clinician (the bedroom rule is a rule and it works)
Sources: Collier 2016; Stoilova 2023; Schmidt-Persson 2024

Children go quiet when telling costs them the phone. Say the no confiscation promise out loud before the phone is handed over.

Reliability 6/10

The Children’s Commissioner for England found many children would not turn to their family first when something goes wrong online, and urged parents to let children talk about what they saw without simply confiscating the device. In the government’s national consultation of 116,211 people, two thirds of responding children backed some under 16 restrictions while 90% of parents did, and children asked for feature fixes: explicit images (63%), stranger contact (49%), livestreaming (45%). In a poll of nearly 2,000 UK 10 to 16 year olds, 62% said a ban would feel like punishment and only 15% felt it would make them safer.

Supported: Child · Challenged: Economist (the law is coming regardless)
Sources: Children’s Commissioner 2025; DSIT 2026; Girlguiding poll 2025

AI is already here, mostly for homework, and the child who leans on it as a friend is the lonely or SEND child.

Reliability 8/10

Over half of UK 8 to 17s (56%) have used AI, rising to two thirds of 16 to 17s, and 11% have used it as someone to talk to. In the US 64% of teens use a chatbot, about three in ten daily and mostly for schoolwork, 81% of 9 to 12 year olds already use some form of AI, and 72% of teens have tried an AI companion; a third of companion users have taken a serious conversation to it instead of a person. Among UK 9 to 17s, 64% use chatbots, 71% of vulnerable children against 62% of the rest, and 35% of users say it feels like talking to a friend, rising to half among vulnerable children. In a randomised trial with nearly 1,000 Turkish high school pupils, a chatbot that gave answers lifted practice scores and left pupils 17% worse on the unassisted exam; a version that gave only hints lifted practice by 127% and largely avoided the harm.

Supported: All six · Challenged: Economist quoting Common Sense (no companions under 18)
Sources: Ofcom Children and Parents 2026; Pew 2025 chatbots; Pew 2026 AI; Common Sense companions 2025; Common Sense AI census 2026; Internet Matters 2025; Bastani 2025

Different children, different transitions: the ADHD link is small and runs both ways, screens do not cause autism, and the anxious teen hides in the phone.

Reliability 7/10

Across 45 studies the link between media use and ADHD behaviours is r 0.12, and in 2,587 tenth graders each extra high frequency activity raised the odds of new ADHD like symptoms by about a tenth; small, real, never cause. A 2023 meta analysis in JAMA Network Open found the screens and autism link not sufficiently supported, and autistic children play more than their siblings (boys 2.4 hours a day against 1.6, girls 1.8 against 0.8), often because the game is the predictable place. In 17,174 Canadian teenagers, heavier screen use predicted more anxiety and depression symptoms a year on. Build the exit for each child: a visible schedule for the autistic child, the same stop cue for the ADHD child, one protected offline thing a day for the anxious one.

Supported: Clinician, Academic · Challenged: Skeptic (demote any cause claim)
Sources: Nikkelen 2014; Ra 2018; Ophir 2023; Mazurek and Wenstrup 2013; Mazurek and Engelhardt 2013; Mougharbel 2023

The ending is the fight. Let the content stop itself, at the end of the episode or the match, agreed before it starts.

Reliability 6/10

In 27 interviews and a 28 family diary study of preschoolers, endings set by the technology (the show finished, the device timed out) went far better than endings called by a parent, and the spoken two minute warning made transitions worse on every analysis. For older children the same lab watched 17 eight to thirteen year olds tour Roblox and Minecraft and found that pauses the game starts, and travel the child paces, are when they can hear a parent; an online match cannot be paused, so a clock time that lands mid match is what turns a stop into a fight.

Supported: Clinician, Child · Challenged: Skeptic (small samples, US)
Sources: Hiniker 2016; Baughan 2024

The game is a room full of friends, not a solo screen. Ask who they were playing with before asking how long.

Reliability 6/10

Ofcom’s filmed panel of 21 children aged 8 to 18 found gaming working as a social activity, with children running Discord, FaceTime or Snapchat alongside Fortnite and Roblox so they can hear the game and their friends at once. Stopping feels like leaving a room. The parent who knows who was in the room can set the stop at the end of the match instead of the middle.

Supported: Child, Historian · Challenged: None
Sources: Ofcom Children’s Media Lives 2025

Holidays are hard because the structure goes, not because children get worse.

Reliability 5/10

Across 190 studies of primary aged children, about 80% found sleep, activity and screen habits slide on unstructured days compared with school days. This is weekday versus weekend data about weight, not a holiday screen study, so it stands as a principle only: give each holiday day two or three fixed anchors, a morning outing and a set screen window, and let the rest stay loose.

Supported: Clinician · Challenged: Skeptic (a hypothesis paper, not a holiday study)
Sources: Brazendale 2017

The ban will move the pull, not remove it. Games, group chats and AI sit outside it.

Reliability 8/10

From spring 2027 the UK ban is set to cover Snapchat, TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook and X, with messaging apps like WhatsApp outside it and games not named. Australia’s platforms restricted 4.7 million under 16 accounts within a fortnight of its ban, and four months later the regulator found more than eight in ten 10 to 15 year olds still using social media. Britain has been here before: the 9pm watershed from the 1964 Act, PEGI ratings made law in 2012 and the 2007 junk food advert rules all lowered the odds of the worst and none changed what a parent has to do at 8pm. The 2008 Byron Review’s swimming pool still holds: gates and lifeguards, but you also teach the child to swim.

Supported: Economist, Child, Historian · Challenged: None
Sources: GOV.UK 15 June 2026; eSafety Australia; Byron 2008; Ofcom watershed

Every panic before this one ended with the same parenting move surviving: a shared room, a routine, and a grown up beside the child.

Reliability 7/10

In 1958 the first big British television study followed over 4,000 children in five cities, including Norwich before and after its transmitter switched on, and asked what television displaced rather than how long it was on. Twenty three preschoolers who watched Sesame Street with an adult naming the letters and numbers remembered more three days later, and 570 adolescents followed from preschool did better if their early viewing was educational, more consistently for boys, with content mattering more than the medium. Children with a bedroom television scored lower at school while children with a family computer scored higher. The research restarts for every new medium, and the answer has not changed.

Supported: Historian, Skeptic · Challenged: Clinician (no past panic had the parent’s own phone in the loop)
Sources: Himmelweit 1958; Reiser 1984; Anderson 2001; Borzekowski 2005; Orben 2020

Six perspectives

Lens Panels

The Clinician

The bedroom is the whole ballgame

A device present overnight, even unused, costs sleep in 125,000 children, and sleep is the road every other harm travels. In clinic the screen is nearly always the symptom: an unmet sensory need, an avoidance, an exhausted parent. The surgent three year old, the autistic ten year old and the anxious fifteen year old need three different exits, and pooled effect sizes hide all of them.

The Academic

The harm lives in three places, none of them the clock

The best within person evidence points at sleep displacement, the calming shortcut in under sixes, and a susceptible minority of about one in ten. The imaging effects are small and no child study measures dopamine; habitual checking is the only mechanism with longitudinal support. And the parent is inside the effect, because technoference runs both ways.

The Skeptic

Only the boring findings survive

Devices in the bedroom cost sleep and total hours predict almost nothing; that is the list. Gaming disorder is 3 in 100 and the diagnosis itself was contested by 26 scholars. The one trial that improved mental health cut screens for the whole family. Drop “dopamine”, drop “cause”, and never build a claim on a survey of what teenagers say about other teenagers.

The Economist

The money arrives before the phone

97% of UK 8 to 17s play online games and 53% spend inside them, while none of the top 100 games with loot boxes honours the industry’s own consent rule. Parental control adoption is falling and strict use raises conflict, so the defensible product is the agreed pathway with the control as its fence. The ban does not touch games, chats or AI, and Australia shows the pull relocates.

The Historian

The word changed every twenty years; the move never did

Radio crime, comics, the television sets of 1958, arcade games, the bedroom internet: each panic produced a gate, and each gate lowered the odds of the worst without changing what a parent does at 8pm. Sesame Street proved that an adult beside the child naming the letters beats a time limit. Co viewing is also the template for AI: sit beside, ask it something together, check the answer against a person.

The Child

Tell me the phone stays mine and I will tell you what happened

Children hide bad experiences because telling costs them the phone. They rank social media far below money, exams and loneliness among their own worries, and 44% of US teens have already cut back without being told. The game is a room full of friends and the match cannot be paused. For the SEND or lonely child, the bot feels like a friend because the human alternative is a waiting list.

What This Means for Guided Childhood

  • DiGi The bank learns the moments. Migration 257 adds one row per confirmed finding tagged by situation (after school, morning, holidays, parent stress, the calming screen, balanced use, pull and design, history, temperament, ADHD, autism, anxiety, new phone, new game, AI use, AI readiness, forecast) and by age band, so a parent who types “he won’t come off after school” gets the ending research and not a screen time lecture.
  • DiGi DiGi says it a term early. A horizons table by age band (what arrives next, the move to make before it lands, the source and the country the figure is from) goes into every chat and every DiGi’s word brief for this band and the next. The first entries: money and the first account at 8 to 10, the phone and sleep at 11, AI as a friend at 11 to 13, the ban and companions at 13 to 15.
  • DiGi Three questions before any advice. Who were they playing with. How did they feel afterwards. Where does it charge at night. Findings 1, 2 and 13 say those three answers carry more signal than the number of hours, and none of them is a judgement of the parent.
  • Scripts Six new scripts fall straight out of the evidence. The no confiscation promise (finding 9). The end of the match rule (finding 12). The Year 6 phone plan (finding 5). The gift card rule (finding 6). The AI together habit, hints not answers (finding 10). The parent’s protected phone free slot (finding 3).
  • Stages Stage 2 gets the money and the account, Stage 3 gets the phone and sleep, Stage 4 gets companions and the ban. The evidence dates the first spend loop and the first profile to 8 to 12, a full stage before the phone. Stage 3 content leads with the bedroom charging rule because the ABCD cohort says sleep is where the phone shows first. Stage 4 carries the AI companion conversation and what the spring 2027 ban does and does not cover.
  • Schools Year 6 summer is the pitch. Ownership jumps 27 points across the Year 6 to Year 7 boundary and the leavers chat forms in the summer term. A school that runs the phone plan and the no confiscation promise in the summer term reaches every family before the box is opened.
  • Marketing Safe claims and unsafe claims. Safe: a device out of the bedroom is the best house rule (125,198 children); hours are a poor thermometer (355,358 teenagers); the parent’s phone is part of the picture (46% of teens). Unsafe, never post: “addiction”, “dopamine like drugs”, “screens cause ADHD or autism”, and any teen survey about what harms other teens.
  • Ban readiness Build for the relocation. Games, group chats and AI sit outside the under 16 law, Australia’s regulator found eight in ten 10 to 15 year olds still on social media four months after the ban, and 62% of 10 to 16 year olds say a ban feels like punishment. The Stage 4 content under the social_media_law flag should lead with what moves (the group chat, the game, the companion) rather than with what is banned.
Honest limits

Assumptions and the Missing Lens

This briefing rests on

The UK picture rests on Ofcom’s May 2026 Children and Parents report and the forward view on three US bodies: the ABCD cohort, Pew Research Center and Common Sense Media, whose adoption figures are American and are marked as such wherever they appear. Almost every effect in this briefing is an association, and the honest reading of each is a direction to watch rather than a cause. The sandbox blocked direct fetches of most journal, regulator and survey domains during the lens pass, so the verifier pass leaned on exact phrase searches against the primary pages; the ledger says where. The Child lens is mostly survey voice rather than sat down qualitative work, which flatters the articulate teenager and misses the eight year old.

The missing seventh lens

The SEND parent. Justin asked how different children react, and the evidence here on ADHD, autism and anxiety is thin, adult sized and mostly about hours. A lens sat in the home of a child with ADHD or autism would know which transitions work, what a visual schedule looks like at 7pm, and what the game does that nothing else in the day does. The Skeptic named autism and anxiety specific evidence as the gap it could not fill. That is the V3 to run, and it would change the temperament rows in the bank more than anything else would.
