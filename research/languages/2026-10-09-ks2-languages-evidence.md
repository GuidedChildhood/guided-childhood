## 1. Statute: the national curriculum programme of study

**Source:** Department for Education, *National curriculum in England: languages programmes of study*, published 11 September 2013.
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-languages-progammes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-languages-progammes-of-study

**Licence:** confirmed on the page: "This publication is licensed under the terms of the Open Government Licence v3.0 except where otherwise stated", with "© Crown copyright 2013". So we can copy the statements verbatim into `shared/languages-pos.ts`, with an attribution line. OGL v3.0: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/doc/open-government-licence/version/3/

The text below is copied verbatim from the HTML page (downloaded and extracted, not retyped). The hyphens inside it are the Department's own compound words.

### Purpose and aims (verbatim)

> Learning a foreign language is a liberation from insularity and provides an opening to other cultures. A high-quality languages education should foster pupils' curiosity and deepen their understanding of the world. The teaching should enable pupils to express their ideas and thoughts in another language and to understand and respond to its speakers, both in speech and in writing. It should also provide opportunities for them to communicate for practical purposes, learn new ways of thinking and read great literature in the original language. Language teaching should provide the foundation for learning further languages, equipping pupils to study and work in other countries.

> The national curriculum for languages aims to ensure that all pupils:
> - understand and respond to spoken and written language from a variety of authentic sources
> - speak with increasing confidence, fluency and spontaneity, finding ways of communicating what they want to say, including through discussion and asking questions, and continually improving the accuracy of their pronunciation and intonation
> - can write at varying length, for different purposes and audiences, using the variety of grammatical structures that they have learnt
> - discover and develop an appreciation of a range of writing in the language studied

> By the end of each key stage, pupils are expected to know, apply and understand the matters, skills and processes specified in the relevant programme of study.
>
> Schools are not required by law to teach the example content in [square brackets].

### Key stage 2: Foreign language (verbatim)

> Teaching may be of any modern or ancient foreign language and should focus on enabling pupils to make substantial progress in one language. The teaching should provide an appropriate balance of spoken and written language and should lay the foundations for further foreign language teaching at key stage 3. It should enable pupils to understand and communicate ideas, facts and feelings in speech and writing, focused on familiar and routine matters, using their knowledge of phonology, grammatical structures and vocabulary.
>
> The focus of study in modern languages will be on practical communication. If an ancient language is chosen, the focus will be to provide a linguistic foundation for reading comprehension and an appreciation of classical civilisation. Pupils studying ancient languages may take part in simple oral exchanges, while discussion of what they read will be conducted in English. A linguistic foundation in ancient languages may support the study of modern languages at key stage 3.
>
> Pupils should be taught to:

|---|---|
| ks2-1 | listen attentively to spoken language and show understanding by joining in and responding |
| ks2-2 | explore the patterns and sounds of language through songs and rhymes and link the spelling, sound and meaning of words |
| ks2-3 | engage in conversations; ask and answer questions; express opinions and respond to those of others; seek clarification and help* |
| ks2-4 | speak in sentences, using familiar vocabulary, phrases and basic language structures |
| ks2-5 | develop accurate pronunciation and intonation so that others understand when they are reading aloud or using familiar words and phrases* |
| ks2-6 | present ideas and information orally to a range of audiences* |
| ks2-7 | read carefully and show understanding of words, phrases and simple writing |
| ks2-8 | appreciate stories, songs, poems and rhymes in the language |
| ks2-9 | broaden their vocabulary and develop their ability to understand new words that are introduced into familiar written material, including through using a dictionary |
| ks2-10 | write phrases from memory, and adapt these to create new sentences, to express ideas clearly |

| ks2-12 | understand basic grammar appropriate to the language being studied, including (where relevant): feminine, masculine and neuter forms and the conjugation of high-frequency verbs; key features and patterns of the language; how to apply these, for instance, to build sentences; and how these differ from or are similar to English |

> The starred (*) content above will not be applicable to ancient languages.

### Key stage 3: Modern foreign language (verbatim)

> Teaching may be of any modern foreign language and should build on the foundations of language learning laid at key stage 2, whether pupils continue with the same language or take up a new one. Teaching should focus on developing the breadth and depth of pupils' competence in listening, speaking, reading and writing, based on a sound foundation of core grammar and vocabulary. It should enable pupils to understand and communicate personal and factual information that goes beyond their immediate needs and interests, developing and justifying points of view in speech and writing, with increased spontaneity, independence and accuracy. It should provide suitable preparation for further study.
>
> Pupils should be taught to:

**Grammar and vocabulary**

| ID | Statement (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| ks3-1 | identify and use tenses or other structures which convey the present, past, and future as appropriate to the language being studied |
| ks3-2 | use and manipulate a variety of key grammatical structures and patterns, including voices and moods, as appropriate |
| ks3-3 | develop and use a wide-ranging and deepening vocabulary that goes beyond their immediate needs and interests, allowing them to give and justify opinions and take part in discussion about wider issues |
| ks3-4 | use accurate grammar, spelling and punctuation |

**Linguistic competence**

| ID | Statement (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| ks3-5 | listen to a variety of forms of spoken language to obtain information and respond appropriately |
| ks3-6 | transcribe words and short sentences that they hear with increasing accuracy |
| ks3-7 | initiate and develop conversations, coping with unfamiliar language and unexpected responses, making use of important social conventions such as formal modes of address |
| ks3-8 | express and develop ideas clearly and with increasing accuracy, both orally and in writing |
| ks3-9 | speak coherently and confidently, with increasingly accurate pronunciation and intonation |
| ks3-10 | read and show comprehension of original and adapted materials from a range of different sources, understanding the purpose, important ideas and details, and provide an accurate English translation of short, suitable material |
| ks3-11 | read literary texts in the language [such as stories, songs, poems and letters] to stimulate ideas, develop creative expression and expand understanding of the language and culture |
| ks3-12 | write prose using an increasingly wide range of grammar and vocabulary, write creatively to express their own ideas and opinions, and translate short written text accurately into the foreign language |

So there are **12 KS2 statements** and **12 KS3 statements** (4 grammar and vocabulary, 8 linguistic competence). Note that ks3-6 (transcribe what they hear) is the statutory root of dictation, and ks2-2 is the statutory root of phonics.

### Who has to teach it

- **Maintained primary schools:** a foreign language is a statutory national curriculum subject at key stage 2. GOV.UK lists "ancient and modern foreign languages (at key stage 2)" among the compulsory national curriculum subjects at primary. https://www.gov.uk/national-curriculum/key-stage-1-and-2
- **Since when:** September 2014. The Curriculum and Assessment Review final report (p. 89) refers to "introducing a statutory requirement to teach Languages from Key Stage 2 in 2014". Link in section 2.
- **Academies and free schools:** they do not have to follow the national curriculum. GOV.UK: "Other types of school, like academies and private schools, do not have to follow the national curriculum. Academies must teach a broad and balanced curriculum including English, maths and science." https://www.gov.uk/national-curriculum
  In practice almost all academies teach a KS2 language and are inspected on curriculum breadth, but there is no statutory duty to follow this programme of study. Our coverage matrix is still the right sales tool for them, because trusts benchmark against it.
- **Changes since 2014:** none to the languages programme of study. The page shows no "last updated" entry and the text is the 2013 text. The change that is coming is the 2028 national curriculum (section 2).

## 2. Curriculum and Assessment Review (England), 2025, and the government response

**Final report:** *Building a world-class curriculum for all*, Curriculum and Assessment Review, published 5 November 2025. Languages section, pages 87 to 91.
https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/690b96bbc22e4ed8b051854d/Curriculum_and_Assessment_Review_final_report_-_Building_a_world-class_curriculum_for_all.pdf
(Landing page: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/curriculum-and-assessment-review-final-report. I read the identical copy deposited in Parliament, https://data.parliament.uk/DepositedPapers/Files/DEP2025-0723/Curriculum_and_Assessment_Review_final_report.pdf)

**Government response:** *Government response to the Curriculum and Assessment Review*, November 2025, licensed under OGL v3.0. Languages section, page 33.
https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/690b2a4a14b040dfe82922ea/Government_response_to_the_Curriculum_and_Assessment_Review.pdf

### What the Review found (KS2 and KS3)

- The KS2 programme of study "is unclear about what constitutes the requirement to make 'substantial progress in one language'", which leads to "inconsistent interpretations and delivery" (p. 89).
- Teaching time: "some primary pupils receiving less than 30 minutes a week and many secondary students receiving less than two hours" (p. 89, citing British Council Language Trends 2024 and 2023).
- Transition: "only 14% of English secondary schools said they could organise classes based on the languages pupils had studied at Key Stage 2" (p. 89), and many pupils "have to repeat content that they have already covered" or start a new language from scratch (pp. 89 to 90).
- Non specialist teachers: "low subject knowledge among non-specialist teachers and little access to relevant professional development" (p. 89).
- Polling: "80% of primary teachers in state schools in favour of a language-specific list of minimum vocabulary and grammar to be covered by the end of Key Stage 2" (p. 90).

### What the Review recommended

Verbatim from p. 91:

> Updates the Key Stage 2 Languages Programme of Study to include a clearly defined minimum core content for French, German and Spanish to standardise expectations about what 'substantial progress in one language' looks like.

> Should not make immediate changes to the new content of the GCSEs in French, German and Spanish but that the DfE should review the impact of these following the first exams in 2026.

It also suggested local authorities and trusts explore one coordinated main language from KS2 to KS4 (the Hackney Spanish model, pp. 90 to 91).

The core content should cover "commonly occurring phonics, vocabulary, and grammar" and "align closely with the content and teaching approaches at secondary" (p. 90). That is our plan's three pillars, in the Review's own words.

### GCSE and EBacc

- The Review recommended removing the EBacc performance measures while keeping an "Academic Breadth" bucket in Progress 8 (final report, p. 10 of the PDF).
- The government response (p. 45): "we will remove EBacc headline and additional measures at key stage 4. This will take effect for the key stage 4 performance measures for the 2025 to 2026 academic year to be published in autumn 2026." Languages become one of three breadth categories (Humanities, Creative, Languages), with at least two breadth slots drawn from two of the three.
- The British Academy and the Association for Language Learning both warned that dropping the EBacc may reduce GCSE language entries [sector reaction, from search results, not opened in full].

### What the government said it will do (p. 33, verbatim extracts)

> We will work with experts to strengthen the primary languages curriculum and clarify expectations for pupil progress, as well as define core content in French, German and Spanish.

> We will explore the feasibility of developing a new qualification which enables all pupils to have their achievements acknowledged when they are ready rather than at fixed points.

> We will explore how AI and education technology (edtech) can support stronger outcomes in language education, including exploring how these tools can help deliver consistent curriculum content and support more coherent language provision across key stages, as well as reduce teacher workload.

### The timetable that matters to us

- Revised national curriculum to be published "by spring 2027", for first teaching from **September 2028** (government response p. 52).
- Updated GCSEs for first teaching from 2029 and 2030 (p. 52). The 2024 French and Spanish GCSEs are not being changed for now.
- I found no published draft of the new KS2 languages core content as of 9 October 2026. The February 2026 schools white paper (*Every Child Achieving and Thriving*, CP 1508) restates the 2028 date but adds nothing on languages. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69ef496808ecdb5c6f34afc2/Every_Child_Achieving_with_Correction_-_PRINT.pdf

**Read across:** we launch in September 2027 against the 2014 statements, and the first school year we sell into a full second year is the one where the new core content arrives. Section 9 covers what to build for that.

## 3. Ofsted

### The languages research review (7 June 2021)

**Source:** Ofsted, *Research review series: languages* (originally *Curriculum research review series: languages*), 7 June 2021. OGL.
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/curriculum-research-review-series-languages/curriculum-research-review-series-languages

Phonics, vocabulary and grammar sit under the heading "Pillars of progression". That is where the three pillars framing comes from.

- **Phonics.** "Clear and reliable pronunciation and the links between sounds and spelling are integral parts of second language learning." A feature of a strong curriculum: "Planned practice and review of phonemes and how these link to graphemes is in place."
- **Vocabulary.** "This highlights the importance of considering words' frequency when planning a languages curriculum." Leaders should "prioritise high-frequency words" and "consider carefully which topic-based vocabulary (other than high-frequency words) they teach".
- **The topic list problem.** The review notes "Many schemes of work and textbooks are based around topic areas like hobbies and holidays", and that learners need to meet words "across a wide variety of contexts". It does not ban topics. High frequency words come first, and topic words are chosen with care. (The plan's line that "colours, fruit, animals" is the trap the review names is our paraphrase, not the review's wording.)
- **Grammar.** "Grammatical progression needs to be carefully planned across time." The recommended shape is an explicit but short explanation, then practice through listening and reading, then use in speaking and writing.
- **Time.** No minutes per week figure. The review reports "wide variation in the amount of time spent learning languages in primary schools", Year 6 time sometimes cut for national tests, and English pupils getting about "an hour a week less for the first foreign language" than the EU comparison.
- **Transition.** "Transition between primary schools and secondary schools is reported to be inconsistent", and "Assessment information is therefore limited at the point of transition".
- **Assessment.** "Research on language learning in primary schools suggests that assessment is both scarce and limited in effectiveness." A strong feature: "Meaningful assessment is part of the curriculum in primary languages classrooms."

### A later Ofsted languages subject report

**There is none.** The subject report series (2023 to April 2024) covers science, maths, history, geography, PE, music, English and RE, and no languages report has been published. https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/curriculum-research-reviews

The nearest official primary evidence is an Ofsted blog of 4 May 2021 drawn from 24 subject inspections in outstanding primaries (2019 to 2020): https://educationinspection.blog.gov.uk/2021/05/04/languages-in-outstanding-primary-schools/ . Three staffing models all worked; some schools neglected phonics; weaker schools added topic word lists with little progression; "there wasn't much evidence of a joined-up approach between key stage 2 and 3"; and "assessment tended to be very limited in languages."

### Deep dives: withdrawn

**Deep dives no longer exist.** The 2019 guidance that defined them, *Inspecting the curriculum*, was "withdrawn on 10 November 2025": https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/inspecting-the-curriculum/inspecting-the-curriculum . Under it, a deep dive looked at leaders' intent, "curriculum leaders' long- and medium-term thinking and planning", linked lesson visits, work scrutiny, and talks with teachers and pupils. Ofsted never published a languages question list; the lists online are blogs.

The current operating guide (for use from September 2026) does not use the phrase. In its place are learning walks with leaders and focused leadership meetings, where a school may choose "a reading leader or subject leader" and inspectors set aside time "so that you can understand their rationale".
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/school-inspection-toolkit-operating-guide-and-information/school-inspection-operating-guide-for-inspectors-for-use-from-september-2026

### The renewed framework (from November 2025) and the toolkit

Publication page, first published 9 September 2025, updated 28 August 2026, with versions for September 2026 added 12 June 2026: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/school-inspection-toolkit-operating-guide-and-information

- **Report card areas:** safeguarding (met or not met), inclusion, curriculum and teaching, achievement, behaviour and attendance, personal development and wellbeing, early years or post 16 where relevant, leadership and governance.
- **Grades:** exceptional, strong standard, expected standard, needs attention, urgent improvement.

From the toolkit (v2.0, June 2026), https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a915d1c01bbff0bf8f97b51/Schools_inspection_toolkit.pdf :
- The curriculum is "ambitious, broad and balanced, informed by the best available evidence" and "at least as ambitious in breadth and depth as the national curriculum".
- "the curriculum is coherently planned and sequenced so that pupils build knowledge and skills sequentially and cumulatively"
- "time is available within the curriculum for revisiting content and dealing with gaps"

- Expected standard: "suitable and well planned for each subject and year group. It identifies clear end points".
- Schools "may adopt, adapt or construct their curriculum", which is the door a bought scheme walks through.

### Vocabulary size and how the lists are built

- "Students will be expected to know 1,200 lexical items for foundation tier, and a further 500 lexical items for higher tier" (para 13). So 1,200 at foundation and 1,700 at higher.
- Plus up to 30 short multi word phrases and up to 20 cultural or geographical items (para 15).
- "At least 85% of the 1,200 and 1,700 words selected should be from the 2,000 most frequent words occurring in the most widely used standard forms of the language" (para 19).
- Frequency must come from a corpus of "20 million words or more" covering speech and writing (footnote 3). Named examples: Lonsdale and Le Bras (2009), *A frequency dictionary of French*; Davies and Davies (2018), *A frequency dictionary of Spanish* (2nd ed.), both Routledge.
- Reading texts may gloss up to 2% of words, and up to 2% may be untaught cognates (para 16).
- A line that matters to us: "Because no vocabulary is specified for KS2 or KS3, the Vocabulary List proposed for GCSE must be comprehensive and should make no assumptions about vocabulary previously taught" (para 13). There is no official KS2 word list yet.

### Who publishes the actual word lists (this corrects the plan)

**The DfE does not publish a full GCSE vocabulary list.** Each exam board writes its own 1,700 item list to the DfE rules. The DfE publishes only:
- **Annex E**, the words the grammar annex forces onto every list (irregular forms of high frequency verbs, exemplar nouns and so on). About 280 rows for French foundation and about 270 for Spanish foundation, more at higher. Crown copyright, OGL.
- **Annex D**, worked examples of what counts as a regular inflection or derivation.

The full lists belong to the boards:
- AQA GCSE French 8652, list in specification Appendix 2, frequency source Lonsdale and Le Bras: https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/french/gcse/french-8652/specification/subject-content/vocabulary
- AQA GCSE Spanish 8692, frequency source Davies and Davies: https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/spanish/gcse/spanish-8692/specification/subject-content/vocabulary
- Pearson Edexcel GCSE Spanish 2024 vocabulary extract: https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/GCSE/Spanish/2024/specification-and-sample-assessments/vocabulary-and-grammar-updates-from-gcse-spanish-specification-issue-1.pdf [found in search, not opened]

### Sound symbol correspondences (SSCs)

The grammar annexes list the SSCs pupils must know to "read out loud and transcribe with sufficient accuracy". Only sounds that differ from English are listed.

- **French (Annex A), 35 items:** silent final consonant; a; i/y; eu; e; au/eau/closed o/ô; ou; u; silent final e; é (-er, -ez); en/an/em/am; on/om; ain/in/aim/im; è/ê/ai; oi/oy; ch; ç (and soft c); qu; j; -tion; -ien; s liaison; t liaison; n liaison; x liaison; h; un; -gn-; r; open eu/œu; open o; -s-; th; -ill-/-ille; -aill-/ail.
- **Spanish (Annex C), 29 items:** a; o; e; i; u; ll; ch; ca; co; cu; cu + vowel; ce; ci; z; que; qui; ga; go; gu; ge; gi; gue; gui; j; ñ; v; -r-/-r; rr/r-/-r- (word initial r, and r after n, l or s, sounds as rr); silent h. Higher tier adds the stress and written accent rules.

The example words for each SSC sit in a table column that my text extraction dropped; read them from the PDF before writing the phonics sequence. These two lists are the obvious end point for our Year 3 to Year 6 phonics strand: if a child leaves Year 6 secure on most of them, secondary does not start again.

### Dictation and reading aloud

From para 7 of the subject content:
- Listening includes "dictation of short, spoken extracts (including some vocabulary from outside the vocabulary list) with credit for accurate spelling".
- Speaking includes "read aloud a short written text and undertake a short unprepared conversation relating to the text".

Both rest on SSC knowledge, which is why phonics in KS2 pays off at 16. The plan's "dictation from Year 4" is consistent with this.

## 5. NCELP, the Language Hubs and the people who carry them on

### Who is who

- **NCELP** (National Centre for Excellence for Language Pedagogy) was the University of York and the Cam Academy Trust, funded by the DfE, and published resources from 2 December 2018 to 2 March 2023. Since 3 March 2023 the work carries on as **Language-Driven Pedagogy (LDP)** at the University of York. https://ldpedagogy.york.ac.uk/cite . The old resources.ncelp.org now points to https://ldpedagogy.york.ac.uk/ . NCLE also hosts an archive copy "reproduced in their original form as received from NCELP": https://ncle.ucl.ac.uk/ncelp-resources
- **The Language Hubs** were run by **NCLE** (National Consortium for Languages Education: UCL IOE with the British Council and the Goethe-Institut), contracted in March 2023, with pedagogy following the Teaching Schools Council MFL Pedagogy Review. They were not run by NCELP. Source: DfE, *Language programmes evaluation: interim report*, September 2025: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68b86adf536d629f9c82aa5b/Language_programmes_evaluation_interim_report.pdf . That report finds 89% of Year 7 pupils had learned a language at primary, mostly French, and that primary schools are "'time poor', and languages were not a priority for them".
- **Now:** Schools Week reported on 16 September 2025 that the hubs were replaced by Language Educators Online (LEO) and 14 strategic learning networks (press source): https://schoolsweek.co.uk/language-hubs-showed-signs-of-positive-impacts-before-being-cut/ . NCLE's LEO page says it is "funded by the Department for Education" and serves primary teachers too: https://ncle.ucl.ac.uk/language-educators-online . The government response (section 2) names NCLE and LEO as the support route for the new curriculum.

### Licence

The LDP site states: "All resources are licensed as CC BY-NC-SA 4.0" (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/4.0/). The Rachel Hawkes primary French and Spanish pages state the same licence (section 6). **NC** means no commercial use and **SA** means anything adapted must carry the same licence, so we cannot reuse or adapt NCELP or LDP materials in a paid product without written permission from York. We can follow the same published research and principles in our own materials, which is what the plan already says. This confirms the plan's licence assumption.

### The pedagogy in a paragraph

NCELP and LDP build a languages curriculum on three planned strands, matching Ofsted's "pillars of progression": **phonics** (sound spelling links taught explicitly in a sequence and revisited), **vocabulary** (chosen mainly by frequency, with the most useful verbs early, and recycled many times across units rather than taught once per topic), and **grammar** (explained briefly and explicitly, then practised first through listening and reading where the meaning depends on noticing the form, then in speaking and writing). Lessons are built to make pupils attend to meaning and form at the same time, and old material is deliberately brought back in later units. LDP lists rationale documents for each strand at https://ldpedagogy.york.ac.uk/about/rationales-and-research ; I opened that page but not the documents themselves, so the detail of this paragraph beyond the three strands and frequency is **[NOT VERIFIED]** against their text.

## 8. Data protection: speech recognition for children in the browser

### The fact that changes the plan

The plan says speech recognition "runs in the browser only. No child audio is ever stored." The browser API does not guarantee that by default. MDN, *Using the Web Speech API* (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Speech_API/Using_the_Web_Speech_API):

> By default, using speech recognition on a web page involves a server-based recognition engine. Your audio is sent to a web service for recognition processing, so it won't work offline.

And on the `SpeechRecognition` page (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/SpeechRecognition): "On some browsers, like Chrome, using Speech Recognition on a web page involves a server-based recognition engine."

MDN also documents the fix: set `recognition.processLocally = true` before starting, after checking `SpeechRecognition.available()` and, if needed, `SpeechRecognition.install()` for the language pack. With on device processing "neither the audio nor the transcribed speech are sent to a third-party service for processing." MDN marks the API as not Baseline; which browser versions support `processLocally` I did not verify **[NOT VERIFIED]**.

So the rule for the build: **speech recognition only with `processLocally = true`, and if on device recognition is not available, the "say it" button falls back to listen and repeat with no recognition at all.** Never the default server path, because that sends a child's voice to the browser maker.

### UK GDPR and the Children's Code

- **A child's voice is personal data.** It becomes special category biometric data only when processed to identify someone uniquely. ICO biometric guidance, key concepts: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/biometric-data-guidance-biometric-recognition/key-data-protection-concepts/ (from search result text; page not opened in full). We never identify by voice, so we stay out of Article 9, as long as no recogniser we call does voice matching.
- **Schools app:** the Children's Code does not apply to schools processing pupil data for education, and an edtech provider acting only on a school's instructions, not offered direct to consumers and not using the data for its own purposes (such as product development) is generally outside it. ICO, *The Children's code and education technologies*: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/the-children-s-code-and-education-technologies-edtech/ . With on device recognition and nothing sent to us, we process no pupil data at all, which is the cleanest position.
- **Child app (home practice):** sold to families, so it is an information society service likely to be accessed by children and the Code applies in full: best interests, data minimisation, privacy by default, DPIA, no nudge techniques. ICO, *Introduction to the Children's code*: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/introduction-to-the-childrens-code/ ; standard 2, DPIA: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/2-data-protection-impact-assessments/
- **The ICO audits found** almost 70% of edtech providers were in fact controllers for some uses (product development, analytics) without realising it, and some sub processors kept children's data to train AI. *Edtech examined*: https://ico.org.uk/media2/13yfm55z/edtech-examined-key-findings-from-our-audits.pdf (search result text). In June 2026 the ICO said it is discussing a possible edtech code: https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/06/ico-statement-on-edtech-examined-report/ (not opened).

### What to avoid

1. The default (server) Web Speech path. Always `processLocally = true` or no recognition.
2. Any cloud speech API (vendor ASR, an LLM audio endpoint) for children's speech, in school or at home.
3. Recording, uploading or keeping audio, even "temporarily for quality".
4. Storing transcripts or pronunciation scores against a named child in the schools app. A class level count is enough.
5. Using any child audio or score for product development or model training, which would make us a controller and pull the schools product into the Code.
6. Voice matching of any kind (that would be special category data).
7. Microphone on by default. The child taps to speak each time, with a visible "listening" state.
8. Streaks or scores that pressure a child to keep speaking; the Code's nudge standard applies to the home app.

The hub's "Data protection note on in browser speech" should say these eight things in plain words for the school's data protection officer.

## 9. What this means for our plan

Items marked **contradicts** are places where the evidence says the plan should change.

1. **Statute confirmed.** 12 KS2 statements and 12 KS3 statements, Crown copyright under OGL v3.0, so `shared/languages-pos.ts` can hold them verbatim with an attribution line. Map phonics to ks2-2 and dictation to ks3-6.
2. **A new KS2 languages programme of study is coming.** The government accepted the Review: core content for French, German and Spanish (phonics, vocabulary, grammar), published by spring 2027, taught from September 2028. Version the statements file (2014 and 2028), keep the coverage generator rerunnable, and design the spine so units can be remapped. "Ready for the 2028 curriculum" is a real selling line, and the response invites edtech to help deliver consistent content.
3. **Contradicts: the GCSE word lists are not Crown copyright.** The DfE publishes only the rules and Annex E (the grammar driven required words, about 280 per language at foundation), which are OGL. The 1,700 item lists belong to AQA, Pearson and Eduqas. Build the spine as a subset of Annex E plus words found on both the AQA and Edexcel lists, used as a check, not copied. Take frequency ranks from a source we are licensed to use.
4. **Contradicts: Ofsted deep dives were withdrawn on 10 November 2025.** Rename the hub's "deep dive pack" to an inspection pack written in the toolkit's words: clear end points per year group, coherent sequence, time for revisiting, subject rigour, and the leader's rationale.
5. **Contradicts: browser speech recognition is not on device by default.** Chrome sends audio to a server unless `processLocally = true`. Make on device processing mandatory, and fall back to listen and repeat with no recognition when it is unavailable. Never a cloud speech API for children.
6. **Contradicts, in part: 95% known words is a floor, not a target.** Hu and Nation put unassisted reading at 98%. Keep 95% for teacher led class readers with pictures; set 98% for anything a child reads alone at home, and make the vocabulary guard check both.
7. **Contradicts, in part: interleaving new words hurts.** The best meta analysis found blocking beat interleaving for word learning. Introduce new words in themed blocks; interleave only what children confuse (gender, verb endings, look alike sounds). Mixing old words into starters is spaced retrieval, which is fine.
8. **Spacing schedule.** 1, 3, 7, 21 days holds words for a few months. To hold them across the year, add a review near day 60 and at term end, and let each child's next gap grow or shrink with success (successive relearning), rather than a fixed ladder.
9. **Phonics end point.** The GCSE annexes list 35 French and 29 Spanish sound symbol correspondences. Use them as the Year 3 to Year 6 phonics target, so the transition record can say which ones a class has secured. Dictation from Year 4 fits the GCSE.
10. **Contradicts, wording only: the Ofsted review names "hobbies and holidays", not "colours, fruit, animals".** It does not ban topics; it says high frequency words first and topic words chosen with care. Fix the plan's line before it reaches any sales copy.
11. **Licences.** NCELP and LDP are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, so study and never reuse (confirmed). The Language Hubs were NCLE, not NCELP, and have been replaced by LEO. Oak's KS2 French and Spanish are under the OGL, so unlike NCELP they could legally be adapted with attribution; that is a choice for Justin, not a default.
12. **The market and the home loop.** Duolingo for Schools closes 31 July 2027 (confirmed at source). A languages only school pays £259 to £299 a year for Language Angels; Oak and LDP are free, so we win on non specialist ease, done for you admin and the home loop. Time is the strongest lever in UK primary evidence (Graham and colleagues 2017; the Review's "less than 30 minutes a week"). The home app is sold to families, so the Children's Code applies in full: a DPIA before Phase 3.
