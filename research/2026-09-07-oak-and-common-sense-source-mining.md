# Oak and Common Sense, mined from the live sites, 7 September 2026

Justin sent two links and asked for everything we can use from them:

- `thenational.academy/teachers/programmes/computing-primary-ks1/units/digital-painting/lessons/painting-using-computers`
- `commonsense.org/education/digital-literacy`

This is the read of both, what we take, what we already beat, and what we
deliberately leave. It is the evidence sitting under the Oak plan
(`plans/2026-09-07-oak-as-the-basis-plan.md`), and it corrects that plan in
two places where the live site does not match the downloaded pack.

## 1. What the live Oak lesson page actually carries

The page is one KS1 Year 1 Computing lesson, "Painting using computers",
from the Digital Painting unit, Oak with the Raspberry Pi Foundation.
Its sections in order:

| Section | What is in it |
| --- | --- |
| Lesson slides | A download, PPTX |
| Lesson details | Learning outcome, key learning points, keywords, common misconception, **teacher tip**, **equipment**, licence |
| Lesson video | With a **transcript** and a **sign language** option |
| Worksheet | A download, PPTX and PDF |
| Prior knowledge starter quiz | Four questions, question and answer versions |
| Assessment exit quiz | Four questions, question and answer versions |

The content itself, so the shape is judged on the real thing:

- **Learning outcome**, one sentence: "I can use digital tools to draw a picture."
- **Key learning points**, three, each a flat declarative sentence: digital
  devices are controlled in different ways; the paintbrush tool makes marks
  and the erase tool removes marks; the fill tool adds colour to a closed
  shape.
- **Keywords**, four, each a term plus a pupil facing definition. "Tool: a
  selectable feature in a program assisting task completion."
- **Common misconception**, stated as the belief and then the correction in
  pupil facing words: pupils assume a screen works like paper and can be
  touched to draw, when "some devices use a finger but other devices can be
  used with a mouse, a trackpad or in other ways".
- **Teacher tip**: demonstrate handling the device before the practical.
- **Equipment**: a device with freehand digital painting software, paintz.app
  named as an example.
- **Starter quiz**, four questions, all recall of what a pupil should already
  know: what do we look at to see pictures, which device makes pictures, how
  do you switch a computer on, what do we paint with.
- **Exit quiz**, four questions, and the formats are mixed on purpose: true
  or false, a three pair match, a short answer, and a fill the blank.

## 2. Two corrections to the Oak plan

The plan was written from the downloaded pack. The live page disagrees on
two points and the live page is what a teacher actually sees.

1. **Oak does not publish named learning cycles.** The Aila prompt guide
   lists learning cycles as section three of ten, and the deck has a cycle
   map slide, but the public lesson page names no cycles at all. So the
   plan's "Oak is ahead of us on named cycles" is only half true: Oak is
   ahead in its authoring spec, not on the page. That matters because it
   means the cycle map is ours to design rather than ours to copy, and
   Common Sense turns out to publish a better version of it (section 4).
2. **Oak does not publish a prior knowledge statement.** It publishes a
   prior knowledge *quiz*, which is a different and arguably better thing:
   the readiness check is four questions a teacher can actually run, not a
   paragraph they have to interpret. We should build the quiz first and
   treat the statement as secondary.

Two sections in the live page were not in the plan at all, and both are
cheap and useful: **teacher tip** and **equipment**.

## 3. What we take from Oak

- **Key learning points as scannable data.** We hold recap points and
  objective gains, but no single list a teacher can read in ten seconds to
  decide what their class will know by the end.
- **The starter quiz as a real printable**, question version and answer
  version. Ours is one start card prompt.
- **The exit quiz as a real printable**, mixed formats, both versions. Ours
  is an exit card plus two in slide choices, which are checks for
  understanding, a different job.
- **Teacher tip**, one line per module: the thing that goes wrong if you
  skip it. For us that is usually about handling the room on a sensitive
  topic, not handling a device.
- **Equipment**, one line per module. For most of ours the honest answer is
  "nothing beyond the board", and saying so out loud is a selling point to a
  school with a trolley of tablets that never charges.
- **A transcript on every lesson video.** We honour reduced motion and carry
  SEND adaptations on all 21, but a deaf pupil currently has no route
  through a video beat. This is a real accessibility gap and Oak is right.
- **The download bundle expectation**: deck, worksheet, both quizzes, as
  files. Already logged as P2 item 13 in the master audit.

## 4. What the Common Sense pages actually carry

Two pages were read: the digital literacy landing page and one whole lesson,
"Pause for People", Grade K, so the anatomy is judged on a real lesson and
not on the marketing of it.

**The curriculum shape.** More than 165 lessons, K to 12, thirteen per grade
at K to 2 rising to eighteen per grade at 3 to 8. Six strands, taught every
year at rising depth: Media Balance and Well Being; Privacy and Security;
Digital Footprint and Identity; Relationships and Communication;
Cyberbullying, Digital Drama and Hate Speech; News and Media Literacy. Each
lesson is tagged with an activity type from a fixed list: drawing, video,
dilemma discussion, game, media analysis, researching skill, personal
reflection, hands on craft, puzzle, card activity, poetry, song, storybook,
media creation, AI literacy.

**One lesson, in full.** "Pause for People", Grade K, 30 minutes.

- **Essential question**, and it is a question a child would ask, not a
  teacher's objective: "How do you say goodbye to technology when you don't
  want to?"
- **Learning objectives** in "students will" form, two of them.
- **Named sections, each with a verb, a title and a runtime**:
  Explore: See You Later, Tech! (5 mins). Chant: Pause, Breathe, Finish Up!
  (5 mins). Perform: Act It Out! (10 mins). Reflect: Pause and Think Moment
  (10 mins).
- **Vocabulary**, three words at Grade K: device, frustrated, pause.
- **A named portable routine** the child carries out of the room: Pause,
  Breathe, Finish Up.
- **Materials**: slides, a video in English and Spanish, a pupil handout, a
  poem poster, a family activity, family tips in several languages, and an
  SEL conversation starter.

## 5. What we take from Common Sense

- **The essential question.** This is the strongest single borrow on this
  page. Our lessons open with an outcome; a question a pupil would actually
  ask is a better door into the same room, and it costs one line per module.
- **The cycle pattern, verb plus title plus minutes.** This is the cycle map
  Oak specifies but does not publish, and Common Sense's version is better
  because the verb tells a pupil what they will be doing, not just what they
  will know. Our timing strings already carry the minutes and four modules
  already name their cycles in prose, so most of this is promotion of data we
  hold rather than invention.
- **The activity type label.** A teacher choosing a lesson for last period on
  a Friday wants to know it is a discussion or a game before they open it.
- **The conversation starter as its own artefact**, separate from the parent
  note. Ours currently bundles the two.

## 6. Where we already match or beat both

Said plainly, because the job was to find gaps, not to feel good.

- **Scripts on every slide.** Oak's deck has no speaker notes and Common
  Sense gives a lesson plan a specialist reads and adapts. Ours is word for
  word, which is why a non specialist can teach it cold. Neither of them has
  this and it stays.
- **A named portable routine.** Common Sense has Pause, Breathe, Finish Up
  on one lesson. We have Notice, Choose, Tell tagged on all 21 modules
  already, from migration 220.
- **Misconceptions.** All 21 of ours carry them. Oak carries one per lesson.
- **The spiral.** Common Sense's best structural idea, the same strand
  returning every year a rung deeper, is already our staircase.
- **AI.** They start AI literacy at Grade 6. We teach it from age 4.
- **Everything around the lesson**: safeguarding and DSL notes, staff
  briefings, the fifteen minute induction, SEND and EAL adaptations on four
  needs, the parent note and home code, the passport, a paper fallback for
  every lesson, class interactives, statutory mapping. Neither site carries
  this layer.

## 7. What we deliberately do not copy

- **No content from either site.** Oak is Open Government Licence version
  3.0 and Common Sense is its own licence. We take the shape of a lesson,
  never the substance of one. Nothing from either pack goes into our decks.
- **Oak's no notes approach.** Scripts stay on every slide.
- **Oak's image minimisation.** Their cognitive load argument is sound for a
  painting lesson. Our video beats and interactives teach the thing words
  cannot, a fake spreading and a feed learning, so they stay, and reduced
  motion stays honoured.
- **Common Sense's US framing.** Grade labels, US law and US helplines do not
  transfer. Ours stay UK: RSHE 2025, Education for a Connected World,
  Report Remove, CEOP.

## 8. The contract this produces

A Guided Childhood lesson, after this work, carries: an essential question, a
single action outcome, an I can ladder, named cycles with a verb, a title, an
outcome and minutes, prior knowledge, key learning points, keywords with
definitions, misconceptions, a starter quiz and an exit quiz both as
printables in question and answer versions, a word for word script on every
slide, a worksheet with an answer key, SEND and EAL adaptations, a parent
note, a DSL note, a paper fallback, and statutory mapping.

That is Oak's ten sections, plus Common Sense's essential question and cycle
naming, plus the eight things neither of them does.
