## The lesson

- **Module:** `ks3-34-who-owns-what-you-make`, KS3, Years 7 to 9, Orbit.
- **Outcome:** "I can say who owns a thing I made, remixed or made with AI,
  and how to use other people's work fairly."
- **Why KS3:** this is the age of the first public posts, edits and remixes:
  a song under a video, a meme from someone's photo, an AI image for
  homework. The KS2 lesson teaches crediting; this one teaches what the law
  and the platforms actually do, and what a pupil owns themselves.
- **Spine:** the house arc (retrieval starter, teach, practise, prove with
  choice questions, recap), the same slide types as the other KS3 decks, a
  verdict sort, the passport page, a parent note home.

## The content, every claim sourced before it is written

A research pass with a citation check, UK law only, plain enough for Year 7:

1. What copyright is in the UK and that it is automatic (Copyright, Designs
   and Patents Act 1988; UK IPO guidance).
2. The fair dealing exceptions that matter to a pupil: quotation, criticism
   and review, parody, caricature and pastiche (2014 regulations), and their
   limits.
3. Music under a video, memes, screenshots and reposts: what the platforms'
   own terms and takedown systems do.
4. What a pupil owns: their own photos, writing and drawings, and what
   posting them licenses to the platform.
5. AI made images and text: who owns them under section 9(3), what is
   unsettled and under government consultation, and the honest line for a
   classroom ("the law is still being decided; credit the tool and say what
   you did").
6. Creative Commons and how to credit, the KS2 lesson's habit carried up.
7. The exact EfCW 2020 statements for strand 8 at ages 11 to 14 and the KS3
   computing programme line on reusing and repurposing digital artefacts, for
   the mapping.

## The research (8 October 2026, primary sources fetched)

Copies of what was fetched are in the session scratchpad; every line below has
its source.

- **Automatic copyright.** CDPA 1988 s.1(1), s.9(1) ("author... means the
  person who creates it"), s.16 (the owner's exclusive rights, including to
  "communicate the work to the public"), s.12(2) (70 years after the author's
  death). UK IPO: "You get copyright protection automatically... There is not
  a register of copyright works in the UK." gov.uk/copyright
- **Fair dealing, named purposes only.** Quotation s.30(1ZA), criticism and
  review s.30(1), caricature, parody or pastiche s.30A (from 1 October 2014,
  SI 2014/2356), illustration for instruction s.32. UK IPO: "There is no
  statutory definition of fair dealing... how would a fair minded and honest
  person have dealt with the work?" Two tests: does it substitute for the
  original, and was the amount necessary. The UK has no general fair use
  (CDPA s.28; the government's March 2026 AI report names the USA among the
  countries with one, not the UK). YouTube's and Instagram's "fair use" pages
  are US law and must not be taught as UK law.
- **Platforms.** YouTube Content ID claims block, monetise or track; a legal
  removal is a strike; three strikes and the channel can be terminated; strikes
  expire after 90 days with Copyright School. TikTok's IP policy: infringing
  content "may be removed" and repeat infringers banned. Meta's music
  guidelines: content "may be blocked, muted or removed". Instagram's help page
  lists what does NOT protect you: giving credit, a disclaimer, not profiting,
  finding it online, others posting it too. TikTok's muting page could not be
  fetched; do not quote it.
- **What a pupil owns.** They are the author. TikTok's UK terms (July 2026)
  4.9: "We don't own your content", but posting grants TikTok a worldwide,
  royalty free licence, and other users one too through Duet, Stitch and
  download. Instagram: "We do not claim ownership", with a similar licence.
- **AI made work.** s.9(3) and s.178: for a computer generated work the author
  is "the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the
  work are undertaken"; s.12(7) 50 years. The government's report of 18 March
  2026 says the prompter will usually be the author, calls s.9(3) a "legal
  contradiction" untested in court, and proposes removing protection for
  wholly machine made work. Not law yet. Classroom line: the law is still
  being decided; credit the tool and say what you did.
- **Creative Commons.** CC BY 4.0: share and adapt with "appropriate credit,
  provide a link to the license, and indicate if changes were made". Credit by
  TASL: Title, Author, Source, Licence.
- **EfCW 2020, strand 8, ages 11 to 14** (11 statements, PDF page 45),
  including fair dealing, Creative Commons, protecting my own work, plagiarism,
  and "why controlling copyright of my content may be limited when using social
  media". KS3 computing: "create, reuse, revise and repurpose digital
  artefacts... with attention to trustworthiness".
- **Verdict sort, clear answers only:** my own drawing posted on TikTok (mine,
  with a licence to TikTok); a CC BY photo with a TASL credit (fine if
  credited); two lines of a poem quoted in a review, named (fine if credited);
  reposting someone's photo with "credit to them, no copyright intended" (not
  yours to use); a whole chart song under a YouTube video (not yours to use).
  Parody, memes and AI images are left out of the sort because none has a
  single clear verdict; they are taught as "it depends, and here is how to
  decide".
