# The long form newsletter, ideas bank

Justin, 6 November 2026: "can we get soe long form newsltters idea togther".

Six ideas, ranked, plus a series that would give the newsletter a reason to
exist between news events. Each one says what the argument is, why it needs the
length, what evidence we already hold, and where the proof path lands in the
product.

## First, two things the repo already settles

**Long form is what you are already writing.** The content engine skill says 800
to 1,200 words. The last five issues ran 1,398, 1,714, 1,365, 2,146 and 416. So
the spec is stale rather than the practice, and the one that broke the pattern
downward was the 26 October chatbot note inside a waterfall pack, not a proper
issue.

Recommendation: set the long form issue at **1,800 to 2,500 words** and leave the
short note as its own named thing for waterfall packs. The 15 October Social
Reckoning issue at 2,146 is the shape to copy: a cold open, then sections that
each do one job, then something to do on Sunday. If this is adopted it belongs in
`content-engine/SKILL.md`, because a format that lives only in a chat message
gets forgotten by the next session.

**We cannot tell which past issue worked.** There is no Substack analytics file
anywhere in the repo, so there is no open rate, no subscriber count and no way to
say which of the twenty seven past issues earned anything. This is the same
measurement gap as the image type question on 5 November, and the same one column
fixes it. Until then every ranking below is judgement, not evidence, and it is
labelled as such.

## 1. Nobody gets a free pass

**The argument.** Court filings show Meta discussing a whisper strategy against
its loudest critic. A newspaper then implied that the critic's critics were paid
by Meta. Both of those can be true and neither tells you anything about whether
the evidence is good. We have reached the point where both camps argue about
motives because the effect sizes will not settle it.

**Why it needs the length.** This is the one idea on the list where the short
version is the thing being criticised. A 2,000 character post can assert that
careful reading matters. An essay can perform it: here is what the filing says,
here is what it does not, here is what Odgers actually said, here is what the
National Academies actually found, and here is why none of it lets you off the
hook tonight. The form is the argument.

**Evidence we hold.** Strong, and mostly verified on 5 November. The National
Academies sentences were read from the primary Highlights PDF and are exact.
Odgers' statements were read from her own Substack of 29 October. The Meta note
wording could NOT be verified against the filing (CourtListener 403s us, The
Verge and the NY Post block us) and two outlets give incompatible versions, so
the essay describes that line rather than quoting it, exactly as GDC-053 does.

**Proof path.** Thin on purpose. This is a one in ten piece where the thesis
lands plainly: the response should be proportional to the evidence, and the
things that actually predict a child's mental health are not on the front page.
The stage check gets one line at the end, no more.

**Risk to know.** It criticises a national newspaper's implication on the basis
of her characterisation of it. That wording is load bearing and must stay
attributed to her.

## 2. The age is the easy part

**The argument.** Thirteen for an account, sixteen for social media, eighteen for
an AI companion. Three different legal answers about the same child in the same
year, each arrived at by a different route, and not one of them derived from
evidence about what a child that age can actually do. So what would a readiness
test look like, and why does nobody have one.

**Why it needs the length.** The provenance of each age is a short history
lesson: thirteen traces to American privacy law rather than to any finding about
thirteen year olds, sixteen to the UK restriction announced in June, eighteen to
Character.AI closing open ended chats in November 2025 under lawsuits and an FTC
inquiry. Then the constructive half needs worked examples by age band, which a
post cannot carry. The three observable signals are the payoff and they are
genuinely savable.

**Evidence we hold.** The three ages were verified on 23 October. The honest
admission that we have no outcome evidence for the stage check is already written
in Justin's own words in GDC-046 and is the strongest line in it.

**Proof path.** Direct and clean. The stage check IS the attempt at this, and the
piece says plainly that it is a starting point rather than a measurement.

**Why it is ranked second rather than first.** It is evergreen, so it keeps. The
Meta piece is topical and decays.

## 3. What you would have to hand over

**The argument.** A ban on under sixteens needs a way to know who is sixteen.
Ofcom has named four ways. Each one fixes a problem and asks a family to hand
over something else: a child's face, a child's bank details, a passport, or a new
ID that sits behind every app. The live question is not only whether there should
be a ban, but what you would be willing to hand over to make one work.

**Why it needs the length.** Four methods, four different privacy shapes, plus
the exclusion problem. It is a decision aid, not an argument, and parents will
face this choice inside a year. Nobody has written the plain English version.

**Evidence we hold.** Mixed, and this is the one to be careful with. Confirmed:
the four methods and Ofcom's phrase for them, the circumvention wording, the
twelve month circumvention research, the consultation on draft guidance, the
29 October date and the title. Single secondary source only: the 94 percent, the
90 percent bank figure. Corrected on 5 November: the 150,000 is CitizenCard's
estimate from a sample Ofcom itself calls non representative, not Ofcom's own
figure, and there is no Ofcom margin of error stated in years. **Ofcom 403s this
container**, so the primary PDF is still unread and Justin opening it in a browser
would settle the lot.

**Proof path.** Strong. A check decides who gets through a door; it decides
nothing about what a fifteen year old does with a friend's login. That gap is the
product.

**Why this may be the most useful piece on the list.** It is the only one a parent
would bookmark and come back to in 2027.

## 4. The syllabus a law wrote

**The argument.** Australia's ban is three months old and already teaching. Half
the children who still held accounts said the platform never checked their age,
and 37 percent simply put themselves down as sixteen or over. That is a child's
first real lesson in digital life and a law delivered it: identity online is
something you assert, and nobody checks. And parents taught it first.

**Why it needs the length.** GDC-044 had to cut one of its two scenes from
Justin's own house to fit the character band, and the house rule allows one scene
per post. An essay holds both: the Covid admission about using YouTube as a
babysitter, and the Saturday football row where the lesson was that he had
nothing to give his son instead. The confession is what makes the argument land
rather than scold, and the long form is the only place both fit.

**Evidence we hold.** Strong. Citation checked in October: three confirmed, three
corrected, and a false claim about an ID breach cut before it went anywhere. The
eSafety and BMJ figures, the France constitutional council decision and the 2011
First Monday survey are all traced.

**Proof path.** A ban switches the device off. It does not sit down with your
child. That is the whole product in two sentences.

## 5. Two governments, same mistake, opposite directions

**The argument.** Sweden threw out its national digitisation plan for not being
sufficiently evidence based, then replaced it with a printed textbook policy that
is also not evidence based, and got far better press for the second. Britain is
about to legislate before the trial that tests the thing it is banning reports.
Same move, opposite directions, and the press notices only one of them.

**Why it needs the length.** Two national stories side by side, plus the gap
between what Sweden's own education agency said and what its government said,
which is the detail the viral version always leaves out. The agency named
segregation; the government named digitalisation and migration. Only one of those
is fixable with a book order, and that is the hidden thread arriving without
being announced.

**Evidence we hold.** Strong and already verified for GDC-043: the Reimers 2025
paper on the crisis framing, the policy dates, the PISA caveat that those students
were schooled before the reform, and the honest effect sizes on paper versus
screen reading. Three details from the viral version failed verification and were
dropped.

**Needs verifying.** The IRL trial timing against the ban date. The register's
idea row still marks the March 2027 start as unverified reporting, and our own
docs say spring 2027.

## 6. A year of posting, and the thirteen clicks

**The argument.** 275,018 impressions in a year, 70 percent of them out of
network, 165 clicks on the profile button and **thirteen** clicks on links in
posts across the whole year. And one near controlled experiment: the same
Australian story, opened two ways, did 15,186 and under 1,154.

**Why it needs the length.** The A/B pair has to be shown in full, both openings
side by side, for the reader to feel why one worked. That is impossible in a post
and trivial in an essay.

**Evidence we hold.** Our own export, zero verification risk. It is the only idea
here that cannot be wrong.

**The catch, and it is a real one.** This is a piece for founders and marketers,
not for the parent of a nine year old. It belongs on the **LinkedIn newsletter**,
not the parent Substack. Putting it in front of parents would spend their
attention on our business rather than their child, which is the opposite of the
hidden thread. Flagged as venue specific rather than ranked against the others.

## The series worth considering: The Readiness Papers

Four issues with a destination, pegged to the ban calendar rather than to
whatever broke this week. The reason to build it is that a newsletter which only
reacts has no reason to be opened; a series that is going somewhere does.

| | Issue | The job |
|---|---|---|
| One | The age is the easy part (idea 2) | Establish that the number was never the hard part |
| Two | What you would have to hand over (idea 3) | Show what a check can and cannot buy |
| Three | The syllabus a law wrote (idea 4) | Show what a deadline teaches while we wait |
| Four | What to do before 2027 | The constructive close, and the only issue that sells anything |

Issue four does not exist yet and would need writing from scratch. It is also the
only one of the four with a direct ask in it, which keeps the series at roughly
one in four on selling rather than every issue.
