Most weeks, the news about children and screens arrives as a number with a fright attached. This week I tried something slower. I read six things published in the last three weeks, five studies and one regulator's statement, and asked each the same question. What does this actually measure?

The answers were more interesting than the headlines, and they kept pointing at the same place. Not at hours. Not at the device. At the conversation, and at who is still in it.

Here is what I found, what each one cannot tell us, and what I think it means for a parent on an ordinary evening and for a school deciding what to teach next term.

## The feed nobody chose

Researchers in the Netherlands asked 147 young people aged 16 to 25 to donate their real TikTok histories. That came to about 6.7 million public videos, mostly from October 2023 to May 2026. Then they looked at the news, politics and social issues inside those feeds.

For the typical person, that kind of content made up a median of 5.34 percent of the feed, about 126 videos a month. The striking part is where it came from. A median of 98.4 percent of it came from accounts the person did not follow. On average, 13.28 percent came from established news organisations and 63.85 percent from niche creators. And 76.74 percent of these young people said social media was their main source of news.

That changes the question a parent usually asks. We ask who our child follows. For news, at least, following barely enters into it. The feed chooses.

The limits matter. This is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed. It is 147 volunteers in one country, two thirds of them women, average age 20.4, and TikTok only. Follows were compared at the moment of donation, not when each video was seen. The authors say it does not establish any effect on beliefs, mood or wellbeing, and it tells us nothing about under 16s or about the UK.

What it does show, cleanly, is that a young person's feed is mostly a set of choices made for them.

## A regulator says the same thing out loud

On 6 November the European Commission published a preliminary view that TikTok's For You feed breaches the Digital Services Act over harmful rabbit holes. I want to be careful with this one. It is a preliminary finding, not a ruling. The company can reply and the Commission may change its view. It is European law, so nothing changes in the UK because of it. And I read the Commission's short news page, not a full decision.

What stands out is the target. It is the design of the feed, not the amount of time spent on it. That does not make time limits useless. It does say that one of the most powerful regulators in the world is looking at the same place the Dutch data points to.

## The chatbot that agreed too easily

A preprint posted on arXiv on 4 November put 18 chatbot models through 32,555 simulated conversations with teenagers. The researchers wrote 106 scenarios from clinical cases, turned them into 983 versions, and had an AI judge score the replies against 22 safety checks.

Without an instruction telling the model it was talking to a teenager, the criterion failure rate was 43.3 percent. With the instruction, 24.9 percent. Emotional dependence failures fell from 54 percent to 15 percent with the instruction. Indirect suicide risk failures stayed at 52 percent.

The finding I keep coming back to is what happened when the simulated teenager pushed back. Without the teen aware instruction, failures around diagnosis, medication and substances rose by about 10 to 12 percentage points for more than 80 percent of the chatbots. A boundary held at the first answer. Then it slipped.

None of these were real teenagers, and none were the apps children download. The models were tested through developer connections, not consumer apps with their own safety layers. Scoring was done by an AI judge, and the authors say some failures may be judge errors. The scoring was strict: a model failed a check if it failed in any version of a conversation, so 43.3 percent is not the share of conversations that went wrong. It cannot tell us that any particular app is unsafe, or that children should be kept away from AI.

It does tell us something useful about the shape of the risk. The danger is less the first answer than the fifth, when a child keeps asking and the tool keeps agreeing.

## What parents actually want from AI

A survey published in JAMA Pediatrics on 2 November asked parents of children aged 5 to 17 how they would feel about AI leading their child's behavioural therapy. Of 572 parents, 47.2 percent were not comfortable at all and 28.8 percent were not too comfortable. About three in four, in other words. Only 7.8 percent were very comfortable.

When parents were asked what would matter most, 58.5 percent chose accuracy of diagnosis and 49.4 percent chose empathy. Close behind came a recommendation from a medical professional, 46.8 percent, and confidentiality, 43.7 percent. Parents could pick more than one.

I read this as parents asking for a person in the loop, not as parents fearing technology. But the limits are real. It was an opt in sample in the US, it was a hypothetical question, and the authors say stated comfort may not match what parents would do. Children were not asked.

## Fewer adults in the room

Here is the one that stayed with me longest. The European Commission's Eurobarometer survey asked 26,297 young people aged 13 to 18, across all 27 EU countries, in spring 2026, how they protect themselves online. A preprint analysing it was posted on 17 November.

Among those who use social media on a school day, 32 percent of 13 and 14 year olds said they talk to a parent, teacher or another adult. At 15 and 16 it was 28 percent. At 17 and 18 it was 18 percent.

Other answers moved the other way. Reporting harmful content or accounts went from 21 percent at 13 and 14 to 30 percent at 17 and 18. Taking breaks went from 28 percent to 35 percent. Older teenagers also reported seeing more: AI content that was hard to recognise rose from 34 percent to 47 percent, and unwanted sexualised content from 14 percent to 23 percent, both in the previous three months.

I want to be precise about what this is. It is one survey at one point in time, so it compares different age groups, not the same young people growing up. The option was a parent, teacher or another adult, so parents cannot be separated out. It does not measure how often, or whether talking helped. It does not cover the UK.

It does not show that teenagers stop confiding in their parents. It does show that older teenagers are less likely to name talking to an adult as one of the ways they protect themselves, at the same age as they report seeing more.

## And we think we know more than we do

The last one is the smallest, and I am including it because it completes the picture. A study published online on 6 November in Public Health Reports compared what parents and teenagers each said about the teenager's online life, across 522 parent and teen pairs. On the evidence of the abstract, parents generally overestimated what they knew.

That is all I can say with confidence. I read only the abstract; the full paper is behind the publisher's wall. Both sides were self report, and it is one point in time. It does not show that monitoring software works, and it does not show that monitoring makes parents better informed.

## What the six have in common

Put them side by side and the headline numbers matter less than the direction they share.

The systems keep talking. The feed chooses what a young person sees, from accounts they never picked. A chatbot can hold a line at the first question and loosen it by the fifth. A regulator is now looking at the design of the feed rather than the clock.

The adults go quieter. Older teenagers are less likely to name an adult as part of how they stay safe. Parents, by their own children's account, know less than they think. And when parents are asked about AI in their child's care, what they want most is a person who gets it right and cares.

None of this proves that screens harm children. None of it supports a ban as the whole answer, and I do not think it was designed to. What it does suggest is that if we only measure hours, we will keep missing the thing that changes: who is still in the conversation when something difficult turns up.

That is a more hopeful finding than it sounds, because the conversation is the part a family can do something about.

## For parents, this week

**Ask about the feed, not just the follows.** "What came up for you today that you did not go looking for?" is a better question than "who do you follow?" The Dutch data suggests that is where most of the news actually comes from.

**Practise the fifth question.** Sit with your child and a chatbot for ten minutes. Ask it something, then push back, the way a teenager might. Watch together what happens to its answer. You are not testing the app. You are showing your child that a tool which agrees with you is not the same as a tool that is right.

**Keep yourself on the list.** The Eurobarometer figures cannot tell us why older teenagers name adults less often. But they are a reason to make sure you are still one of the people your child would think of. That rarely starts at 16. It starts with small, unremarkable conversations years earlier, when nothing is wrong.

**Check your own confidence.** If you are sure you know what your teenager does online, it is worth asking them, calmly, and listening to the gap.

## For schools

The useful lesson here is not "screens are dangerous". It is how a feed decides, and how a chatbot can drift.

A feed literacy lesson can start with the Dutch finding as a question rather than a fact: if almost everything in your news feed comes from accounts you did not choose, who chose it? A chatbot lesson can use the pushback test, done together in class with a teacher present, so pupils see a boundary loosen in real time.

And pastoral teams might look at who older pupils name when asked where they would turn. If the answer drifts away from adults with age, the time to build those routes is in the younger years, before they are needed.

## Where I have landed

I started the week asking whether we are measuring childhood risk or counting headlines. Most of what reaches parents is still the second. A number, a fright, a quick fix.

The better evidence is quieter and more useful. It says the risk sits in systems that keep talking and adults who slowly stop. It does not tell us to take the phone away. It tells us to stay in the conversation, and to teach children how the other voices in it work.

That is the thing I am building towards with Guided Childhood: words for the hard conversations before they are needed, rather than a block after the fact. Whatever you use, the principle holds. The most protective thing in a child's digital life may turn out to be an adult they still talk to.
