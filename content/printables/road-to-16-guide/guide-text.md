## Five stages, four to sixteen

The same five pages as your Passport. Each stage has a focus, a device that usually fits it, and one talk that matters most.

Pebble, 4 to 7, Foundation. First relationships with technology, before habits form. The device: A shared family device, with you in the room. The talk: The First Device Conversation.

Bloop, 8 to 10, Builder. Building digital habits before the algorithm learns them. The device: A shared device, moving toward a supervised one of their own. The talk: The Bedroom Rule.

Orbit, 11 to 12, Explorer. The algorithm conversation, before social media. The device: A first device of their own, ideally a phone for calls and texts before a smartphone. The talk: The Algorithm Conversation.

Nova, 13 to 15, Shaper. Identity and digital footprint, while the relationship still holds. The device: A smartphone, with family guidelines you agreed together. The talk: When Things Go Wrong Online.

Cosmo, 16 and up, Independent. Real digital independence, before they leave home. The device: Their own smartphone. The talk is now about digital literacy. The talk: Building Independence.

## Balance: three kinds of time

A screen time fight is often really about who decides. Splitting the day into three kinds of time settles that before anyone is upset.

Their time: A small amount every day that is simply theirs. You set it, they do not earn it, and it is never taken away as a punishment. That keeps the screen from becoming the biggest prize in the house.

Earned time: Extra time comes from jobs and chores. One star is five minutes to start with, and you can change the rate. Family jobs like laying the table sit outside the deal, because helping at home is part of belonging.

Protected time: Sleep, meals and school hours. No amount of stars buys these. If they want a screen inside protected time, they ask you, and you decide it together.

Protect these first: Start with what screens can crowd out of a day: sleep, moving about, playing, and being bored long enough to think of something. Look after those, then decide the rest.

Where it is heading: You decide, then you decide together, then they decide for themselves. The aim all along is that by sixteen they do not need the system at all.

The one rule that never moves: Devices sleep outside bedrooms. Set up a charging station in the kitchen or the hallway, start it before the first phone arrives, and it is simply where phones live rather than something taken away later.

Your phone too, stage by stage:

Pebble, 4 to 7: When the timer ends their screen time, put your own phone away too. The rule is for the house.

Bloop, 8 to 10: Charge your own phone in the kitchen overnight, next to theirs. One rule for everyone is the easiest rule to keep.

Orbit, 11 to 12: Say it out loud when you stop scrolling: "I have been on that too long, I am putting it down."

Nova, 13 to 15: Give them the right to call you out when you are on your phone at the table. It is the same right you are asking for.

Cosmo, 16 and up: Be honest about your own phone: what it gives you, and what it costs you.

## Behaviour: what to watch for

These are the signs the app names at each stage. One on its own may just be a bad week, and many of them can come from school, friends or sleep as much as from a screen. A sign that keeps coming back, or a few together, is worth a calm talk. None of them is a diagnosis.

Pebble, 4 to 7:
* Tantrums or real distress when a device is taken away
* Choosing screens over play every time they have a choice
* No longer interested in activities they loved before devices

Bloop, 8 to 10:
* Using devices secretly or hiding what they watch
* Starting to compare themselves to what they see online
* Mood changes around device removal that feel out of proportion

Orbit, 11 to 12:
* Mood drops noticeably after phone use
* Worry about being left out of what is happening online
* Checking the phone after lights out
* Withdrawal from family conversations

Nova, 13 to 15:
* Secretive phone use, or panic if you pick up the device
* Big mood swings tied to things that happen online
* Online relationships that feel more real than offline ones
* Dropping activities they used to enjoy

Cosmo, 16 and up:
* Measuring their worth through likes, followers or numbers
* Struggling to be present in real life situations
* Real distress when they are without their phone
* No offline friendships or activities that matter to them

When you notice one:

Write down what you see for a week, before and after screens. Share it as something you noticed, not a conclusion.

Then ask: "I have noticed your mood sometimes changes after being on your phone. Have you noticed that?" Leave space for the silence. Do not fill it.

Not this: "That app is rotting your brain." That shuts the conversation down before it starts.

If you are worried about your child's health or safety, talk to their GP or their school. If anyone is in danger now, call 999.

## The apps, and when they turn up

Each app sets its own minimum age in its terms, and most say 13. Ofcom found 90% of 8 to 12 year olds say they use at least one service with a minimum age of 13, led by YouTube, which under 13s can use through a parent's supervised account. About a third of parents know the usual age is 13. Each card names the Passport page where the Guided Childhood app brings that app up, so you are ready before your child asks.

YouTube. Its own minimum age: 13. Brought up on the Bloop page, 8 to 10. Younger children can use it when a parent turns it on: YouTube Kids, or a supervised account, which you can set up in Google Family Link. Lock first: Set it up through Google Family Link and choose the content setting that fits their age, then set a daily time limit on the Shorts feed. Watch for: The Shorts feed, which scrolls on with no natural end, so set its limit first.

Roblox. Its own minimum age: 5. Brought up on the Bloop page, 8 to 10. Accounts from age 5, with some features only a parent can turn on. Chat needs an age check first. Lock first: Set a Parental PIN so settings cannot be changed, then a monthly spending limit. Watch for: Chat inside games, user made games with adult content, and free Robux scams.

WhatsApp. Its own minimum age: 13. Brought up on the Orbit page, 11 to 12. A standard account has no parental dashboard, so this one is managed with settings and conversation. WhatsApp is rolling out parent managed accounts for under 13s, which may not have reached the UK yet. Lock first: Settings, Privacy, Groups, set to My contacts, so strangers cannot add them to a group. Watch for: Being added to big groups by unknown numbers, and disappearing messages.

Instagram. Its own minimum age: 13. Brought up on the Orbit page, 11 to 12. Teen Accounts are private by default, with the strictest messaging settings, and under 16s need a parent's permission to make them less strict. Lock first: Check the Teen Account defaults are still on: messages only from people they follow, and Sleep mode overnight. Watch for: A false birthday can stop the teen protections switching on, so check it is real.

TikTok. Its own minimum age: 13. Brought up on the Orbit page, 11 to 12. Direct messages are off for under 16s. Lock first: Link Family Pairing from your own phone, then set daily screen time and the content filters. Watch for: How fast the For You feed narrows to one kind of video.

Snapchat. Its own minimum age: 13. Brought up on the Orbit page, 11 to 12. Family Center shows who they are friends with and have messaged, but not what was said. Lock first: Snap Map to Ghost Mode, so their location is hidden from everyone. Watch for: Quick Add suggesting them to friends of friends they have never met, and the passcode folder called My Eyes Only.

Facebook and Messenger. Its own minimum age: 13. Brought up on the Nova page, 13 to 15. Meta Family Center covers Facebook and Messenger, for time spent and who they are in touch with. Lock first: Who can message them set to friends, and location for Messenger turned off in the phone's own settings. Watch for: Message and friend requests from adults they do not know.

Discord. Its own minimum age: 13. Brought up on the Nova page, 13 to 15. Family Center shows servers joined, friends added and who they message or call, but not what they write or say. Lock first: Filter explicit images in all direct messages, and send non friend messages to a separate inbox. Watch for: Messages from strangers in public servers, and voice channels nobody watches.

The law: Ages read from each app's own terms in November 2026. Terms and laws change. The UK government expects to restrict social media for under 16s from spring 2027, and for 16 and 17 year olds to have social media switched off between midnight and 6am by default, a setting they can change. Whatever the law does, the talks still matter, because a deadline is not a plan.

## The school moments

The pressure tends to arrive with the school year, not the birthday. Here are the moments to be ready for, and what to have in place before each one.

The game the whole class plays, Years 3 to 5, ages 7 to 10. Games like Roblox are a place to meet friends as well as a game, with chat and spending inside. Ask them to teach you their favourite and play together for ten minutes before you set any limit on it. Have ready: A Parental PIN and a spending limit.

The phone ask, Year 6, ages 10 to 11. The walk to school alone and the move to secondary bring the ask, and Ofcom says the key age for owning a phone seems to be around that move. Write the phone plan in Year 5, while it is still a plan: what the phone is for, where it charges at night, and the promise that telling you about a problem will never cost them the phone. Ofcom found phone ownership rises from 56% of 10 year olds to 83% of 11 year olds. Have ready: The phone plan, written together. A phone for calls and texts with no apps gives independence without the feed.

The group chats, Year 7, ages 11 to 12. A new school means new class and club groups, and some of them keep going late into the night. Teach group chat skills before the first group: how to leave a chat that turns nasty, and that a screenshot of anything upsetting comes to you and costs them nothing. The one exception is a nude or sexual picture of anyone under 18: they never screenshot it or send it on, they just tell you. WhatsApp's own minimum age in the UK is 13, so a child in Year 7 is almost always younger than that. Ofcom found 52% of 8 to 12 year olds say they use it, and around two thirds of 8 to 17 year olds had been added or invited to a group chat in the past four weeks. Have ready: Groups set to My contacts, and the phone charging outside the bedroom.

The thirteenth birthday, Years 8 and 9, ages 12 to 14. Most of the big apps set 13 as their minimum, so the birthday brings the ask for TikTok, Snapchat and Instagram. Meet it with a readiness check instead of a yes or a no: have you had the algorithm talk, will you set the settings up together, and do they know you are their first call? Have ready: The Orbit page done, and the settings on pages 6 and 7 locked side by side.

Photos and pressure, Years 9 and 10, ages 13 to 15. Being asked for a photo, or a photo being shared around, is a talk to have before it happens. If anyone threatens to share an image of them or demands money: do not pay, do not delete anything, help them stop replying and block the account, and report it to CEOP or to the police on 101, or 999 if they are in danger now. Tell them out loud that they are not to blame and not in trouble. Have ready: The first call promise from the Nova page, said again.

Exams and revision, Year 11, ages 15 to 16. Revision with a buzzing phone is hard for anyone. Notifications off for the revision window, the phone in another room, and the exam weeks named as protected time in your family agreement. Have ready: Protected school hours and a homework window from the first phone.

Since September 2026, statutory guidance asks schools in England to keep phones out of use all school day, breaks and lunch included, with exceptions such as a medical need. Ask for your school's own policy, and make yours at home match it.

## When something goes wrong online

Say this: "If anything ever goes wrong online, and things do go wrong, I am the first call you make, not the last. I will not overreact. I will help."

Say it before anything happens, then say it again every so often.

1. Stay calm. Thank them for telling you. Whatever it is, they came to you.

2. Keep the phone with them. Telling you should never cost them their phone. A child who expects to lose it has one more reason to stay quiet.

3. Keep the evidence. Screenshot the messages and the account name, keeping any nude or sexual image out of the shot. Never copy, save or send on a nude or sexual image of anyone under 18, even to report it, and do not delete it either. The one exception is Report Remove, below, which your child uses for an image of themselves.

4. Block and report. In the app first. If it involves classmates, tell the school, whose Designated Safeguarding Lead is there for exactly this.

5. Check in again. Decide the next step together, and come back to it in a few days.

Where to get help, in the UK:

Someone is in danger now: 999

Worried about sexual contact, grooming or someone asking for images: Report it to CEOP at ceop.police.uk

A nude image of your child is online: Your child can report it to Report Remove, from Childline and the IWF, for under 18s, at childline.org.uk

Your child wants to talk to someone who is not you: Childline, free and confidential, on 0800 1111

You want advice as a parent: The NSPCC helpline on 0808 800 5000

You are worried about their health: Their GP, or NHS 111 (in Northern Ireland, your GP's out of hours service)

## Sixteen: the handover

At sixteen the job changes. You set fewer of the rules and become more the person they talk things through with.

Cosmo's talk: "You are at the age where you get to decide what kind of digital life you build. What does your online presence say about who you are? Not to me. To the world."

A digital audit, as equals: Go through their online presence together as equals rather than parent and child. What does it say? What would they change?

One calm talk before they go: Sextortion, deepfakes and AI made images are real at this age. Have one specific, calm talk about what they would do if anything went wrong, and who they would call.

Three questions to ask each other:
* What does your phone give you?
* What does it cost you?
* What would you change about it?
