The phone arrives with a plan

A first phone usually turns up in a rush. A birthday, a new school, a box opened on the sofa and handed straight over. The rules come later, one argument at a time. This kit turns that round. You do four things, in this order, and the phone lands on a plan your child helped to make.

1
The check
Pages 3 and 4 · A few weeks before

Honest questions for your child and for you. Every answer is ready, nearly or not yet, and each one comes with the thing to practise next.

2
The deal
Pages 5 and 6 · The week before

Your child writes their phone deal in their own words. You add your promises. You both sign, and a date goes on the calendar to look at it again.

3
Setup night
Page 7 · The night it arrives

One evening side by side, switching on the settings that hold the deal: the codes, the bedtime lock, the app approvals, the charger's new home.

4
The first 30 days
Pages 8 to 11 · The month after

One small thing to practise each week, a tick a day, ten minutes at the weekend, and the words for the first wobbles.

Say this out loud before the box is opened

"If you ever show me something bad on it, the phone stays yours. Telling me never costs you the phone."

The whole kit rests on this promise. Children go quiet when telling costs them the phone, so it goes first, before any settings, and it is the first promise in your half of the deal on page six.

Smartphone, or calls and texts?

The kit works for either. For eleven and twelve year olds, Guided Childhood suggests a first phone that does calls and texts with no apps, and a smartphone as the next step. Already chosen a smartphone? Every page is built for that too.

Nine or ten?

Bloop looks after ages eight to ten. Do the check and the deal now, while the phone is still a plan. Every decision is easier before the box is opened.

What you need: a pen each, this kit printed on A4, an evening with nothing else on, and the phone still in its box until page seven.

Step three · The night it arrives

Setup night checklist

Sit side by side and do it together, before the phone goes in a pocket. Phone menus move with every update, so this list says what to switch on, not where to tap. On an iPhone most of it lives in Screen Time. On Android it lives in Google Family Link, which is easiest set up before your child's Google account is made. Stuck? Search the settings for the words in bold.

Two codes. The unlock code: you both know it. A separate code for the controls: only the grown up knows it. If one person knows both, the controls do nothing.

A bedtime lock at the time on the deal. iPhone calls it Downtime; Family Link calls it bedtime. Only the apps you choose work after it starts.

Daily time limits that match the deal. Start where the deal says, and change them at a review, never in the middle of an argument.

App approvals. New apps and purchases ask a grown up first: Ask to Buy on iPhone, app approval in Family Link. If you only do one thing tonight, do this one.

Age ratings and safe search. Set apps, films and music to their age, and switch safe search on.

Contacts. Save the people from page five into the phone together, and choose who can still get through during the bedtime lock.

Location sharing, agreed out loud. Decide together whether it is on, who can see it, and why. If it is on, they know it is on.

The charger's new home. Plug it in tonight where the phone will sleep, outside the bedroom, and put yours there too. If the phone was going to be the alarm clock, a real alarm clock takes the job.

The weekly report. Pick the evening you will look at it together. Look at it with them, not at them.

A calls and texts phone? Do the codes, the contacts, location and the charger. The rest is ready for when a smartphone comes along.

The words for the first wobble

Four moments that often turn up in the first month, with the words from the Guided Childhood script library. Short on purpose, so they come back to you when you need them.

Too long on it
"Five more minutes. Please."
Bedtime has already been stretched twice.
"I know. And we are done now. Device off. I will come in to say goodnight."
WhyThe five more minutes negotiation reflects genuine disappointment, not defiance.
A group chat upset
"Nothing. I am fine."
They came home quiet, and something happened in the group chat.
"Something happened in the group chat, didn't it. You don't have to tell me what. But I am here if you do."
WhyNaming what you can see and leaving the door open gets you more than demanding to see the evidence.
A new app
"Everyone is on it but me."
Their friends are all on an app or a chat they are not.
"I want you to be connected with your friends. Let us look at this together and work out whether it is designed for your age and what the rules would be if you joined."
WhyBlanket refusal at this stage pushes the conversation underground.
Something went wrong
"If I tell you, will you take my phone?"
"I need you to hear this properly. If you ever see something on your phone that scares you, or someone sends you something horrible, or you get into something you cannot get out of, you come and show me, and the phone stays yours. I will not take it off you for telling me. Telling me is the thing that keeps it."
WhyChildren go quiet when telling costs them the phone.

Screenshots. Anything upsetting can come to you as a screenshot, and it costs them nothing. The one exception: a nude or sexual picture of anyone under 18 is never screenshotted, copied or sent on, not even to you. They just come and tell you.
