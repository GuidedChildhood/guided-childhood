## Post 1. The parent portal (the strongest one, lead with it)

**Hook:** Parents can now ask to see every RSHE lesson. Every one.

Since September, the RSHE guidance says schools should let parents view all
the curriculum materials used to teach it, on request.

It goes further on suppliers.
Schools should not agree to any contract that stops them showing parents the
content.
Where a clause like that exists, the guidance calls it void and unenforceable.

And it names the best way to do it: a parent portal.

That is the part I find most interesting, because it turns RSHE from something
that happens behind the classroom door into something families can follow.

When a school uses our lessons, its families get the home version. The same
lessons, at the child's stage, done together at the kitchen table.
Parents see the actual lesson their child had.

If you lead RSHE: how are you sharing materials with parents this year?

## Post 2. AI chatbots are in the guidance now

**Hook:** The RSHE guidance now names AI chatbots, by name.

The revised secondary content says pupils should learn that AI chatbots can
pose risks by creating fake intimacy or offering harmful advice.

Fake intimacy is the phrase that matters.
A chatbot that is always available, always agrees, and never has a bad day is
a very easy friend to have at thirteen.

We teach it as a skill a child keeps.
At primary: when a machine talks like a friend.
At Key Stage 3: when an AI acts like a friend, and is it doing my thinking.

The aim is a child who can ask the right question about a new tool on the day
it arrives, because the tools will keep arriving faster than any scheme can be
rewritten.

What are you seeing in your school: chatbots as homework help, or as company?

## Post 3. Deepfakes: how to identify them

**Hook:** "How to identify them." Four words schools now have to teach.

The revised guidance asks secondary schools to teach about the prevalence of
deepfakes, how they can be used maliciously as well as for entertainment, the
harm they can cause, and how to identify them.

It also says an indecent image of a child is a crime even when the child made
it, and even when it was generated with AI.

Keeping Children Safe in Education 2026 says the same thing from the
safeguarding side: understanding online harms such as sharing images and the
prevalence of deepfakes.

So the question for a scheme is simple. Does a lesson actually teach a pupil to
spot one, or does it just tell them they exist?

Ours is a Key Stage 3 lesson on misinformation and deepfakes, mapped clause by
clause to the guidance.

How confident are your staff teaching this one?

## Post 4. What Ofsted's toolkit actually says

**Hook:** Ofsted's toolkit has one line on RSHE every head should read.

Under the report card framework, personal development and wellbeing is graded
on its own.

The inspection toolkit in use since September looks for a suitable and well
taught RSHE programme at the expected standard.

And it sets the bar for urgent improvement plainly: a curriculum that fails to
teach pupils about healthy relationships in line with the statutory guidance.

Separately, under safeguarding, leaders are expected to make sure pupils are
taught how to stay safe and keep others safe, including online.

The idea is familiar. The wording has never been this direct.

The honest test of any scheme, ours included, is whether you can point at the
requirement and then point at the lesson. We built the mapping so you can.

Which part of personal development feels hardest to evidence right now?

## Post 5. Online safety at primary: wellbeing online

**Hook:** Primary RSHE now covers screen time. Here is the line.

By the end of primary, health education includes wellbeing online: the
benefits of limiting time spent online, the risks of excessive time on devices,
and the minimum age for joining social media sites, currently 13.

That last line lands at home before it lands at school.
The asking for TikTok often starts years before thirteen.

This is where a school and its families can pull in the same direction. The
lesson in class, and the same idea at home that evening, with the words to use
when the asking starts.

Our primary lessons cover screen routines and being kind and safe online, and
the home version gives parents the same lesson at their child's stage.

Primary colleagues: when does the social media question start in your school?

## Post 6. Online safety belongs in curriculum planning and parental engagement

**Hook:** KCSIE 2026 puts parents into the online safety plan.

Keeping Children Safe in Education 2026 says online safety should be
considered while planning the curriculum, and in any parental engagement.

It frames the risks as the four Cs: content, contact, conduct and commerce.
Contact now includes generative AI applications that simulate a person.

It also says parents should be aware of what their children are being asked to
do online, including the sites they will be asked to access.

Put that next to the RSHE guidance on sharing materials, and the direction is
clear. Online safety is no longer a lesson the school teaches alone.

That is the whole reason we built a school version and a family version of the
same scheme.
