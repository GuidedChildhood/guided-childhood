# AI lesson additions from the UC Irvine panel (July 2026)

Folded in from the UC Irvine children and technology panel (Gillian Hayes moderating, with Katie Salen, Michael Levine, Tammy Balik of Roblox, Michael Preston, Mimi Ito). The panel gave us one child facing idea the current 21 lessons do not yet teach: AI will happily do the making and the thinking for a child, and the developmental work is exactly the part it quietly removes. Katie Salen called it the closing down of open play. Michael Preston called it cognitive offload. This file stages that idea by age so the build session can slot it into the app. Same seven beat format, house style, British English, no dashes.

The throughline for the whole AI strand, in the panel's words: it is the activity, not the device. The same tool grows a child or shrinks them depending on what the child is doing with it. Every beat below teaches the child to stay the maker.

---

## NEW LESSON (KS2, Years 3 to 6, ages 7 to 11)

- **Lesson**: Let AI help you, not do it for you
- **Placement**: module 22 (decided 25 Jul 2026), sitting with Lesson 6, How algorithms work, as the second AI lesson in KS2. module_id ks2-22-ai-maker. See handoff for wiring.
- **Host character**: DiGi, the guide, emblem star
- **Learning outcome**: I can let AI help me and still do the thinking myself
- **Runtime**: about 4 minutes, seven beats
- **Voice**: Justin, warm and plain, second person, on the child's side

### Beat 1 · Hook (0:00 to 0:35)
**Narration.** You ask the computer to draw your dragon, and in one second, there it is. You ask it to write your story, and it writes the whole thing. It feels like magic. It is fast, it is shiny, and it is done. But here is a small question that changes everything. If the computer made your dragon, whose dragon is it?
**On screen.** A child types a few words and a finished, glossy dragon picture pops out instantly with a little sparkle. The child looks pleased, then slightly puzzled.
**Illustration prompt.** Warm illustrated scene, a young child at a tablet with a few typed words, a dazzling finished dragon appearing in a burst of sparkles, the child looking delighted then thoughtful, gentle and inviting not scary, 16 by 9.

### Beat 2 · Big idea and the guide (0:35 to 1:10)
**Narration.** DiGi is here to help you work this out. DiGi's word for today is maker. AI can be a brilliant helper. But when AI makes the whole thing for you, you did not make it. And making it was never the boring bit. Making it is the fun, and it is how your brain grows. So the trick is not to stop using AI. The trick is to stay the maker.
**On screen.** DiGi steps in, star glowing, and writes the word MAKER in big friendly letters, pointing from the child to the dragon.
**Illustration prompt.** Friendly guide character DiGi with a glowing star emblem, warm encouraging expression, hand lettering the word MAKER beside a child, an arrow of ownership from the child to their creation, warm colours, 16 by 9.

### Beat 3 · The idea in small steps (1:10 to 2:00)
**Narration.** Staying the maker has three easy moves. One, ask for help, not for the whole thing. Say give me three ideas, not do it all for me. Two, always add your own bit, a part only you would think of. Three, explain it back. If you can tell a friend what you made and why, in your own words, then you made it. Ask for help. Add your bit. Explain it back.
**On screen.** Three stepping stones. Stone one, a hand asking for a small nudge. Stone two, a child adding a bright detail of their own. Stone three, a child happily explaining to a friend.
**Illustration prompt.** Three big stepping stones labelled ASK FOR HELP, ADD YOUR BIT, EXPLAIN IT BACK, a child crossing them, the last stone showing the child cheerfully telling a friend, clear three step teaching visual, warm style, 16 by 9.

### Beat 4 · Wrong then right (2:00 to 2:50)
**Narration.** Here is the wrong way. AI, write my whole story about a dragon. You copy it out, you hand it in, and then your teacher says, ooh, tell me what happens at the end. And your tummy goes cold, because you do not actually know. You never made it. Now the right way. AI, I am stuck in the middle, give me two ideas for what the dragon does next. You pick one. You write the rest yourself. Now, when someone asks, you light up, because it is yours.
**On screen.** Split scene. Left, a child frozen and pink cheeked as a teacher asks about a story the child cannot explain. Right, the same child beaming, telling the story with their hands.
**Illustration prompt.** Split illustration, left side an anxious child unable to answer a gentle teacher about their own story, right side the same child glowing with pride explaining their story animatedly, warm and kind contrast not shaming, 16 by 9.

### Beat 5 · The twist (2:50 to 3:30)
**Narration.** Here is the part the shiny button hopes you never notice. Doing it yourself is not the boring bit you skip. It is the bit that makes your brain stronger, like lifting a weight makes your arm stronger. If a machine lifts every weight for you, your arm never grows. Every time you let AI do all the thinking, you swap a little bit of your own growing for a little bit of fast and easy. Fast and easy feels great. Strong lasts longer.
**On screen.** A child lifting a small friendly weight and their arm gently growing stronger, beside a robot arm lifting it instead while the child's arm stays the same.
**Illustration prompt.** Playful illustration, a child lifting a light cartoon dumbbell with a small muscle growing, next to a robot arm doing the lifting while the child's arm stays soft, clear friendly metaphor for effort building the brain, 16 by 9.

### Beat 6 · Taking the wheel (3:30 to 4:05)
**Narration.** So here is your rule, and it fits in one line. If you can explain it back, you made it. If you cannot, AI made it. Tonight, use AI for something real, a picture, a story, a hard question. But ask it for help, not for the finish. Add your own bit. Then explain what you made to a grown up, out loud. That is you, staying the maker.
**On screen.** DiGi and a child high five beside a bright card reading IF YOU CAN EXPLAIN IT, YOU MADE IT.
**Illustration prompt.** DiGi and a happy child high fiving beside a colourful card reading IF YOU CAN EXPLAIN IT YOU MADE IT, proud teamwork moment, warm room, 16 by 9.

### Beat 7 · Close and the mission (4:05 to 4:30)
**Narration.** One thing to remember. Let AI help you think, never think for you. This week, make one thing with AI as your helper, then explain it back to someone who loves you. Notice how good it feels when it is truly yours. Next time, DiGi looks at how the apps decide what to show you next. Bring your curiosity.
**On screen.** The maker card glows. DiGi taps the star and winks. A small preview of a phone with arrows swirling, hinting at algorithms.
**Illustration prompt.** The maker card glowing warmly, DiGi tapping the star emblem with a friendly wink, a small preview corner showing a phone with swirling recommendation arrows, hopeful inviting close, 16 by 9.

## KS3 UPLIFT (Lesson 12, Misinformation, deepfakes and AI content, host Zara)

Lesson 12 already teaches spotting fakes. The panel adds a second half the current script does not: AI content is not only fakes to catch, it is also the quiet temptation to let AI do your thinking. Fold this in as an added beat between the current twist and close, or as the mission for older KS3 pupils.

**Added teaching beat, Zara.**
**Narration.** Here is the second trick, and it is sneakier than a fake. It is when the AI is right, and helpful, and does the whole thing for you, so you never build the muscle to do it yourself. Spotting a deepfake keeps you safe. Doing your own thinking keeps you sharp. Use AI to check your work, to argue with you, to give you a start. Then finish it yourself. The test is simple. If you cannot explain it in your own words, you did not learn it, the AI did.
**On screen.** Zara holds up two cards. One reads SPOT THE FAKE. The other reads DO YOUR OWN THINKING. She taps the second and it glows brighter.
**Illustration prompt.** Streetwise honest character Zara holding two cards, SPOT THE FAKE and DO YOUR OWN THINKING, tapping the second so it glows, clever knowing expression, 16 by 9.

**Framing note for the whole lesson:** open on the panel's line, it is the activity, not the device. AI is not good or bad. It depends on whether you are using it to think, or letting it think for you.

## KS4 and KS5 UPLIFT (Lesson 20, AI mastery and data rights, host DiGi)

Lesson 20 is the right home for the grown up version of the same idea, framed as mastery rather than warning. Add a beat naming cognitive offload directly.

**Added teaching beat, DiGi.**
**Narration.** Real mastery of AI is not getting it to do the most for you. It is knowing exactly when to let it, and when not to. Researchers have a name for the risk, cognitive offload. It is when you hand so much of the thinking to the machine that the skill quietly leaves you. A pilot still learns to fly without the autopilot, precisely so the autopilot never becomes the only thing that can. Use AI to go faster and further on things you already understand. Do the hard first climb yourself, so the understanding is actually yours.
**On screen.** DiGi shows a cockpit, autopilot on, then a hand taking the controls for the hard climb, then handing back to autopilot at cruising height.
**Illustration prompt.** Clean confident illustration, DiGi beside a simple cockpit dashboard, a pilot taking manual control for a steep climb then re engaging autopilot at altitude, calm mastery mood, 16 by 9.

## PARENT LESSON (parent facing, connection over control)

For the parent lessons surface. The panel's most useful parent material is Mimi Ito's connection over control and the child written contract from Roblox. Not a child video, a short parent guide in the same warm voice.

**Core idea.** Blanket bans and time limits erode the trust you actually need, at the moment you need it most. The move that works is connection, not control. Take the lead from your child.

**The three parts to hand a parent.**
1. Get curious, not suspicious. Ask what they are making and playing, and let them teach you the thing they are good at. A child who has taught you once will come to you when it goes wrong.
2. Write the contract together, and let them lead it. Ask them what a fair amount is, what times protect the things you both care about, like dinner. A rule the child helped write is a rule they keep.
3. Use the dinner story. Mimi Ito stopped shouting turn it off and started asking her gamer son to plan his game around dinner. He began sorting it himself. The fix was never a time limit, it was the conversation.

**The line for the parent.** When your child hits a problem online, do they come to you first, or hide it. Connection is what decides which. This is the same principle DiGi runs on, a calibrated pathway, never allow or deny.
