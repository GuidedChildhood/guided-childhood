LESSON 2.4 · WHY STOPPING FEELS HARD, AND HOW TO WIN AT IT
PARENT AND CHILD VIDEO v1
Stage 2 Builder (ages 8 to 10) · Strand: Screen habits · Journey step 4 of 5

KNOWLEDGE INTENTION: I can name three things a game or an app does to keep an ending out of my reach, I can explain why something I was pulled out of halfway keeps knocking in my head all evening, and I can choose my own ending before I start and land on it when the cool down warning goes.

EMOTIONAL INTENTION: I feel clever and in training, not ashamed. I know the itch I get when I stop is normal, that my grown up gets it too, and that feeling it does not mean I am failing at it. I know I will never be in trouble for telling my grown up that stopping went badly.

KEYWORD: ENDING (taught as "the place you choose to stop")

CATCHPHRASE (x3): "I choose my ending. The machine doesn't."

MISCONCEPTION (for parent notes): children often think that once you are properly good at stopping it will feel easy, so the wrench in the stomach means they are weak at it, and the sensible plan is to wait until the wanting fades and stop then. That plan quietly hands the decision back to the machine, because nothing in a modern game is built to let the wanting fade. Tonight's Called It game exists to break exactly that: the grown up names their own itch out loud, every single time, and puts the thing down anyway while the child watches. Once a child has seen a practised person still feeling it, the feeling stops being the score. What you do about twenty seconds later is the score.

PARENT NOTE (safeguarding): tonight's promise is "you will never be in trouble for telling me that stopping went badly". That covers going miles over, spending something, sneaking extra, or saying just one more about nine times and knowing it. Spending is the one that will test you, because the number can be real money. You are allowed to be upset about the money. Say so plainly, and say it about the money, never at them for telling you. Sort the money out tomorrow, separately, when you are not cross. The promise is about the telling, and it only counts if it survives the expensive night. A child who expects a row for a bad night hides the next one, and hidden is where every bigger problem grows. Thank them for telling you, sort it out together, and say out loud that telling was the right move. One cross reaction voids the promise.

Three rules make the rest of it work. First, you play Called It too, out loud, at the card, with your own evening scroll. A child who is the only one being trained is being managed, not taught. Second, the cool down warning is never a punishment and is never taken away as a consequence for something unrelated. The moment it becomes a weapon it stops being their tool, and every session ends in a negotiation again. Third, and this is the one parents slip on: a missed tick is never used as evidence later. Do not say "but you managed it on Tuesday". The card is theirs, it carries ticks only, and nothing on it marks a miss, because the record exists so they can watch themselves train, and the second it can be used against them they stop being honest about the hard nights, which is the only thing either of you actually needs to know.

If your house has one player, the game still works. The child's column stands on its own, and the narration says so out loud, so a child whose household will not join in this week is never left holding a game they cannot start.

SEGMENT C

[8. TRY IT TONIGHT · ON SCREEN: "CALLED IT". A card with two columns of names and a row of ticks. Ticks only, nothing marks a miss. One column is shown filling up on its own, so a single player card reads as a proper card. The child is holding it, and it is their call whether it lives on the fridge or in their room. A grown up mid scroll, a child with eyebrows raised, pointing at the card.]
DiGi: Tonight you start Called It. It runs all week. Before anybody here starts a screen, they say their ending out loud, and you write it on the card. Not how long. Where. End of this race. Two videos. A grown up writes only their name at the top, and that is them signing up. The cool down warning is your two minute warning to reach the ending you called, and nobody is taking anything off you. Land it, say your itch out loud, and that is a tick. Saying it is part of the skill, and your grown up does that too. Count the ticks on Sunday. Some weeks there will be more than the week before and some weeks there will not, and both of those are training. The one that counts is that you called an ending at all. And if nobody else in your house joins in this week, your column still counts on its own. You can get properly good at this on your own card. If somebody who signed the card starts without calling an ending, you look at them and say, called it? Names on the card only. Anybody who has not signed up is not in the game, and you are not the screen police for this house.

[9. REFLECT AND CLOSE · ON SCREEN: the workshop at dusk. The three machines still whirring away on the bench, ignored, while the runner rests on her own chalk line.]
DiGi: One last thing, and it matters most. If stopping goes badly, if you go way over, if you spend something, if you say just one more about nine times, you come and tell. You will never be in trouble for telling, and a missed tick is never evidence. Grown up, say that out loud now, in your own words. And if you ever tell and it goes badly, that is not you getting it wrong. Find another grown up you trust and tell them instead. A teacher counts. An auntie counts. A grandparent counts. There is always a second door. You were never bad at stopping. You were up against three machines built so that nobody ever finishes. Now you bring your own ending. See you next time.


WHAT WOULD DiGi DO?
ON SCREEN: DiGi called her ending before she started, end of this race. The race is still running when the cool down warning goes, a chest appears saying OPENS IN 3 MINUTES, and the squad chat fills up with one more, one more.
Question: What would DiGi do?

A) Wait until the itch has passed, then stop, so there is no fight about it. [SEEDED MISCONCEPTION]
   Feedback: Sounds fair, and loads of people try it. The snag is that the itch going quiet is the one moment that game is built never to hand you. The moving line, the almost and the queue are all in there making sure of it. Waiting for the wanting to go is waiting for a bus on a road with no buses. Try again.

B) Play to the end of the race she called, leave the chest, and go. [CORRECT]
   Feedback: Yes. You landed the ending you picked, so your brain files it as finished instead of knocking at you all evening, and you did it while the itch was still going. That is the whole skill, and that is a tick on the card.

C) Drop out of the race this second and shut it down before anything else can appear.
   Feedback: That takes guts and I like the speed of it. In this one nothing is going wrong in there, so a stop halfway goes onto the not finished list and knocks at you all evening, which makes the next one harder. Get to the line you called, then go. One thing though, and it beats everything else in this film. If something in there is upsetting you, if somebody is being horrible, or if your grown up needs you, you get out that second and you never have to finish anything first. That is not stopping. That is leaving, and leaving never needs an ending. Try again.
