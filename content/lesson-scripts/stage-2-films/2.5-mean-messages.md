LESSON 2.5 · MEAN MESSAGES
PARENT AND CHILD VIDEO v1
Stage 2 Builder (ages 8 to 10) · Strand: Online relationships · Journey step 5 of 5

KNOWLEDGE INTENTION: I can explain why a nasty message goes to the whole group instead of to the person it is about, I can run the joke test on anything somebody calls banter, I know there is a third thing to do that is neither joining in nor saying nothing, and I know that keeping a screenshot is keeping evidence, not telling tales.

EMOTIONAL INTENTION: I feel like the one with a move to make, not the one being watched. I know that being in a chat when it turned does not make me a bad person, that going to the person costs me nothing in front of everybody else, and that I will never be in trouble for telling, including when I joined in.

KEYWORD: AUDIENCE (taught as "everybody watching, and the reason it happens at all")

CATCHPHRASE (x3): "Leave the audience. Go to the person."

MISCONCEPTION (for parent notes): children often think that saying nothing is the same as staying out of it, so as long as they did not laugh and did not add anything, they were neutral and the whole business had nothing to do with them. It is a reasonable thing to believe, because from inside the chat it is exactly what it feels like. It is only wrong from one seat in the room, and that is the seat nobody in the chat is sitting in. The child it is about is looking at a list of twenty three names and not one of them said anything, and silence at that end reads as agreement. Tonight's Spot the Turn game exists to break exactly that: the child watches an ordinary funny conversation slide into a pile on and has to name the message where it turned. Once they have seen how quietly it happens, "I did not do anything" stops sounding like a defence and starts sounding like a description.

PARENT NOTE (safeguarding): this is the one where the promise carries the most weight in the whole stage, so make it out loud and mean it. "You will never be in trouble for telling me about a chat." It covers four things. Being the one it turned on. Being in the chat while it turned on somebody else and not knowing what to do. Joining in, which at this age is usually one laughing face added at speed and regretted by bedtime. And leaving it days before telling you. The last two are the ones parents flinch at, and the flinch is what buys their silence next time, because a child braced for a row about what they did will never get as far as telling you what was done to them. Thank them for telling, sort it together, and say out loud that telling was the right move. One cross reaction voids it.

Three things make the rest of it work. First, do not ring the other parent, forward the screenshot to your own group chat, or march into school the same evening without telling your child that is what you are doing. Losing control of what happens next is the most common reason a child decides never to tell an adult again, and at eight to ten they will accept almost any plan as long as they were told it first. Second, back the screenshot. A child taught that keeping one is sly will not have the evidence when it matters, and deleted messages are how most of this disappears. Third, if your child was the one who joined in, deal with it tomorrow, not tonight. Tonight you are the person they came to. Tomorrow you are the person who helps them put it right, which usually means one message to one person and is over in a minute.

One more thing the film says out loud, so it does not surprise you. It names a second person a child can go to if the grown up in front of them is not the one they can say it to, including a teacher and Childline. That is the standard, it is not a comment on you, and a child who hears it named is more likely to come to you first, not less.

RETRIEVAL: Lesson 2.4, Why stopping feels hard, catchphrase "I choose my ending. The game doesn't." The REMEMBER WHEN beat calls it back and turns it over: a chat will not hand you an ending either, because a pile on runs until people get bored of it. But this time putting it down is not enough on its own, because the chat carries on without you and so does the person it is about.

DiGi: Quick reset. This one goes hot in the chest. Sit up, feet flat. In, two, three. Out, slow, two, three, four. Nothing here is telling you off.

[3. REMEMBER WHEN · ON SCREEN: last week's Called It card still stuck to the fridge, ticks down one side, while a phone on the worktop lights up again and again beside it.]
DiGi: Last time. I choose my ending. The game doesn't. A chat will not hand you an ending either. But tonight, stopping is not enough. Put the phone down and the chat carries on without you. And so does the person it is about.

[4. TODAY'S ONE THING · ON SCREEN: the word AUDIENCE, huge, on the chalkboard. Below it the model room again, every chair facing the one in the middle.]
DiGi: Today's word is AUDIENCE. The audience is everybody watching. A nasty message is hardly ever aimed at the person it is about. It goes in the group, because the audience is the point.

[5. TEACH, STORY · ON SCREEN: a chat thread filling up. A photo. "Ha." A laughing face. Another. A silly caption. Then a line that goes a bit further, and the laughing faces stack under it.]
DiGi: It does not start nasty. It starts funny. Somebody says something about Ellis, and three people stick a laughing face on it. So somebody adds a better one, because the first got laughs. Nobody thinks they are being horrible. One small thing each.

[ON SCREEN: split screen. On the left, twenty three phones each tipping a single drop into a chute. On the right, Ellis on his bed, and the whole bucket of it arriving at once. Hold on the bucket. Nothing else moves.]
DiGi: But Ellis is not getting one each. He is getting all of it, at once, on his own, in his bedroom. Everybody puts in a drop. One person gets the bucket.

[ON SCREEN: the split screen falls away. DiGi alone, close, talking straight out at the child.]
DiGi: And if the person in that bedroom has ever been you, listen twice. It was not something you did. You did not bring it on by being you. It was easy for twenty three people because it was easy, not because it was true. Your move is three things. Screenshot it. Put the phone down. Tell a grown up tonight. You are not the one with the job here.

[ON SCREEN: a message reading "it's only banter", with two small dials beside it: WHO IS IT ABOUT, and ARE THEY LAUGHING.]
DiGi: When somebody says it's only banter, here is the check. Who is it about, and are they laughing? A real joke has everybody laughing, including the one in the middle. If they are not laughing, it is an audience.

DiGi: Pause me here. Grown up, ask why they think it goes in the group instead of to the person.

◆ platform slide: QUICK CHECK
[Two jobs. First, six chat lines to sort into JOKE or AUDIENCE using who it is about and whether they are laughing, with two deliberately close calls. Then one private tap: have you been in a chat that turned? Yes, no, not sure. No score and no streak, and it is never shown back to the grown up at all. It sits on the child's own page and it opens a door for them, not for anybody else. A number across the family is that child when there is one child, and a promise of privacy a parent can read straight through is not a promise. Use the answer to decide what the child sees next instead: a yes or a not sure quietly brings the third door and the telling line back onto their page.]

[6. TEACH, APPLY · ON SCREEN: two heavy doors side by side, labelled JOIN IN and SAY NOTHING. DiGi looks at both, unimpressed.]
DiGi: So what do you do. Most people think there are two doors. Join in, which is one laughing face and half a second. If that was you once, you are not a bad person. Or say nothing, which feels like staying out of it.

[ON SCREEN: the same chat from Ellis's side. A long list of names down the screen, every one of them quiet, slowly greying together into a crowd.]
DiGi: But Ellis is looking at twenty three names and not one of them said anything. Silence is quiet at your end and very loud at his.

[ON SCREEN: a child half typing a reply into the chat, and the whole audience of chairs swivelling round to face them instead.]
DiGi: Nobody sticks up for him in the chat, because having a go at everybody in front of everybody is how you become the next one.

[ON SCREEN: a third door appears in a wall where there was no door, small and plain, leading out of the room altogether. It opens onto one person, on their own.]
DiGi: So there is a third door, and it costs you nothing in front of the audience. You leave the chat alone. You go to the person. On their own, one message, short is fine. That was out of order. You alright? That is the whole move. About four seconds, and it is the part they will still remember in a year.

[ON SCREEN: a screenshot being taken, then going one way into a grown up's hand where it glows steady, and the other way back into the chat, where it bursts into flame.]
DiGi: Take a screenshot, then show your grown up tonight. Messages get deleted, and a deleted one sounds made up when you describe it. That is not telling tales. That is evidence. But watch where it goes. To your grown up, evidence. Back into a chat, fuel. Same picture, opposite job. And if there is a photo of somebody with their clothes off, do not screenshot it and do not send it on. Close it and tell a grown up. You will not be in trouble.

▐ PAUSE AND SAY #2  [ON SCREEN: "PAUSE. Say out loud the exact words you would send to the person. Grown up goes first." · Older child variant: "Now say it for somebody you do not really like, and say why that one is harder."]
DiGi: Pause me, and say out loud the actual words you would send. Not what you would do. What you would type. Grown up first.

[7. SAY IT TOGETHER · ON SCREEN: the catchphrase in chunky letters, split down the middle into its two halves, the little third door standing open behind it.]
DiGi: Grown up takes the first half, you take the second. Leave the audience. Go to the person. Again, quicker. Leave the audience. Go to the person. Swap. Leave the audience. Go to the person.

◆ platform slide: SAY THIS TONIGHT
[Carries the catchphrase for the house, plus the four second message bank for the child to keep: "That was out of order." "You alright?" "Ignore that lot." "I'm not laughing at it." And the line for the grown up to say out loud tonight: "You will never be in trouble for telling me about a chat. Not even if you joined in." It also carries the Spot the Turn pack the grown up reads from, so nobody has to invent anything: a ten message thread about a made up child nobody knows, funny for the first six and quietly turned at the seventh, and a small card with the three excuses on it.]


SEGMENT C

[8. TRY IT TONIGHT · ON SCREEN: "SPOT THE TURN". A grown up on the sofa reading the ten message thread off the slide, a child leaning in hard with one hand already up.]
DiGi: Tonight you play Spot the Turn. Grown up, read the ten message thread off the slide and do not let on which one is the turn. The name in it is made up on purpose. Nobody from their class, and never your own child, because the second it is a real person it stops being a game and somebody gets hurt for real. They shout TURNED the second it happens. The turn is never the nastiest message. It is the quiet one just before. Then read the three excuses off the card and let them run the test on you.

[9. REFLECT AND CLOSE · ON SCREEN: the model room on the bench, the chairs now facing every which way and the little door open. Beside it, five small stamps in a row, the fifth one still wet.]
DiGi: Last thing. If a chat turns on you, or turns on somebody else and you do not know what to do, or if you joined in and feel rotten now, you tell your grown up. Especially that last one. And if that grown up is not the one you can say it to, you tell another. A teacher, or the grown up at the house you like being at, or Childline on 0800 1111, free and never on the phone bill. There is always a second person. You will never be in trouble for telling. Not for what you said, not for the screenshot, not for leaving it three days. Grown up, that is your promise, and all five films stand on it.

[ON SCREEN: five quick flickers, one per lesson: a jigsaw piece, a message with three tells lit up, a phone asleep on a kitchen worktop, a chalk finishing line, the little third door.]
DiGi: And that is five. One piece is never just one piece. Rushing, promising, asking, stop and check. Phone sleeps first, brain lands after. I choose my ending, the game doesn't. And now, the third door. Go on, be the one who goes to the person. See you in the next stage.


WHAT WOULD DiGi DO?
ON SCREEN: DiGi is in a group chat with twenty other people. Somebody posts a photo of Sam from PE with a caption on it. Laughing faces stack up, and somebody adds a better caption. Sam is in the chat and has gone quiet.
Question: What would DiGi do?

A) Reply in the chat, tell all of them to pack it in, and stick up for Sam in front of everybody.
   Feedback: That takes guts, and it is not wrong. Sticking up for somebody out loud is a good thing and it is never the wrong instinct. Here is the catch. It is the hard version, because the audience is still sitting there and it can swing round towards you. There is one in this list that works every time and costs you nothing in front of them. So do Sam first, on his own, and then say it in the chat as well if you still want to. Go back and pick the first move.

B) Say nothing at all, do not laugh, do not pass it on, and stay well out of it. [SEEDED MISCONCEPTION]
   Feedback: That feels like the safe one, and almost everybody picks it. The trouble is that Sam is looking at a list of twenty names and not one of them said anything, and from his seat, quiet looks a lot like agreeing. Staying out of it is not a thing you can do from inside the chat. Try again.

C) Screenshot it, message Sam on his own, then show your grown up. [CORRECT]
   Feedback: Yes. You stepped out of the audience, you went to the person, you kept the evidence in case somebody deletes it, and you handed it to a grown up the same night instead of carrying it about on your phone. Four seconds of work, and it is the bit Sam will still remember in a year.

OPEN QUESTION
The teacher wants the Spot the Turn thread scripted off the slide and the safeguarding lead wants it improvised with a made up name rule, so I have taken the safer both: it is read off the slide AND the name in it is made up, which removes the improvising parent and the risk of a real classmate in one move.
The teacher says nothing may be added after "One person gets the bucket" and the safeguarding lead wants the child who is Ellis addressed right there, so the bucket line keeps its own shot and ends on a hold, and the address to that child starts a new shot rather than explaining the line.
Segment C runs at about 30 per cent of the narration rather than the usual 20, because the safeguarding close is the one block that cannot be trimmed and it is 123 words on its own, with the made up name rule now restored above it. The alternative was cutting the third door or the joke test to buy C down to proportion, which costs the film its mechanism. Flagging it in case the house rule on segment shape is the harder one.

Two restores did not fit the 920 word ceiling and are left out, named here so nobody assumes they were judged unnecessary. The film now runs 916 words, seven minutes flat at 131 words a minute, so there are four words of room and no more.
The Spot the Turn excuses, spoken in full ("When they call it, read the three excuses off the card, so it is the chat saying them and not you. It was only banter. Everybody was laughing. You cannot take a joke. And they run the test on you. They overrule you, out loud, and you take it."), costs 49 words in place of the 16 word instruction that is there now, so 33 net. It is the beat where the child practises overruling an adult, and the card on the SAY THIS TONIGHT slide still carries the three excuses, so a parent has them in hand even though DiGi no longer says them.
The audience proof ("Nobody ever sends it to that one person on their own, with nobody else watching.") costs 15 words in the keyword beat, and is the evidence PAUSE AND ASK #1 was written to be answered from.
Buying both back needs 48 words. The only places left to find them are the safeguarding close, the joke test or the pause beats, and none of those should pay. So either the cap moves to about 965 words, which is seven and a half minutes, or one of the two goes to the printed parent notes instead of the film. Justin picks.
