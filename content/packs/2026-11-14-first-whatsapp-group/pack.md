## 1. The answer post, LinkedIn

56 percent of 10 year olds have a phone.

At 11 it is 83 percent.

Both figures are Ofcom's, from May, as reported by parents. Ofcom calls starting senior school the tipping point.

And one of the first things to land on that phone is the class WhatsApp group.

Parents ask me the same thing every time. Should I let them join?

So I went looking for the study. How many messages a class group sends. How long the rush lasts. What it does to an eleven year old. I could not find one. Everything any of us has heard is another parent's story.

Then I looked at what has been measured.

Messaging friends is a different thing from scrolling a feed. A Dutch study followed 812 children aged 10 to 17 and found that messaging the friends they already had made those friendships better six months on. It is old research, from before WhatsApp existed. I have not found anything that overturns it.

Most children are not bullied online. The Office for National Statistics puts it at 19.1 percent of 10 to 15 year olds in a year. About four in five were not.

Now the part I will not skip.

WhatsApp's own terms say 13. Nobody in Year 6 is 13.

The app's default lets anyone who has your child's number add them to a group. No request, no asking first. That is in WhatsApp's own help pages.

Children do get removed from groups on purpose. In research for Ofcom, secondary pupils and their teachers named it as bullying.

And there is sleep. A US study of 9,398 children aged about 12 linked leaving the ringer on overnight with slightly more disturbed sleep a year later. The link is small. Phones on silent looked no different from phones switched off.

WhatsApp has moved this year too. In March it announced accounts for under 13s that a parent sets up and manages. In September it announced controls for teenagers, including who can add them to groups. I have not been able to confirm that either has reached the UK, so look on your own phone before you rely on it.

I have stopped answering yes or no. I ask three things.

Who can add you.

Where the phone sleeps.

And will you show me.

The first is a setting. Settings, Privacy, Groups, My Contacts. Change it together, so your child knows it is there and why.

The second is a place. Somewhere outside the bedroom, agreed before the phone arrives and not after the first bad night.

The third is a promise, said out loud before anything goes wrong. "If something in that chat worries you, you come and show me, and the phone stays yours."

Teenagers told the Children's Commissioner that fear of losing the phone stops them asking for help. They were 13 to 18. I would rather make the promise at 10 than find out at 13.

I have not found a trial that tests joining early against holding off. This is a judgement, and that is mine.

What do you wish you had agreed before the first group chat arrived?

## 2. The second angle, LinkedIn

For the week's other post. It takes the night, which the first post only
touches.

9,398 children. One question about where their phone was at bedtime.

It is a US study of children aged about 12, and it is the closest thing we have to evidence on the class group chat at night.

The children who left the ringer on had slightly more disturbed sleep a year later than the children who turned the phone off. The link is small, and it was the parents who reported the sleep.

The detail I keep thinking about is the other group. The phones left on silent looked no different from the phones switched off.

I went looking for more. I wanted the study that counts the messages a class group sends after bedtime. I could not find one. Nobody has measured it.

So I will not tell you the group chat is wrecking your child's sleep. I do not know that, and neither does anyone else.

Here is what I do know. "Just turn your phone off" is easy to say when you are 45. At 11 the cost of switching off is being the one who did not know what happened. That cost is real to them, even when it looks small to us.

So I would not ask a child to leave the group. I would take the child out of the room.

Mute the group until the morning, and do it together the first time. Put the phone to bed somewhere that is not their bedroom. Then tell them the two things no one has told them.

Muting is not leaving.

And nobody gets told you muted it.

If they say they will miss something, agree with them. They will. Ask what the worst thing they could miss would be. Usually it is knowing, and not anything they would have done.

One honest limit. Muting has not been tested in a trial, and I am not promising better sleep. I am offering a calmer bedtime conversation and one skill they will use for the next ten years.

Where does the phone sleep in your house?

### Day 2. What the evidence says, calmly

Subject: Four things the research can tell you
Preview: Two are reassuring. Two are worth planning for.
Link: none

Hello,

Four things, as plainly as I can put them.

One. Messaging friends is different from scrolling a feed. A study of 812 Dutch children aged 10 to 17 found that messaging existing friends made those friendships better six months later. It is older research, from before WhatsApp.

Two. Most children are not bullied online. The Office for National Statistics found that 19.1 percent of 10 to 15 year olds had an online bullying experience in a year. About four in five did not.

Four. Sleep. A US study of 9,398 children aged about 12 linked leaving the ringer on overnight with slightly more disturbed sleep a year later. The link is small, and phones on silent looked no different from phones switched off.

That is all of it. Anything scarier you have read is about teenagers on other apps, or has no source I could find.

### Day 3. The words to say

Subject: The one promise to make before anything goes wrong
Preview: "You come and show me, and the phone stays yours."
Link: none

Hello,

If you only do one thing this week, do this one.

Teenagers told the Children's Commissioner for England that they worry about losing their phones as a punishment, "and that stops them asking for help". They were 13 to 18. I would make the promise years earlier, while it is still easy to say.

Here are the words.

"If something in that chat worries you, you come and show me, and the phone stays yours. I will not take it off you for telling me."

Say it out loud, and say it before there is a problem. Then keep it the first time it is tested, because the first time decides whether there is a second.

Keeping it does not mean doing nothing. You can still act on what you are shown. You deal with the chat, and the child who told you keeps the phone.

Tonight: say the promise. Then ask them to say it back in their own words.

### Day 4. The rule to agree together

Subject: Three lines, written by both of you
Preview: Who can add you. Where the phone sleeps. Will you show me.
Link: the first phone agreement

Hello,

You have the promise. Today is the agreement, and your child helps write it.

One. Who can add you. WhatsApp's help pages say the group setting starts on Everyone, so anyone with the number can add your child to a group. Three. Will you show me. You said it yesterday. Now write it down.

### Day 5. What to do when it goes wrong

Subject: When the group chat goes wrong
Preview: It probably will, once. Here is the first thing to say.
Link: the scripts in the app

Hello,

At some point something will go wrong in a group. That does not mean the plan failed. It is what the plan was for.

If they are added to a group of strangers, say: "You did not do anything wrong here." Leave it together and check the setting is still on My Contacts.

If they are taken out of a group on purpose, do not tell them to shrug it off. Say: "Being taken out of that group was meant to hurt and it did." Then screenshot it with the date. If it keeps happening, that record is what a school can act on.

If something frightening or illegal is shared, that goes beyond a script. Tell the school. If you think a child is being groomed, report to CEOP. If a child is in immediate danger, call 999.

Whatever it is, they showed you. The phone stays theirs.

### Day 6. The child's side of it

Subject: Why "just turn it off" does not work on an eleven year old
Preview: Muting is not leaving.

Today is your child's side.

To us the group is noise. To them it is where the class is. If they switch off, the cost is being the one who did not know what happened. That is a real cost at eleven, even when it looks small from where we stand.

So "just turn your phone off" asks them to pay it. They will not, and the evening ends in a row.

## 7. Parent checklist

NOT READY TO SHIP. Every menu path below must be confirmed by a person on a UK
handset first. Whether parent managed accounts and the teen controls are
available in the UK is not confirmed.

1. Know the term. WhatsApp says 13 to use it on your own.
2. Look for a parent managed account. If your child is under 13, check whether
   your app offers to set one up. It may not be in the UK yet.
3. Set who can add them to groups. Settings, Privacy, Groups, My Contacts. Do
   it with your child and say why.
4. Agree what happens with invite links. The setting does not stop a link
   being sent. The rule is: ask before joining.
5. Agree where the phone sleeps. Outside the bedroom, chosen together.
6. Show them how to mute a group until the morning.
7. Make the promise before anything happens: "You come and show me, and the
   phone stays yours."
8. If they are taken out of a group on purpose, screenshot it with the date.
9. Know where to report. If you think a child is being groomed, report to
   CEOP. If a child is in immediate danger, call 999.

What this checklist does not claim: that any setting makes WhatsApp safe, or
that it will prevent harm or improve sleep. It gives you a clearer picture and
an easier conversation.
