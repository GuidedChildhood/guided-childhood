Center for Democracy and Technology with danah boyd at Cornell. Its argument:
circumvention is a signal rather than a nuisance, enforcement pushes children

## The viewpoint, and why it is new

Every version of this argument, on both sides, measures the same quantity:
**access.** The ban's supporters count children kept out. Its critics count
children who got in. The Australian numbers, the BMJ paper, the French ruling,
Ferguson's 2025 paper on school phone bans, the Birmingham study, the Norwegian
findings: all of it is evidence about access.

Nobody is measuring what a child can do.

So the thought that is ours, and that neither camp is making:

**A ban is a teaching instrument whether we intend it or not, and right now the
lesson it teaches is that identity online is something you assert and nobody
checks.** A child who gets past it by registering an account in an older name has
been taught something precise, by a government, at the exact age we claim to be
protecting.

Two supports that make it land rather than sound clever:

1. **Parents were always the teacher.** Research from 2011 found parents coaching
   their own children past Facebook's age limit, and CDT's own research finds
   parents still doing it. The ban does not introduce a teacher. It changes the
   syllabus.
2. **The enforcement is its own lesson.** France struck its ban down partly on
   privacy grounds. Making a ban hold means building a place for a child's
   identity document to sit, and the industry cannot agree on the size of the
   last spill from one of those services.

## Where we part company with CDT

Adopted: circumvention is a signal; enforcement has real privacy costs; the
displacement risk to less moderated spaces is real.

**Declined: the implication that the ban is therefore the wrong move.** A delay
is not preparation, and we have said that from the start, but a delay is not
useless either. It buys a protected period, and the question Guided Childhood
exists to answer is what a family does with that time. CDT's answer is better
platform regulation and better tools, which is a policy answer to a policy
question. Ours is a parenting answer, and both can be true.

**Also declined: treating children's circumvention as mainly about rights of
access.** It is that. It is also a skill being learned without a teacher, and
that framing is the one with something in it for a parent tonight.

## The post

Nobody can tell you yet whether banning under sixteens from social media works. Australia can already tell you what it teaches.

Its regulator went back three months after the ban began. Before it, nearly 86 percent of the children surveyed were using at least one age restricted platform. After, still above 81 percent. A separate study in the BMJ, 408 adolescents, found insufficient evidence that use had meaningfully fallen.

Then look at how they got round it. An account registered to someone older. An account in a name that is not theirs. A private browser.

That is a child's first real lesson in digital life, and a law delivered it. Not that the internet is dangerous. That identity online is something you assert, and nobody checks.

We have been teaching it longer than the law has, mind. Research going back to 2011 found parents coaching their own children past Facebook's age limit. Parents were always the teacher here. A ban does not change that. It changes what the lesson says.

None of which makes the ban wrong, and I want to be careful here, because I think a protected period is worth having. A deadline is not a plan. It is also not nothing.

What it buys is time. What nobody is answering is what the time is for.

France's constitutional court struck down its own under fifteen ban in August, partly because the enforcement on offer would not protect privacy. That is the bit that gets skipped. Making a ban stick means building somewhere for a child's passport photo to sit, and the industry cannot currently agree whether the last spill from one of those services was seventy thousand documents or two million.

Both sides of this argument are counting the same thing. Whether children got in. Whether children were kept out.

Nobody is counting what a sixteen year old can actually do.

Years ago my son was watching a dance video and would not come off it, and I threatened to take away Saturday football. I knew inside a minute I had got it wrong. The question that followed is the one I have been working on ever since, and it was not how to stop him. It was what I should have said instead.

A ban can hold a door shut. It cannot answer that.

If the door opens on your child's sixteenth birthday, what will they already know how to do?

### First comment

Sources, and where I have been careful. Australia: the eSafety Commissioner's follow up found nearly 86 percent of surveyed children using at least one age restricted platform before the ban and above 81 percent three months after, which is continued use rather than a measure of breaking in. The circumvention routes come from the University of Newcastle study in the BMJ, 408 adolescents aged 12 to under 17, which reports accounts registered to older people, fake accounts and private browsers, and finds insufficient evidence of a substantive reduction so far, while noting the period was one of limited implementation. France: Conseil constitutionnel decision 2026-911 DC, 14 August 2026, struck the under fifteen ban down two weeks before it was due to start, holding that a blanket ban disproportionately infringed freedom of expression and lacked privacy safeguards; the rest of the law stands and the government has said it will try again. The breach I refer to is Discord's: Discord put it at about 70,000 government ID images exposed at a third party used for age appeals, the vendor says none of its systems were involved, and the attackers claimed far more, which is why I have described the disagreement rather than chosen a figure. The 2011 finding on parents helping children past age limits is older research and I have not restated it as a current rate. On school based restriction more broadly, Ferguson's 2025 paper is titled Smartphone bans in schools remain unproven and the English study of 1,227 students across 30 schools found no difference in wellbeing or attainment, while Norwegian work across roughly 400 middle schools did find lower bullying and better grades for girls. The field is split and I am not pretending otherwise. One thing I left out on purpose: a widely shared description of children drawing on fake facial hair to beat age checks, because I could not trace it to a source.
