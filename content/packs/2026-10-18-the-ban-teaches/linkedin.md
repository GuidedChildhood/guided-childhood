## The viewpoint, and why it is new

Every version of this argument, on both sides, measures the same quantity:
**access.** The ban's supporters count children kept out. Its critics count
children who got in. The Australian numbers, the BMJ paper, the French ruling,
Ferguson's 2025 paper on school phone bans, the Birmingham study, the Norwegian
findings: all of it is evidence about access.

Nobody is measuring what a child can do.

So the thought that is ours, and that neither camp is making:

**A ban is a teaching instrument whether we intend it or not, and right now the
lesson it teaches is that identity online is something you assert and nobody
checks.** A child who gets past it by registering an account in an older name has
been taught something precise, by a government, at the exact age we claim to be
protecting.

Two supports that make it land rather than sound clever:

1. **Parents were always the teacher.** Research from 2011 found parents coaching
   their own children past Facebook's age limit, and CDT's own research finds
   parents still doing it. The ban does not introduce a teacher. It changes the
   syllabus.
2. **The enforcement is its own lesson.** France struck its own ban down on
   proportionality first and privacy second, holding that parliament had written
   no safeguards for a scheme which, to work at all, makes every user prove their
   age. (Corrected at v4. This point originally rested on the claim that the
   industry cannot agree on the size of the last ID spill, which is false: it is
   a company's own estimate against an unverified criminal claim the company
   denies. The France ruling carries the point on its own.)

## Where we part company with CDT

Adopted: circumvention is a signal; enforcement has real privacy costs; the
displacement risk to less moderated spaces is real.

**Declined: the implication that the ban is therefore the wrong move.** A delay
is not preparation, and we have said that from the start, but a delay is not
useless either. It buys a protected period, and the question Guided Childhood
exists to answer is what a family does with that time. CDT's answer is better
platform regulation and better tools, which is a policy answer to a policy
question. Ours is a parenting answer, and both can be true.

**Also declined: treating children's circumvention as mainly about rights of
access.** It is that. It is also a skill being learned without a teacher, and
that framing is the one with something in it for a parent tonight.

## The post

Nobody can tell you yet whether banning under sixteens from social media works. Australia can already tell you what it teaches.

Its regulator went back three months after the ban began and surveyed 803 children. Before it, nearly 86 percent were using at least one age restricted platform. After, still above 81 percent. A separate study in the BMJ, 436 adolescents, found insufficient evidence that use had meaningfully fallen.

Then look at how. The BMJ study found fake accounts, someone else's account, private browsing. The regulator found something blunter. Half the children who still had accounts said the platform had never checked their age, and 37 percent had simply put themselves down as sixteen or over.

That is a child's first real lesson in digital life, and a law delivered it. The lesson is that identity online is something you assert, and nobody checks.

We have been teaching it longer than the law has, mind. A 2011 survey of a thousand American parents found a third whose child had joined Facebook before turning thirteen, and two thirds of those parents helped set the account up. I am in no position to be smug about it. During Covid we had two small girls at home and a business to keep alive, and we used YouTube as a babysitter to get through the day. Parents were always the teacher here. A ban only changes the syllabus.

None of which makes the ban wrong. A protected period is worth having. A deadline is not a plan, but it is not nothing either.

What it buys is time. What nobody is answering is what the time is for.

France's constitutional council struck down the under fifteen ban at the heart of its own new law in August. A blanket ban on every under fifteen, whatever the child and whatever the service, was out of proportion. And parliament had written no safeguards for a scheme that, to work at all, makes every user prove their age, adults included.

Britain has its own version coming in 2027, and the argument will be the same one.

Both sides are counting the same thing. Whether children got in. Whether children were kept out.

Nobody is counting what a sixteen year old can actually do.

Years ago my son was watching a dance video and would not come off it, and I threatened to take away Saturday football. I knew inside a minute I had got it wrong, and what stayed with me was not the row. It was that I had nothing to give him instead.

We spent a lot of nine o'clocks after days like that wishing something existed that would help us manage the devices rather than just switch them off.

A ban switches them off. It does not sit down with your child.

So if the door opens on your child's sixteenth birthday, what do you want them to come and tell you first?

### First comment

Sources, and where I have been careful. Australia: the eSafety Commissioner's three month follow up, published July 2026, surveyed 803 children aged 10 to 15 and found 85.9 percent using at least one age restricted platform before the ban and 81.5 percent after, measured as using a platform with or without an account, so it includes logged out viewing that the Act does not restrict. Account ownership did fall, 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent, which eSafety calls modest but statistically significant, and eSafety says plainly that the report is not an assessment of whether platforms complied. The reasons children gave come from that same report: about half said the platform never checked their age, 37 percent had listed their age as 16 or above, 18 percent said they were wrongly estimated as over 16. The circumvention routes are a different source, the University of Newcastle study in the BMJ, which recruited 436 adolescents aged 12 to under 17 and followed up with 408 three months later. Its reported categories are fake accounts at 15 to 19 percent, someone else's account at 9 to 29 percent, private browsing at 6 to 11 percent and VPN use at only 2 to 3 percent, which is worth saying because VPNs are what everyone assumes. The paper found insufficient evidence of a substantive early reduction, and its own authors say the full impacts may not be clear for a decade. It is one Australian state, self reported, and the confidence intervals are wide enough that modest effects cannot be ruled out, so I am not using it to say the ban failed. France: Conseil constitutionnel decision 2026-911 DC, 14 August 2026, which struck down Article 1, the under fifteen ban, two weeks before it was due to start. The rest of the law stands. The main ground was proportionality, a blanket ban regardless of the child or the service. The second, separate ground was that parliament had provided no legal guarantees for a scheme that by its own operation requires every user, including adults, to prove their age. The breach I refer to is Discord's, October 2025: Discord said approximately 70,000 users may have had government ID photos exposed at a third party used to review age appeals, the attackers claimed over two million and Discord called that an extortion bluff, and the vendor says none of its systems were involved. No outside party has verified any of the three figures, and Discord has since moved to different age assurance providers, so this is not a description of where it is today. The 2011 finding is boyd, Hargittai, Schultz and Palfrey in First Monday, a survey of 1,007 American parents: 36 percent said their child joined Facebook before 13 and 68 percent of those parents helped set the account up. Their argument was not that those parents were careless. It was that an age rule parents do not accept gets opened by the parents, which is the point I am making. US sample, parent reported, and about a platform's own terms rather than a law. On school based restriction more broadly, Ferguson's 2025 paper is titled Smartphone bans in schools remain unproven and the English study of 1,227 students across 30 schools found no difference in wellbeing or attainment, while Norwegian work across roughly 400 middle schools did find lower bullying and better grades for girls. The field is split and I am not pretending otherwise. One thing I left out on purpose: a widely shared description of children drawing on fake facial hair to beat age checks, because I could not trace it to a source.
