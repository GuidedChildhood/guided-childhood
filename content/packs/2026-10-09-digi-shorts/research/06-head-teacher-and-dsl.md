# The head teacher's view: what lands in the classroom, what parents bring to the gate, and what a head will share

I wrote this as a primary head and designated safeguarding lead (DSL) who has also run Year 6 to 7 transition. It reflects the position on 9 October 2026. Every line in quotation marks was read on the page linked next to it. Where I could not open a page, I say so. The web search allowance ran out partway through. The later checks were done by opening pages directly, and what is still unchecked is listed in section 8.

---

## The one line

From this term, the law makes the school day phone free. What heads now write home about happens between the 3.15pm bell and 8.45am the next morning. That is the parent's time, and it is where Guided Childhood works. A head will share something that helps parents with those hours. A head will not share anything that scares, blames or sells.

The film can also state something heads will agree with: school now keeps phones away all day, so the evening is where a plan has to work. That fact is checkable and statutory, and it puts us beside the school rather than against it.

## 2. The problems that reach the classroom, most frequent first

### 2.1 The group chat, Years 5 and 6, usually WhatsApp

**What arrives on Monday.** Children come in upset about something said in a chat on Saturday night. Parents arrive with screenshots. Friendships fall apart at playtime. The DSL spends the morning on something that did not happen on school premises.

**The evidence**
* Tes Safeguarding Report 2025: 1,949 staff and governors responded, 41 percent of them from primary schools. Online bullying was named the most common online risk by 66 percent overall and **74 percent of primary respondents**. "Monitoring online activity outside of school" was the top challenge, at 92 percent. One respondent wrote that "most incidents occur outside school." [Tes report PDF](https://hs.grad2teach.ac.uk/hubfs/Tes%20Safeguarding-Report-UK%202025.pdf?hsLang=en), [Tes release](https://www.tes.com/corporate/news-and-blogs/92-teachers-say-monitoring-online-activity-outside-school-their-biggest)
* Ofcom, *Children and Parents: Media Use and Attitudes*, published 7 May 2025: 23 percent of 3 to 12s have a WhatsApp profile, and 40 percent of under 13s have a social media profile. [Ofcom PDF](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/media-literacy-research/children/childrens-media-use-and-attitudes-report-2025/childrens-media-literacy-report-2025.pdf)
* BBC Teach, Censuswide survey of 1,001 primary teachers, December 2024: four in five knew of at least one online safety incident in the past year. [STEM Community, BBC Teach](https://community.stem.org.uk/blogs/tim-bradbury1/2025/02/11/online-safety-issues-reach-primary-school-children)
* The under 16 ban leaves WhatsApp out ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/social-media-to-be-banned-for-under-16s-in-landmark-government-move-to-givekids-their-childhood-back)). **The ban will not solve the primary school group chat.** That makes it the strongest issue for a film that stays neutral on the ban.

**What heads have written to parents** (verbatim, from the letters themselves)
* Archbishop Wake CE Primary, Dorset, 23 January 2025. Mr Lasbury-Carter, head: "I understand the complexities of navigating and supporting children with apps and social media". "my first and best suggestion would be to speak to your child about their involvement in Whatsapp groups". [letter](https://www.archbishopwake.dsat.org.uk/_site/data/files/migrated/year-specific-letters/yr-5-whatsapp-messaging-23.01.25.pdf)
* Brough Primary, East Riding, 27 February 2024. Deputy head: "Whilst this group is organised entirely outside school, and therefore entirely outside our ability to control". He suggests the child "take a screenshot and show a parent." [letter](https://broughprimaryschool.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Y5_6-WhatsApp-Group-2024.pdf)
* Wouldham, Kent, 22 April 2024. Mrs Baldwin, head: "We treat social media conversations with the same level of seriousness as face-to-face interactions". "Large online groups can often lead to conflicts and instances of miscommunication". [letter](https://www.wouldham.kent.sch.uk/content/pages/documents/year-6-whatsapp-group.pdf)
* Jennett's Park CE, Bracknell, 23 September 2024. Year 5 team: "it appears that some of that trust has been broken". "we will always follow things up, even if they occur outside of school." [letter](https://jennetts.bonitas.org.uk/children/learning-in-school/year-groups/y5-whatsapp-letter-23-09-2024)
* Kelvingrove, Lewisham, 30 June 2026. Kris Clark, deputy head: "Some of this activity occurs late at night, when I'm sure you assume children are asleep." "please could you look through their chats with them and offer support and advice". [letter](https://kelvingrove.lewisham.sch.uk/2026/06/30/online-safety-year-5-and-6-group-chats-2/)
* Uphill Village Academy, 22 February 2023. Points parents to a National Online Safety WhatsApp guide. [letter](https://uphillvillageacademy.clf.uk/letters/year-5-6-whatsapp/)

**Something heads keep getting wrong.** These letters disagree about WhatsApp's minimum age. Holy Cross and Brough say 16. Jennett's Park says "Age 16 recommended app or 12+". Archbishop Wake quotes an FAQ giving 16 for Europe. Coverage from 2024 reports that WhatsApp lowered the UK minimum to 13 in early 2024 ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240415-whatsapp-eu-uk-minimum-age)), but I could not open WhatsApp's own FAQ to confirm it. **A film that gets this right, and shows its source, earns a head's trust. One that gets it wrong will be taken down.**

**Where the product proves it** (to be confirmed on the live product):
* `lib/content/device-issues.ts` has two entries for this: `class_group_chat` ("The Year 6 WhatsApp group") and `group_chat_nasty` ("The group chat turned nasty").
* The scripts include "The class group at eleven at night" and "When Group Chat Drama Comes Home".
* The `group_chat_nasty` entry's own response says "the school route used early".

### 2.2 WhatsApp at 11pm, and the tired child

**What arrives.** A child who is flat, tearful or falling asleep by the afternoon. Usually it comes up alongside the group chat story, not on its own.

**The evidence**
* Whiteman's line above is the public statement from the heads' union.
* Holy Cross Catholic Primary, Essex, 9 May 2024. Mrs Power, head: "We strongly advise a device free bedroom policy after bedtime to allow for uninterrupted sleep and rest for all children." [letter](https://www.holycrossprimary.com/_site/data/files/migrated/letters-summer-2024/whatsapp-year-5-letter-may-2024.pdf)
* Primary RSHE now teaches the children "not using screens in the bedroom". At secondary it becomes "removing phones from the bedroom" ([RSHE PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6970e7e67e827090d02d42e0/Relationships_education_relationships_and_sex_education__RSE__and_health_education__for_intro_1_September_2026_.pdf)). **A film that backs this is backing the statutory curriculum.**
* I found **no recent, sound UK survey of teachers** that measures tiredness caused by devices. The figures that come up are from a Sleep Council poll in 2012 and a 2016 pupil survey. Use Whiteman's quote and the RSHE line instead, and do not put a number on screen.
* For the youngest children: Kindred Squared, January 2026 (1,000 primary staff and 1,000 parents). Staff said 37 percent of children starting Reception were not ready for school, while 88 percent of parents said their own child was ready ([Schools Week](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/more-reception-children-not-school-ready-survey-finds/)). Coverage says staff blamed screen time more than parents did, but I could not confirm the figures for that, so leave them out.

**Where the product proves it.**
* "Phone in the bedroom, awake at 2am" in `device-issues.ts`, with scripts including "Phone to bed, unasked, and a night light for it".
* Bedtime is protected by default for each age band (migration 223; for ages 8 to 10 it runs 20:00 to 07:00).
* Nothing on a child's phone buzzes between 19:00 and 07:00 (`lib/push/quiet-hours.ts`). A DSL will like that.

### 2.3 The Year 6 phone

**What arrives.** "Everyone in the class has one." Parents ask the head at the gate what the school thinks. Many Year 6s start walking home alone. Secondary schools have started setting their own rules for Year 7.

**The evidence**
* Ofcom, 21 May 2026: smartphone ownership "rises sharply from 56% of 10-year-olds to 83% of 11-year-olds." [Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/younger-phone-owners-the-rise-of-ai-and-consumption-over-creation-our-latest-look-at-uk-childrens-media-lives)
* Parentkind, March 2024 (2,496 parents in England): 77 percent of primary parents backed a smartphone ban for under 16s, against 16 percent of secondary parents. 69 percent said limiting access would make life easier for them as parents. [Parentkind](https://www.parentkind.org.uk/research-and-policy/parent-research/research-library/school-education-policies/parent-poll-on-smartphones-march-2024)
  * That drop from 77 to 16 percent, in the year the child changes school, is the cliff edge seen in parents' own answers.
* In May 2024, St Albans primary heads wrote jointly asking parents to wait until 14 before giving a smartphone. Smartphone Free Childhood says 33 of the 36 heads took part ([SFC](https://www.smartphonefreechildhood.org/resources/success-stories/cunningham-hill-infant-and-junior-schools-st-albans)). The Telegraph reported 20 of 24 ([PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/uk/the-daily-telegraph/20240523/281779929241959)). The counts conflict, so do not quote either.
* DfE case study 2 (secondary): "children in year 7 are now only permitted to have what we call a 'simple' (or brick) phone". Case study 5 (primary): Year 6 pupils may walk home alone and hand their phones in at the gate. [gov.uk case studies](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/mobile-phones-in-schools/creating-a-mobile-phone-free-environment-school-case-studies)

**Where the product proves it.** "Everyone in the class has a phone" in `device-issues.ts`, with the script "The Year 6 phone plan, before the box is opened". Its advice is to write the plan in Year 5.

### 2.4 Gaming

**What arrives.** Children in Key Stage 2 talking about 18 rated games, rows over in-game spending, falling out during online matches, and messages from strangers through in-game chat.

**The evidence**
* Ofcom 2025: 75 percent of 10 to 12s play games online. 31 percent of 8 to 17s who game online chat to people they do not know.
* Ofcom 2025, parents' biggest gaming worries: strangers (62 percent), bullying (54 percent), the game's content (52 percent), and pressure to buy in the game (52 percent). [Ofcom PDF](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/media-literacy-research/children/childrens-media-use-and-attitudes-report-2025/childrens-media-literacy-report-2025.pdf)
* Primary RSHE now names "video game monetisation" and that "gaming can become addictive".

**The warning from heads' own history.** In 2015, the Nantwich Education Partnership told parents that allowing 18 rated games meant "we are advised to contact the police and children's social care as this is deemed neglectful." Margaret Morrissey of Parents Outloud replied: "It will be construed by many parents as a threat and it is not helpful." ([The Educator](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/principals-threaten-to-call-cops-on-parents-of-gaming-students/198718)). This is the clearest example of a school blaming parents, and it failed.

### 2.5 AI homework, and AI as a friend

* Teacher Tapp, 8 April 2026. The share of primary teachers reporting a pupil using AI without permission went from 1 to 3 to 5 to 8 percent between 2023 and 2026. In Key Stage 2 it went from 1 to 10 percent. [Teacher Tapp](https://teachertapp.com/articles/neurodivergent-teachers-cheating-with-ai-devices-in-schools/)
  * In primary this is growing but small. **Parents do not yet raise it at the gate.**
* Ofcom, May 2026: "AI is used by over half of 8-17s (56%)". [Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/younger-phone-owners-the-rise-of-ai-and-consumption-over-creation-our-latest-look-at-uk-childrens-media-lives)
* For the DSL, the concern is companionship more than cheating:
  * KCSIE 2026 now names harmful contact through generative AI.
  * Secondary RSHE warns about chatbots "creating fake intimacy or offering harmful advice".
* Internet Matters' *Me, Myself and AI* (2025) is widely quoted as finding 64 percent of 9 to 17s had used a chatbot. The page returned 403 and I could not check it. `device-issues.ts` quotes that figure, so check it before it goes on screen.

### 2.6 Also on the DSL's desk

* Scams: a third of primary teachers said a child in their class had reported being scammed online (BBC Teach survey above).
* Harmful content: 15 percent of primary respondents in the Tes 2025 report say they often see its effects.

Both are later episodes, not the opening one.

## 3. What parents bring to the school gate

In order of how often a primary head hears them:

1. **"Can you sort out the group chat?"** Parents bring screenshots, and heads say thank you for them. Archbishop Wake: "We appreciate parents/carers providing screenshots". Wouldham: "I want to express my gratitude to those parents who reached out". The parent hopes the school can fix something that happened at home. The head can't.
2. **"Everyone has a phone. What does the school think?"** Parents want a shared norm so they are not the only family holding out. St Albans and Smartphone Free Childhood class ambassadors exist because of this. Parentkind found 69 percent say limiting access would make life easier.
3. **"Is the walk home a reason for a phone?"** This is the Year 6 independence question, which DfE case study 5 deals with.
4. **The game they let them play**, and the money spent on it.
5. **New this term: "So the ban means I don't need to worry?"** The answer: nothing changes until spring 2027, and WhatsApp is excluded.
6. **Sleep**, which the school raises far more often than parents do. The Kindred Squared gap, 88 percent of parents against 37 percent of staff, shows how differently home and school see the same child.

**What makes these conversations hard.** In the Tes 2025 report, "resistance from parents and students" was a top challenge for **78 percent of primary respondents against 54 percent of secondary respondents**. The report notes "greater difficulty engaging parents of younger children".

Parents and heads arrive defensive. Anything that helps a parent feel capable, not judged, makes the next gate conversation easier for the head. **That is the value a head sees in a parent video.**

## 4. What would make a head share a parent video in the newsletter

Each test comes from guidance a head already works under.

1. **It backs the school's policy and does not compete with it.** The DfE asks parents to "reinforce and discuss the policy at home". Ofsted now looks for "partnerships between home and school" on phones. A video that says "school keeps phones away all day, here is the evening plan" is a resource the head can show an inspector.
2. **It prepares parents rather than scaring them.** DfE hoaxes advice, 2021: "Helpful messages to share with parents and carers include encouraging them to focus on positive" ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/harmful-online-challenges-and-online-hoaxes/harmful-online-challenges-and-online-hoaxes)). LGfL's test asks "is it going to scare or prepare; is it designed for panic or protection?" ([LGfL](https://safeblog.lgfl.net/2018/11/parents-scare-or-prepare/))
3. **It talks about features and behaviour, not "dangerous app" lists.** UKCIS 2022 says "'Naming and shaming' specific apps or online challenges can be counterproductive" ([UKCIS PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/632b1800d3bf7f75c2a5fa08/OS_UKCIS_Draft_External_Visitors_to_Support_Online_Safety_Final.pdf)).
4. **It does not judge.** At the launch of the under 5s guidance, Dame Rachel de Souza said: "Parents tell me they want clear, non-judgemental information." Parentkind's Frank Young said: "we need to help parents without pointing the finger." ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/new-screen-time-guidance-for-parents-of-under-5s))
5. **It gives one thing to do tonight.** The best letters do exactly this. Brough gives three steps. Kelvingrove says to look through the chats with them. Our "one thing to do, and the words to say" is the format heads already use.
6. **It gives parents the words.** RSHE para 55 asks schools to support "parents in managing conversations with their children about RSHE topics". Para 56 asks for resources that let parents "continue conversations started in class". **This is our clearest match with what a head is required to do.**
7. **It is accurate and every number carries its source,** including the WhatsApp age. Heads have been caught out by bad facts before.
8. **It is short, captioned and works without sound.** UKCIS asks for "subtitles for pre-recorded content" and translations for parents with English as an additional language.
9. **Watching it costs nothing and needs no sign up.** The stage check, "three questions, no sign up", passes this test. A price, a trial or a login wall does not.
10. **It sits comfortably beside the trusted list.** KCSIE 2026 points parents to Childnet, Internet Matters, Parentzone, LGfL, CEOP and Common Sense Media. Tes found sharing "accessible resources (eg, NSPCC guides)" builds trust. A commercial product has to look and sound at least as careful as those.
11. **It points parents to the school when something is a safeguarding matter.** No school wants a resource that keeps a disclosure inside an app.
    * DiGi already does this. `lib/digi/safety.ts`, `SAFEGUARDING_OPENER`, opens with: "report it to CEOP at ceop.police.uk, and tell the school's Designated Safeguarding Lead".
    * **Showing that line on screen is the strongest single reason a DSL would share the film.**
12. **It arrives at the moments the school is already writing home.** Those are Safer Internet Day (February), Year 6 transition evenings, before Christmas when new devices arrive, before the summer holidays, and the week after a group chat letter. LGfL's advice is to "Drip feed information throughout the school year."

## 5. What would make a head refuse it

| What triggers a refusal | Why a head refuses | What it means for our cut |
|---|---|---|
| **Fear** | UKCIS: scare tactics "are likely to be ineffective and in some cases, harmful". They can make children feel blamed and stop them asking for help | No worst case stories, no frightening numbers, no "your child is at risk". Show the moment and the fix |
| **Blame** of the parent or the child | The Nantwich letter in 2015 was read as "a threat". UKCIS says to avoid victim blaming language | The "guilty because you handed over the phone" pain must play as relief ("most of us did; here is the plan"), never as an accusation. The child is never the villain |
| **Selling** | UKCIS: providers may use sessions "to "sell" a particular product… be cautious about endorsing specific products". A newsletter link carries the head's name, and THE-STORY.md itself says one primary is 250 families "with the school's endorsement attached" | The school cut has no price, no founder rate, no trial countdown and no "only 50 places". It ends on the free stage check |
| **Outcome claims** | No head signs under a promise about how a child will turn out | Never "fewer arguments guaranteed" or "your child will be ready". Show the parent watching their own number move, as the film already does |
| **Seeming to contradict school rules** | The school now confiscates phones under statutory guidance. Our home "no confiscation promise" could be heard as undermining that | Present it only as a promise about telling: "if you show me something upsetting, you will not lose your phone for telling me". That fits the DfE line that pupils should be able to reach their phone when "raising concerns" about online issues |
| **A synthetic person presented as real** | KCSIE 2026 now covers AI-generated images. A DSL will spot one | No actor or AI-generated parent, teacher or head. Scenario lines labelled "Illustrative" |
| **Researchers who appear to endorse us** | Scene 6 shows Odgers, Orben, Przybylski and Livingstone. A head, or the academics themselves, will ask whether they endorse the product | Label it "Research we draw on" and give the paper beside each name. Never place a face next to the logo as though it were a quote |
| **AI and data questions** | KCSIE 2026 sends schools to the DfE's generative AI product safety expectations, so a DSL will ask where a parent's words go | Have a plain answer ready, one tap from the end card |
| **Taking a side on the ban** | Heads must stay politically impartial with parents | Stay neutral on the ban. "A deadline is not a plan" works because it supports neither side |
| **Inaccuracy** | One wrong fact, such as the WhatsApp age, and the head looks careless in front of 250 families | Put a source on every number. Use RSHE and DfE wording where possible |

## 6. How a school angle can sit in the series without taking over from the parent

**The rule: the school appears in three roles and never as the main character.** It is where the problem shows up, it is the set of rules the parent is backing at home, and it is the route DiGi sends the parent to when something is a safeguarding matter. The parent and child always stay at the centre.

**Six concrete ways to do it**

1. **One five-second beat in the hero short.** On screen text, sourced to the DfE guidance:
   * *"School keeps phones away all day. The evening is where the plan has to live."*
   * Source line: "DfE, Mobile phones in schools, statutory from September 2026"

   This is fact rather than endorsement, and it turns the after-school hours into our ground.
2. **Show the route to the school's safeguarding lead inside DiGi.** In the group chat episode, let the real `SAFEGUARDING_OPENER` line, which names the school's Designated Safeguarding Lead, appear for one beat on the phone. It must come from the real component and be labelled illustrative. The school appears exactly as a DSL would want: as the right person to tell.
3. **The Monday morning cold open, shown as text only.**
   * *Illustrative. "Saturday, 10.52pm. 47 unread in Year 6 chat." Cut to: "Monday, 8.50am." Cut to the parent, that evening, asking DiGi what to say.*
   * No teacher on screen and no school uniform.
4. **A newsletter cut of every short**, 30 to 45 seconds:
   * one problem only, captioned, works without sound;
   * the words to say tonight;
   * ends on *"What stage is your child? Three questions, no sign up."*;
   * no price anywhere.

   A head can paste this under the safeguarding section of Friday's newsletter.
5. **The Year 6 episode timed to transition.** Publish it for the summer term transition evenings and the Year 5 autumn term, using the product's own "Year 6 phone plan, before the box is opened" and Ofcom's 56 to 83 percent jump. Secondary schools can share the same cut with incoming Year 7 families.
6. **The RSHE bridge, only where it is true.** "Continue the conversation started in class" is the guidance's own wording. The schools parent pack (`schools/app/hub/parents/page.tsx`) already sends "The question coming home" for each lesson. Only say a school is teaching something when it uses our programme. THE-STORY.md notes the code refuses to guess what one school teaches, and the film must do the same.

**A real head's voice, only if it is real.** If a pilot school's head agrees in writing to give one named line to camera, use it. Otherwise no head appears.

**Avoid**
* A school logo, a uniform you can identify, or children in uniform.
* "Recommended by schools" unless a named school has said so in writing.
* Making the school the villain ("your school can't help you") or the hero ("your school says buy this").

**Two things to fix in the product before a school-facing cut**
* **Protected school hours are off by default.** Migration 223 says "protect_mealtimes and protect_school_hours default off", and `SCHOOL_WINDOW` is 08:45 to 15:15. THE-STORY.md lists school among the protected times.
  * Now that phone free schools are statutory, a head will expect a school-hours window to be on by default for school-age children.
  * Until it is, the copy must say "you can protect school hours", not "it protects school hours".
* **Check `class_group_chat` in `device-issues.ts`.** It says "Over half of 8 to 11s already use WhatsApp". The Ofcom 2025 figure I could confirm is 23 percent of 3 to 12s with a WhatsApp *profile*. These may measure different things, so confirm the source before it reaches a screen.

## 7. My top three from this view

1. **The group chat at night (Years 5 and 6).** Heads write home about it more than anything else. The ban does not touch it. The fix is words and habits, which is what we have.
2. **The phone in the bedroom.** It is the tiredness heads see, the RSHE sleep line, and the union's "late into the evening" concern in one, and it backs what children are now taught in class.
3. **The Year 6 phone plan.** This is the cliff edge, shown by Ofcom's 56 to 83 percent jump and Parentkind's 77 to 16 percent split, and it fits the transition calendar.

Gaming, including money and strangers, and AI as a friend come next. AI homework is still too small in primary to lead with.
