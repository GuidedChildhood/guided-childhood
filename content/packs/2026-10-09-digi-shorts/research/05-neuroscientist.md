# The brain science behind the DiGi shorts: what is solid, what is myth, and lines a neuroscientist would let through

Lens: developmental cognitive neuroscience (adolescent brain, sleep, reward, attention). Written for the next cut of the DiGi launch film and the new screen timer short. 9 October 2026.

## The verdict in six lines

1. **The strongest science for our parent is not a brain scan. It is about stopping and about sleep.** Both are behavioural and well measured, and they are also what the product does: the timer ends the session, and bedtime is protected.
2. **Most of the dramatic brain findings are about teenagers.** Our core parent has a primary age child, and for that age the honest evidence is about transitions, self control that is still growing, and light and sleep. Lead with those. Keep the teen social reward science for a Stage 4 piece.
3. **Never say dopamine hit, addicted, digital heroin, rewiring, or "not finished until 25."** Each one fails a hostile expert, and each pulls the film into the fear frame we are trying to leave.
4. **The best evidence for the timer comes from one research group (Hiniker, University of Washington) and covers children under six.** Children were less upset when the device ended screen time than when a parent did. Autoplay made stopping harder. A warning on its own did not help.
5. **A hostile expert's first question will be about the star quest.** Stars become screen minutes, which is screen time used as a reward. Two cross sectional studies link that practice to *more* screen time. The film must not claim the stars reduce screen time. It can show what is true: the plan is agreed together, bedtime cannot be bought, and the device ends the session.
6. **The one true visual metaphor is the last page.** A story has an ending, autoplay tears it out, and the timer puts it back. Details are in section 6.

## 1. How I graded, and what I could open

* **SOLID:** replicated, or a strong design, and the claim stays inside what was measured.
* **SOLID WITH LIMITS:** real, but small samples, one lab, or one age band. It needs its caveat tag on screen.
* **OVERREACH:** a true finding stretched past its data. Usually an association sold as a cause, or adults sold as children.
* **MYTH:** no data behind it, or the data say the opposite.

**Source access.** I read in full: Hiniker 2016 and Hiniker 2018 (both PDFs), Sherman 2016, the RCPCH 2019 parent fact sheet, the Odgers 2024 Nature review, Tang 2018, Telzer 2013, Diamond 2013, Schultz 2016, Somerville 2013 (Current Directions), Mousley 2025, the Scottish Government 2020 review table (for Bartel), the HBSC data browser, Burnett 2024 and the Piotrowski chapter. The rest I took from abstracts or search summaries, and those are marked *(abstract)* or *(secondary)* in the sources list. The shared web search budget for this run ran out partway through, so I fetched known primary URLs directly after that. Five sources I never opened and do **not** rely on: Fuligni 2019, Sebastian 2010, Blakemore and Mills 2014, Pfeifer and Allen 2012, and the original AAP family media plan text. Any of them can be checked in a follow up run.

**Quotes.** I paraphrase sources rather than quote them. There is one verbatim parent line, in section 2A.

## 2. The five explanations that are robust and filmable

### 2A. Why stopping is harder than starting. SOLID (behaviour), SOLID WITH LIMITS (brain)

**What is true**
* Coming off a screen is often painful for young children, and some things predict how painful. Hiniker and colleagues interviewed 27 parents and ran a diary study with 28 more families, all with children aged 1 to 5, logging 380 transitions. Children were less upset when the technology ended the session than when a parent did (2.98 against 3.47 on a five point upset scale). They were also less upset when screen time was a routine part of the day (2.84 against 3.10). [Hiniker et al. 2016, CHI](http://faculty.washington.edu/jkientz/papers/Hiniker-Tantrums-CHI2016.pdf)
* Parents found episode and film endings easy to enforce, and stopping a video partway through much harder. Eleven of the 27 parents raised autoplay unprompted as something they fight. A real parent from that study, verbatim: "If the new one starts, then he's usually very upset." (same URL)
* **The counterintuitive finding.** Children were *more* upset when a parent warned them that screen time was ending (3.35 against 3.03). That held even after removing transitions the child chose. A warning on its own is not the fix. (same paper)
* **The brain level, kept honest.** Inhibitory control is unusually hard for young children and keeps maturing through adolescence. [Diamond 2013, Annual Review of Psychology 64](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4084861/) Dopamine neurons signal the gap between what was expected and what arrived. They fire more when an outcome is better than expected, stay at baseline when it is as expected, and dip below baseline when an expected reward is withdrawn. [Schultz 2016, Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience 18(1)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826767/)
* In teenagers, holding back a response gets *harder* specifically when the cue is rewarding. On neutral cues their control improves steadily with age. Teens showed a dip on rewarding cues (happy faces), alongside greater ventral striatum activity. [Somerville, Hare and Casey 2011, Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience 23(9)](https://resolver.caltech.edu/CaltechAUTHORS:20110803-103931835) *(abstract)*

**Where it stops**
Nobody has measured dopamine in a child turning off a tablet, a point our own 6 October research already made (plans/2026-10-06-two-brains-research.md). The prediction error account explains why ending a rewarding activity is hard in principle. It has not been shown at the screen. Both Hiniker samples are small, US based and mostly white, and their children are under six. The upset scores are diary associations, not a trial.

**Filmable as:** a child mid episode. The episode ends and the screen simply stops. Then a second take where the next episode slides in before anyone decides.

### 2B. Design that removes the ending. SOLID WITH LIMITS (one strong field experiment)

* In a three week experiment in 24 homes (children aged 3 to 5, mean 3.6), preschoolers used a video app in three versions. One was neutral. One locked them out at their own limit. One played extra videos once the planned ones finished. The extra videos version lengthened viewing, made children less likely to stop on their own, and drew more parent intervention. **The hard lock out did not reduce viewing or parent intervention.** [Hiniker, Heung, Hong and Kientz 2018, CHI](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/CocosVideos.pdf)
* The detail that matters for us: children planned their playlist and chose what they would do next. When the end screen asked whether they were ready for that activity, many switched off and announced their next activity themselves, without a parent stepping in. The authors conclude that design which does not undermine a child's own intentions helps self regulation more than rigid controls do. (same paper)
* **Why this is our thesis in miniature:** not parental controls, but a plan made together and kept by the device.

### 2C. Sleep: time first, light second. SOLID (time), SOLID WITH LIMITS (light)

* **The association is large and consistent.** A meta analysis of 20 studies covering 125,198 children (mean age 14.5) found bedtime device use linked to roughly double the odds of too little sleep (OR 2.17). Simply *having* a device in the bedroom without using it was also linked to too little sleep (OR 1.79). All the studies were observational and heterogeneity was high. [Carter et al. 2016, JAMA Pediatrics 170(12)](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/91557/) *(abstract)*
* **Light is real but smaller than the headlines.** Twelve adults read on an iPad at full brightness for four hours a night over five nights. They took about 10 minutes longer to fall asleep than after a print book (25.7 against 15.8), with evening melatonin suppressed and their clock delayed. [Chang et al. 2015, PNAS 112(4)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4313820) *(abstract)* One hour of bright tablet use in 16 teenagers produced no significant sleep effects and only small changes in alertness. [Heath et al. 2014, Chronobiology International](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/does-one-hour-of-bright-or-short-wavelength-filtered-tablet-scree/) *(abstract)* iPhone Night Shift made no difference to sleep in 167 young adults. Only putting the phone down helped, and only in a subgroup. [Duraccio et al. 2021, Sleep Health](https://gwern.net/doc/zeo/2021-duraccio.pdf) *(secondary)*
* **But children are more light sensitive than adults.** Primary school children (mean age 9.2) showed about twice the melatonin suppression of adults under the same bright light. The subgroups were very small: 5 children against 6 adults. [Higuchi et al. 2014, JCEM 99(9)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24840814/) *(abstract)* In 36 children aged 3 to 5, an hour of light before bed suppressed melatonin by 85 percent on average, and even dim light (5 to 40 lux) suppressed it by about 78 percent. [Hartstein et al. 2022, Journal of Pineal Research 72(2)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8933063) *(abstract)*
* **The field's current model.** Gradisar's group now treats screens and sleep as a two way street: poor sleepers also reach for devices to fill time or to calm down. They recommend harm minimisation rather than total exclusion. [Bauducco et al. 2024, Sleep Medicine Reviews 76:101933](https://em-consulte.com/article/1677669/resume/a-bidirectional-model-of-sleep-and-technology-use-) *(abstract)* An author summary I could not open directly puts light and arousal at a few minutes of delay and use past bedtime at around an hour of lost sleep. **Do not put those two numbers on screen until the full paper is checked.**
* **The intervention signal.** 63 Australian teenagers aged 14 to 18 stopped their phone an hour before bed for one school week. Lights out moved about 17 minutes earlier (22:57 to 22:40) and total sleep rose by about 21 minutes (7:36 to 7:57). The design was single arm with no control group. [Bartel et al., Health Communication, as tabled in Scottish Government review 2020](https://gov.scot/publications/systematic-literature-review-relationship-between-adolescents-screen-time-sleep-mental-health/pages/18)
* **Teen clocks really do shift later.** Across puberty the circadian clock delays and sleep pressure builds more slowly, so teenagers find it easier to stay up. [Carskadon and Tarokh 2014, Nutrition Reviews](https://boris.unibe.ch/59665); [Crowley et al. 2018, the perfect storm update](https://prdext3.cga.ct.gov/kid/tfs/20221118_Task%20Force%20to%20Study%20the%20Comprehensive%20Needs%20of%20Children%20in%20the%20State/School%20Start%20Times/Crowley%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20An%20update%20on%20adolescent%20sleep_%20New%20evidence%20informing%20the%20perfect%20storm%20model.pdf) *(secondary)*
* **UK guidance agrees with the product default.** The RCPCH advises avoiding screens for an hour before planned sleep. It also tells families to make a plan together in a calm moment, praise and, where appropriate, reward keeping to it, and apply it consistently. [RCPCH parent fact sheet, January 2019](https://www.justonenorfolk.nhs.uk/media/q3mntxp4/rcpch_screen_time_parent_fact_sheet_-_final.pdf) The UK Chief Medical Officers advised leaving phones outside the bedroom at bedtime. [CMO advice, February 2019, via AOP](https://www.aop.org.uk/ot/news/2019/02/11/uk-chief-medical-officer-issues-screen-time-guidance) *(secondary)*
* **Proof path in the product.** `lib/quests/time-tiers.ts`: protected windows that no stars can buy (bedtime, meals, school). The default bedtime window starts an hour before the typical bedtime for each age band (18:00 for ages 4 to 7, 19:00 for 8 to 10, 20:00 for 11 to 13, 21:00 for 13 to 15). A child who starts inside the window becomes an ask to the parent.

### 2D. Adolescent sensitivity to peers, likes and being watched. SOLID WITH LIMITS

* Adolescents respond with more emotional intensity to social evaluation. Somerville calls the evidence preliminary but mounting, and says the neural models are tentative. [Somerville 2013, Current Directions in Psychological Science 22(2)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3992953)
* Just *believing* a peer was watching through a camera raised embarrassment, physiological arousal and medial prefrontal activity, and the effect peaked in adolescence. The sample was 69 people aged 8 to about 22. [Somerville et al. 2013, Psychological Science 24(8)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/why-self-consciousness-peaks-in-teenage-years.html) *(secondary)*
* In a simulated Instagram feed, 32 teenagers aged 13 to 18 (some viewing their own photos) showed more nucleus accumbens activity to photos with many likes, and they liked whatever had many likes. **The like counts were assigned by the researchers.** [Sherman et al. 2016, Psychological Science 27(7)](https://greenfieldlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/168/2025/04/sherman-et-al-2016-the-power-of-the-like-in-adolescence-effects-of-peer-influence-on-neural-and-behavioral-responses-to.pdf)
* At population level, UK data showed a negative link between social media use and later life satisfaction for girls aged 11 to 13 and boys aged 14 to 15, and for both sexes at 19. The link ran both ways, and it cannot predict which individual child is at risk. [Orben, Przybylski, Blakemore and Kievit 2022, Nature Communications](https://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/blog/2022/03/scientists-find-that-the-impact-of-social-media-on-wellbeing-varies-across-adolescence/) *(secondary)*
* **How the field caveats itself (use this as the model).** 169 North Carolina pupils who checked social media habitually showed rising brain sensitivity to anticipated social feedback over three years, while their peers' sensitivity fell. Co author Eva Telzer said publicly that they cannot claim social media changes the brain, and the change could even be adaptive. [Maza et al. 2023, JAMA Pediatrics 177(2)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9857400) *(abstract and press)*
* **The positive pathway has brain evidence too.** Telzer's review argues that teenage reward sensitivity can protect as well as endanger. [Telzer 2016, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience 17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4727991) *(abstract)* In 32 Mexican American teenagers aged 15 to 17, stronger ventral striatum activity when giving money to their *family* at a cost to themselves predicted less risk taking about ten months later. [Telzer, Fuligni, Lieberman and Galván 2013, DCN 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4598077) This is the honest neuroscience behind "jobs for the family". It is one small sample, it is association, and it must never be sold as an outcome.

### 2E. Dopamine, done right (if at all). SOLID, but do not put it on screen

* Dopamine tracks prediction error (Schultz 2016, above), and it drives *wanting* rather than *liking* (Berridge and Robinson 2016, American Psychologist 71(8), [PMC5171207](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5171207/)). The Berridge paper was verified in our 6 October research. This run hit a captcha, so I could not reopen it.
* **Recommendation:** leave the word dopamine out of the parent film. The accurate version is too subtle for three seconds, and any mention invites the "hit" frame. If it must appear, the only line I would sign is: **"Dopamine is about wanting the next one, not enjoying this one."** Even that is a simplification a purist would query.

## 3. Myths and overreach, with what to say instead

| Popular claim | Grade | Why | Say instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Screens give a dopamine hit / dopamine addiction" | MYTH as phrased | Dopamine is a learning and wanting signal released by any engaging activity, including food, music and friends. It is not a pleasure dose. Nobody has measured it in a child at a screen (Schultz 2016; Berridge and Robinson 2016). | "Autoplay starts the next one before anyone decides." |
| "Digital heroin" / "like a drug" | MYTH | Drugs act directly on dopamine chemistry. The one PET study of a video game found striatal dopamine release in eight adult men, about a 13 percent average change in a binding measure, with a wide range. That is consistent with any rewarding task, not with drug equivalence. [Koepp et al. 1998, Nature 393](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9607763/) *(abstract)* Critics of the drug metaphor: [Hartogsohn and Vudka 2022, Transcultural Psychiatry](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13634615221105116) *(abstract)* | "Most children are not addicted. Some really do struggle." |
| "Addicted" as a general label | OVERREACH | Gaming disorder is a real ICD 11 diagnosis requiring impaired control, rising priority, continuing despite harm and significant impairment, usually over 12 months. WHO says it affects a small proportion of players. [WHO Q&A](https://www.who.int/news-room/q-a-detail/gaming-disorder) *(secondary)* 11 percent of adolescents showed signs of problematic social media use in 2022, up from 7 percent in 2018, with the peak at age 13. [HBSC data browser, WHO Europe 2024](https://data-browser.hbsc.org/topics/social-media/) | "For about one teen in ten, it is more than a habit." (HBSC 2022) |
| "Social media is rewiring their brains" | OVERREACH | All experience changes the brain. That is what learning is. Odgers' Nature review of The Anxious Generation found the rewiring and epidemic claims unsupported, the data mostly correlational, and the ABCD study showing no drastic brain changes. [Odgers 2024, Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-024-00902-2) Note that critics of Odgers exist. We are ban neutral, so do not pick a side on Haidt in the film. | "Every experience shapes the brain. That is called learning." |
| "The frontal lobe is not finished until 25" | MYTH | No dataset sets 25 as an endpoint. The number probably reflects where early scanning studies stopped recruiting. [Burnett 2024, BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/comment/brain-myth-25-development) Lifespan network data put the biggest turning point at about 32, with further change at 66 and 83, from cross sectional scans that do not measure decision making. [Mousley et al. 2025, Nature Communications 16:10055](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65974-8) | "Self control is still developing, right through the teens." (Diamond 2013) |
| "Teens are all accelerator, no brakes" | OVERREACH | Teen control is good on neutral cues. The dip is specific to rewarding cues (Somerville, Hare and Casey 2011). | "Teens find holding back hardest when the reward is right there." |
| "Blue light kills melatonin, so that is why they cannot sleep" | OVERREACH | The light effect is real but small at normal brightness. Night Shift did nothing. Displacement and arousal are the bigger levers (2C). Our own sleep film already says light is the smaller half (content/lesson-scripts/stage-2-films/2.3-screens-and-sleep.md). | "The bigger sleep cost is the time, not the light." |
| "Screens thin or shrink the cortex" (60 Minutes, 2018) | OVERREACH | The ABCD analysis was exploratory and cross sectional, and cortical thinning is normal maturation. NIH's spokesperson said they did not know whether screen time caused it. [Paulus et al. 2019, NeuroImage 185](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6487868/) *(abstract and press)* | Do not go there. |
| "Attention span of a goldfish, 8 seconds" | MYTH | Traced to a marketing statistic with no study behind it. [BBC More or Less, via Wisconsin course copy](https://internet.psych.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/532-Master/532-UnitPages/Unit-09/Goldfish_MythBusting.pdf) *(secondary)* | Never use. |
| "Fast cartoons damage attention" | OVERREACH | Nine minutes of a fast fantastical cartoon lowered 4 year olds' executive function scores *immediately afterwards* (60 children). A follow up found fantasy content, not pace, drove it, and nothing was shown about lasting effects. [Lillard and Peterson 2011, Pediatrics](https://pepe.univ-paris8.fr/s/impact_num/item/6275); [Lillard et al. 2015, Developmental Psychology](https://www.ovid.com/journals/depsy/pdf/10.1037/a0039097~further-examination-of-the-immediate-impact-of-television-on) *(abstracts)* | Leave it out of a sales short. |
| "A two minute warning stops the meltdown" | Contradicted | Warned transitions were *more* upsetting in the Hiniker 2016 diary data. | "Warnings alone do not fix it. Endings do." |

Both studies are cross sectional. Our mitigations are real: a free daily core, a weekly cap, protected windows, and a rate that can fade as the child grows (the starMinutes comment in time-tiers.ts). In a quick read I found no path that takes stars away as a punishment, and holiday-bank.ts and bank.ts comments explicitly avoid punishing; confirm before the film says so. **The film shows how it works and makes no claim about less screen time.** |

**What I would tell Justin plainly (this is feedback, so it goes through the feedback filter skill):** the star to minutes exchange is the single feature a hostile developmental expert will challenge. The defensible framing is "a plan you make together, with the device keeping time and bedtime that cannot be bought." The framing that will not survive is "earn your screen time."

## 6. One visual metaphor that is true: the last page

**The image.** A picture book at bedtime. A small hand turns pages until the last one: the end, and the book closes. Cut to a tablet. The episode ends, but before the last frame fades, the next one slides in. The last page has been torn out, so the page turning never stops. Then the real Guided Childhood timer runs down to "Time is up", using the real component from the ref pages, never an invented screen. The last page is back.

**Why it is true**
1. Parents rely on episode and film endings as natural stopping points, and stopping partway through is much harder (Hiniker 2016).
2. Autoplay and its cousin post play removed the ending. It lengthened viewing, reduced children's self stopping and increased parent intervention (Hiniker 2018).
3. When the device, not the parent, ended screen time, children were less upset (Hiniker 2016).
4. A feed with no bottom literally has no last page. That is a description of the design, not an effect claim, so it holds for older children too.

**Where it must stop**
* Do not show the child suddenly calm. No outcome is promised, and the evidence is for under sixes.
* Do not show a two minute warning as the cure. The data point the other way.
* Do not show a next activity card. The timer does not have one.

**Runner up for the brand film, not the timer film:** scaffolding that comes down one pole at a time. Self regulation grows inside warm relationships, structure and coaching ([Rosanbalm and Murray, OPRE Report 2017 80, the co regulation practice brief](https://acf.gov/opre/report/co-regulation-birth-through-young-adulthood-practice-brief), *secondary*). The product literally fades: star rate per child, and no bedtime window at 16 plus. It is true, but it is more abstract than the last page.

**Rejected:** brakes and accelerator (oversimplified, see section 3); brain under construction until 25 (myth); the dopamine slot machine (fear frame, unmeasured in children).

## 8. Age map for the stage check

* **Ages 2 to 6:** transitions, autoplay and device endings (Hiniker 2016, 2018), high light sensitivity (Hartstein 2022), immediate effects of fantastical TV (Lillard).
* **Ages 7 to 12, the heart of the market:** self control still developing (Diamond), children more light sensitive than adults (Higuchi, mean age 9.2), and the girls' 11 to 13 window starting (Orben 2022). Lead the primary age film with stopping and sleep, not scans.
* **Ages 13 to 16:** social evaluation (Somerville, Sherman), the reward cue dip (Somerville 2011), the later body clock (Carskadon), the pre bed phone stop (Bartel), peak problematic use at 13 (HBSC), and the prosocial reward flip side (Telzer).
