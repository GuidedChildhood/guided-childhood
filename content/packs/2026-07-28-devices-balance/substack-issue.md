## We have been counting the wrong thing

For years, every one of us has been counting the same number. Screen time.
Hours and minutes. The figure the phone hands you on a Sunday morning, the one
the school newsletter quotes, the one that fills the news each time a report
lands. We have organised the whole worry around it. And the best recent evidence
quietly says that number barely predicts anything at all.

I want to walk through why, because it changed how I parent, and it is the reason
I am building what I am building instead of the obvious thing everyone expects.

### The clock cannot tell two children apart

Start with the study that stopped me. A paper in JAMA last year followed more
than four thousand young people across four years. Total screen time was not
associated with the harm everyone fears. What was, and strongly, was a particular
shape of use. The compulsive kind. Cannot put it down. Anxious without it. Using
it to fill a gap where something else used to sit. The clock said the same two
hours for two different teenagers. The pattern underneath said one was fine and
one was not.

Picture them. Two children, the identical two hours. One comes off calm, having
messaged three friends and finished editing a video she is proud of. The other
comes off frayed, having scrolled in silence and met no one. Same number. Two
completely different evenings, two completely different children. And the only
tool most of us reach for, the timer, is blind to the difference that actually
matters.

This is the whole reason I am wary of the screen time counter as a solution. It
measures the easy thing precisely and the important thing not at all. It counts
the hours and misses the child.

None of this means screens are harmless. I want to be careful here, because this
argument gets misread as everything is fine. It is not that. For a child already
carrying something heavy, a phone can absolutely make things worse, and the same
JAMA study is the evidence for it. The point is narrower and more useful. The
thing we can measure cheaply, the hours, is mostly the wrong thing. The thing
that matters, the pattern and what it is feeding, takes a parent paying
attention, which is slower and more human and cannot be turned into a red number
on a dashboard.

And underneath even that sits a bigger, quieter truth I have come to believe.
The largest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are not inside the
phone. They are poverty, the things that happen to a child at home, and a
parent's own mental health carried with far too little help. Social media takes
the blame because it is visible, and because it is rich enough to make the
headline. The real drivers sit in the dark, because they are old, and expensive,
and awkward to put in a photograph. I do not lead with that, because said cold to
a worried parent it sounds like minimising. But it is the floor everything else
here stands on.

### So I built a calendar, not a counter

Everyone building in this space builds the counter first. The dashboard of hours.
The number that goes red. I went the other way, and the choice explains what I
think the job actually is.

Here is the insight the whole thing runs on. A child's birthday gives you their
exact school year. The England school calendar is fixed, and it is identical for
every family in the country. Put those two facts together and you can tell a
parent what is coming, and when, without knowing one single thing about how their
own child is doing. Year 4 in April means the multiplication tables check is
about six weeks out. Year 1 means the phonics screening is near. Year 6 in
October means the secondary school application deadline falls at the end of the
month, the one genuinely immovable date in the whole of primary school, and the
one most parents find out about too late.

Notice what is missing. No test. No score. I made a deliberate decision that the
product will never tell you whether your child is on track, and it says so out
loud, because a parent arriving from a screen time app reasonably expects to be
told. I cannot know if your child is on track without testing them, and the
moment you test a child they quietly stop telling you what they found hard. The
valuable thing was never the score. It was the order, and the timing.

The single highest value moment the product has is Year 6 into Year 7. That is
when the first phone usually arrives. And the thing nobody else can hand you is
not a verdict on whether to buy it. It is the order to do it in. Rules agreed a
fortnight before the phone are simply how it works. The exact same rules bolted
on a fortnight after feel like a punishment, like something taken away. Same
rules, word for word. Completely different child on the other side of them,
depending only on timing. That is the part that cannot be redone, and it is the
part we protect.

There is a homework decoder built on the same idea. Paste in the homework, and
instead of a verdict you get the statutory line sitting behind it, what that line
is actually for, and one thing you could do tonight to help. It needs nothing
about your child. Only their year group, and the curriculum. It ships while on
track cannot, precisely because it does not need to judge anyone.

A counter tells you what already happened. I wanted to build the thing that taps
you on the shoulder before the moment that matters, while there is still time to
do it well.

### The five good reasons to reach for a screen

Which brings me to the part we almost never teach. We spend so long on why to put
the phone down that we forget to teach the thing that actually protects a child.
Why to pick it up.

Because there are good reasons, and a child who knows them is far harder to catch
out than one who has only ever been told no. In the curriculum we teach five, and
we teach every one of them holding hands with its catch, never the good clean and
never the catch alone.

Connection. It keeps a friendship alive across a distance, the friend who moved
away, the cousin abroad, the group chat that keeps a team together after the trip
ends. The catch is that always reachable can start to feel like you can never log
off.

Belonging. It helps a child find their people, and it matters most for the child
on the edge of things, the young carer, the LGBTQ+ teen, the one with a condition
nobody at school has heard of. Finding a group who say me too can lift a real
weight, and the research backs that plainly. The catch is that the same community
that holds you is the one that can pile on.

Voice. Standing for something and being heard. The catch is that being visible is
also being a target.

Creativity and learning. Making something beats scrolling past everyone else's
making, and the skill learned, the thing built, the homework done are real goods.
Ofcom finds a large share of UK young people create rather than only consume. The
catch is the pressure to perform and chase the numbers.

Support. Finding people who understand exactly what you are going through. The
catch is that a warm stranger is still a stranger, and online advice is not the
same as a trained adult.

Teach a child only the catches and you have taught them fear, which they will set
aside the first time a screen is genuinely good to them, and one day it will be.
Teach them the good with its catch attached, and you have taught them judgement,
which travels into every room you will never be standing in.

### The offline half is the ballast

And here is where the balance actually lives. The offline half of a child's life
is not the enemy of that list of good reasons. It is the ballast that keeps the
screen one good thing among many, rather than the only thing in the boat.

There is a fact I keep returning to. Independent childhood mobility, the freedom
to roam, to walk to school alone, to be unsupervised for an afternoon, collapsed
decades before the smartphone existed. Something close to eighty percent of young
children walked to school on their own in the early seventies. By 1990, well
before any of this, it was under ten percent. Which makes me think the screen is
partly filling a hole we dug long before we handed anyone a phone. If that is
even half right, then taking the screen away without refilling the hole just
leaves the hole.

So the move in our house, and the move the whole product is built around, is not
subtraction. It is rebuilding the real world time beside the screen. Unstructured,
a little unsupervised, genuinely their own. Not because screens are wicked, but
because a child with a full offline life reaches for the phone for the good
reasons and puts it down when the good runs out. That is balance. Not a number.
A life with enough in it that the screen has to earn its place.

### Where this leaves us

Put the three together and you have the whole of what I believe and what I am
trying to build. It was never really the hours, so I stopped counting them. It
was the pattern, and the timing, and the judgement, so I built for those instead.
A calendar that warns you before the moment, not a counter that scolds you after
it. A curriculum that teaches the good with the catch, so a child arrives at
sixteen with judgement rather than just rules. And a steady rebuilding of the
offline life that keeps all of it in proportion.

The ban delays the apps. It does not build the judgement. That is the part we
build, one stage at a time, from age four.
