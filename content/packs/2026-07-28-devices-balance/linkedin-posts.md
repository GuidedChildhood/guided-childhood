## THREAD 1 (Believe) — It was never the hours

**Post text:**

For years we have all been counting the wrong thing.

Screen time. Hours and minutes. The number every app puts in front of you on a
Sunday, the number schools quote, the number that fills the news. And the best
recent evidence quietly says that number barely predicts anything.

The clearest example. A study in JAMA last year followed more than four thousand
young people for four years. Total screen time was not associated with the harm
everyone fears. What was, and strongly, was a particular shape of use. The
compulsive kind. Cannot put it down. Miserable without it. Filling a gap where
something else used to be. Same hours on the clock, completely different child,
depending on the pattern underneath.

Sit with what that means, because it changes the whole job. Two teenagers spend
the identical two hours on a phone. One comes off calm, having spoken to three
friends and edited a video she is proud of. The other comes off frayed, having
scrolled in silence and met no one. The clock cannot tell them apart. Only the
pattern can.

So the honest question is not how much, it is what kind, and how does he seem
when he puts it down. Is it connection, or is it a hole being filled. Is he
making things, or only watching. That is a harder question than a time limit,
and it is the only one that has ever actually helped in my house.

None of this means screens are harmless. For a child already struggling, a phone
can clearly make things worse. It means the thing we can measure easily, the
hours, is mostly the wrong thing, and the thing that matters, the pattern, takes
a parent paying attention. Which is slower, and human, and cannot be automated.

We reached for the clock because the clock was easy to read. The child was
always the harder text.

What do you actually watch for, the minutes, or how they seem when they stop.

## THREAD 2 (Build) — I built a calendar, not a screen time counter

**Post text:**

Everyone building in this space builds the same thing first. A screen time
counter. A dashboard of hours. A number that goes red.

I built a calendar instead, and I want to explain why, because the choice says
everything about what I think this job actually is.

Here is the insight the calendar runs on. A child's birthday gives you their
exact school year. The England school calendar is fixed, and identical for every
family in the country. Put those two together and you can tell a parent what is
coming, and when, without knowing a single thing about how their child is doing.
Year 4 in April means the multiplication tables check is about six weeks away.
Year 6 in October means the secondary school deadline is at the end of the month,
the one genuinely immovable date in primary school, the one most parents miss.

No test. No score. I deliberately do not tell you whether your child is on track,
because I cannot know that honestly, and the moment you score a child they stop
telling you what they found hard. The valuable thing was never the score. It was
the order, and the timing.

The highest value moment the whole product has is Year 6 into Year 7. That is
when the first phone usually arrives. And the thing nobody else can give you is
not a verdict on whether to buy it. It is the order. Rules agreed a fortnight
before the phone are just how it works. The exact same rules added a fortnight
after feel like something taken away. Same rules. Completely different child on
the other side of them. Timing is the whole game.

So the product does not count your child's hours. It watches the calendar for
you and taps you on the shoulder a few weeks before each moment that matters,
while there is still time to do it well.

A counter tells you what already happened. I wanted to build the thing that tells
you what is about to.

If you could be warned about one parenting moment a month before it arrived,
which one would you pick.

## THREAD 3 (Solve) — The five good reasons to reach for a screen

**Post text:**

We spend so long on why to put the phone down that we almost never teach the
thing that actually keeps a child safe. Why to pick it up.

Because there are good reasons, and a child who knows them is far harder to
catch out than a child who has only ever been told no. In our curriculum we teach
five, and we teach each one holding hands with its catch, never one without the
other.

Connection. It keeps a friendship alive across a distance, the mate who moved,
the cousin abroad. The catch, always reachable can start to feel like you can
never log off.

Belonging. It helps a child find their people, and this matters most for the one
who feels on the edge of things, the young carer, the LGBTQ+ teen, the kid with a
rare condition. The catch, the place you belong is the same place that can turn.

Voice. Standing for something and being heard. The catch, being visible is being
a target.

Creativity and learning. Making something beats scrolling past everyone else
making things, and the homework, the skill, the thing built on a screen are real
goods. The catch, the pressure to perform and chase numbers.

Support. Finding people who understand exactly what you are going through. The
catch, a warm stranger is still a stranger, and online advice is not a trained
adult.

Teach a child only the catches and you have taught them fear, which they will
quietly ignore the first time the screen is good to them, and it will be. Teach
them the good with its catch, and you have taught them judgement, which travels
with them into rooms you will never see.

And the offline half is not the enemy of that list. It is the ballast. Real
world time, unstructured and a bit unsupervised, is what keeps the screen one
good thing among many rather than the only thing. We rebuild it beside the phone,
not by taking the phone away.

This is the whole approach. Not the device, and not the ban. The judgement to
know which reason you are reaching for, and whether it is a good one today.

We put a free place to start at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack, if you want to
begin this with your own.

What is a genuinely good reason your child reaches for a screen, that you would
not want to take away.
