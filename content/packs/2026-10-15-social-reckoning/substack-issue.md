# Substack issue: The Social Reckoning, fact checked

**Subject line options**
1. The Social Reckoning: what is true, what is drama, and what it leaves out
2. I fact checked the Facebook film so you can talk about it at home
3. A painting of the truth, and the child in the feed tonight

**Preview line:** Sorkin calls his film a painting. Here is what the record says, and what to do with it on a school night.

---

Aaron Sorkin has a good line for what he does with real events. "What I do is a painting," he told Reuters this month, explaining that the major facts in The Social Reckoning are accurate and that the conversations and the timeline are his.

I like that honesty, and I think it is the right way to hold the film. A painting can be truer than a photograph about how something felt. It is less reliable about what happened, in what order, and who said what. If your teenager has seen it, or wants to, both of those matter.

So this week I went through the record. Here is what is solid, what has been changed, and what the evidence on children actually supports. Then the part I care about most, which is what a parent can do on an ordinary evening.

## What is on the record

Frances Haugen worked on Facebook's civic integrity team. In 2021 she took tens of thousands of pages of internal research, shared them with the Wall Street Journal, and her lawyers filed at least eight complaints with the US Securities and Exchange Commission. The Journal's series, the Facebook Files, ran that September.

Two of the stories matter most for parents. One covered Instagram's own research on teenage girls. The other covered a 2018 change to the news feed, meant to promote "meaningful social interactions", which Facebook's own staff warned was rewarding reshared material where toxic and violent content was disproportionately common.

In October 2021 she told the US Senate the company had put "their astronomical profits before people". Three weeks later she gave evidence to the UK Parliament's committee on the draft Online Safety Bill, and the committee's report cites her throughout. That scrutiny fed into the Act that now sets out what platforms owe children here.

None of that is dramatised. It is the spine of the film, and it is real.

## What the film changes

Sorkin says himself that he invented conversations and moved the timeline. Jeff Horwitz, then the Journal reporter at the centre of the story, says Sorkin told him the film's reporter would be a composite, and that he had no say over its content. Haugen has said she is very happy with how it turned out.

Writers who know the story well have pointed to specific changes.

The first contact is moved earlier. In reality Horwitz's message reached her after the 2020 US election, and she replied the day the civic integrity team was disbanded.

She is shown as shy, coaxed along. Slate's account describes a sophisticated source who tested him before she trusted him.

The way she reaches the Instagram research is invented. The findings shown on screen are the real ones.

Several characters are invented or blended, including the investor played by Bill Burr.

The film shows the civic integrity team being shut down, as Haugen and Horwitz describe it. Facebook said at the time that the work moved into a larger team.

Two closing title cards need care. One says 43% of Americans get news from Facebook; the most recent Pew figure I could find is 38% who regularly do. Another says Facebook eliminated fact checking. It ended third party fact checking in the United States in 2025, not everywhere.

None of these changes the core of what happened. Some of them change who you come away thinking the story is about.

## What the research actually said about teenagers

The slide most people remember read "We make body image issues worse for one in three teen girls." It was real, and it deserved the attention it got.

It also came with limits the film has no time for. The figure applied to girls who already said they struggled with body image. The research was internal surveys and focus groups, some with as few as 40 teenagers, and it was not designed to show cause and effect. Facebook's own annotated slides make that point, and so did independent reporting at the time.

That does not make the slide comforting. It makes it a warning about some children rather than a measurement of all of them.

## What the wider evidence supports

The researchers I trust most, partly because they disagree with each other in public, land roughly here.

Across large populations the link between social media use and teenage wellbeing is small on average. One well known analysis of more than 350,000 young people put technology use at no more than 0.4% of the variation in wellbeing. That is a ceiling on an association, not proof of no effect.

The risk is not spread evenly. Work led by Amy Orben at Cambridge found windows in early adolescence, around 11 to 13 for girls and 14 to 15 for boys, when heavier use was linked to lower life satisfaction later on. The effects were small, and nothing in them can tell you which individual child is at risk.

And the design matters. Ofcom describes personalised feeds as children's main pathway to harmful content online, and since July 2025 the law has required risky platforms to filter the most harmful content out of children's feeds and reduce the rest.

What we still do not have is a proper experiment. A trial led from Bradford, with Orben, is randomly assigning year groups, around 4,000 pupils in all, to a daily limit and overnight curfew or their usual use. No results for a while yet. Anyone who tells you the question is settled, in either direction, is ahead of the evidence.

## Where blame helps, and where it stops

The film puts the weight of blame on the algorithms and the people who built them. Some of that is earned, and accountability is arriving. In August, Meta agreed a settlement with more than fifty US attorneys general worth at least $12.1 billion, including a default two hour daily limit for under 18s across Facebook and Instagram that a parent can lift. Meta did not admit wrongdoing. Two US juries have found against it this year, and both verdicts are under appeal.

But blame is a verdict, and verdicts arrive late. The feed your child opens tonight will be shaped as much by the next forty minutes of scrolling as by any courtroom.

That is why I keep coming back to the things a family can actually do.

## What to do tonight

**Teach the noticing.** Not how the algorithm works. Three questions a child can ask in the moment: is this getting narrower, is this getting darker, did I choose this? If the answer is yes, stop scrolling and tell someone.

**Keep the door open.** A child tells you the second time only if the first time went well. Stay calm, get curious, and never make losing the phone the price of telling you. The NSPCC warns that an angry reaction makes children more reluctant to come to you next time, and that matches everything I have seen.

**Use the tools, honestly.** Mute the account, unfollow it, tell YouTube not to recommend the channel, reset the feed. Mozilla's research on YouTube found the stronger options worked far better than tapping Not interested. Expect to do it more than once. It helps, and it does not make the feed safe, which was never your job alone.

**If you watch it together.** It is rated 15 in the UK and it mentions a child's self harm, so watch it first or watch it with them. Afterwards, three questions will do more than any lecture. What do you think was real? Who did the film want you to blame? How would someone your age notice a feed pulling them somewhere they did not choose?

If you teach, I have put together a one page discussion sheet for Years 10 and 11: what is on the record, what is drama, and the safeguarding notes. It is free for any school. In our schools scheme the same skill is taught in full in the Year 10 and 11 lesson on radicalisation and the earlier one on bodies, image and pressure.

A painting is allowed to choose its villain. Our children need something less dramatic and more useful: the habit of noticing, and an adult they will still tell.

Justin
