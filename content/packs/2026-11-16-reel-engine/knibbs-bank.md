Source of every finding: `briefings/2026-08-18-cybertrauma-knibbs-series.md`

## The rules for this bank

- She is credited by name every time: a child and adult trauma psychotherapist
  with over twenty years in practice. That is the load bearing fact. Don't stake
  a claim on the doctorate.
- Her study figures are hers, and small: "of sixteen children she worked with,
  ten..." Never a population percentage, never "children are...". Mechanism, not
  prevalence.
- Cybertrauma is "a pattern she calls cybertrauma", never a diagnosis.
- Hold the reconciliation: the average effect is small and the tail is real.
  These Reels must never read as a flip into panic. Her own line is that
  precision is the opposite of panic.
- We take her findings, never her words or her pieces. Her stance is already
  ours, and she is designed into the product: DiGi's research bank
  (`lib/digi/system.ts`) and the script rehearsal engine
  (`app/api/scripts/rehearse/route.ts`), which says to keep yourself the safe
  person to tell and never make the child the problem.
- K1, K2 and K7 sit in the fear lane: one post in ten at most, across all channels.

## The bank

**K1. The child who doesn't always tell** (week two, Reel 06)
- Her finding, in her published words: the seven to ten year olds she studied,
  who had seen violent and gory content, had already decided adults were not
  safe to tell, not because adults did not care but because they did not know
  what to do with it (catherineknibbs.co.uk/about). Red teamed 16 November.
- Parent question: would my child actually tell me?
- Hook: **Nine in ten children say they'd tell someone. Just over half say they always would.**
- The figure: Ofcom 2025, children aged 8 to 17 (92 percent would tell someone,
  usually a parent; 56 percent always, 36 percent sometimes, 3 percent not), read at source on 16 November 2026. The fair concession: 86 percent of those who did see something worrying told someone.
- Proof: script "A message from someone they do not know" (migration 180), whose
  first words are "Thank you for telling me", and the no confiscation promise in
  `lib/digi/horizons.ts` ("if you show me something bad, the phone stays yours").

**K2. The askable adult beats the report button**
- Her finding: the protection was an engaged adult who doesn't flinch, a calm
  adult to settle alongside.
- Parent question: what actually keeps them safe if the apps don't?
- Hook: **Seven in ten children say they've run into harm online. About a third reported it to the app.**
- The figure: Internet Matters, May 2025, 1,000 UK children aged 9 to 17, a broad
  list of harms; 36 percent of those reported it to the platform. It does not
  measure telling a parent (the 13 November correction). Say exactly that.
- Proof: DiGi as the guide a parent asks first, the family agreement, the scripts
  for the moment they tell you.

**K3. The state under the behaviour**
- Her finding: the digital world acts on a child's nervous system.
- Parent question: why does my child explode when the tablet goes off?
- Hook: **The meltdown when the tablet goes off isn't always defiance.**
- Proof: the rehearsal engine line (above) and script "The visible ending, for the
  child who finds stopping hardest" (migration 298). Pairs with Reel 07.

**K4. Teach before they see it**
- Her finding: some of what affected seven to ten year olds sat below the line the
  Online Safety Act was built to catch.
- Parent question: aren't the new laws dealing with this?
- Hook: **The new law goes after the worst content. A lot of what upsets a seven year old sits under that line.**
- Proof: the pathway starts at age 4 and teaches before exposure; the under 7
  shelf (lesson eyfs-30, migration 358).
- Check before filming: the threshold wording against her report, and keep it to
  "some of what she found".

**K5. The family TV**
- Her finding: some of the content in her study was on the family TV, chosen by
  grown ups, outside any platform's duty.
- Parent question: is it only phones I need to think about?
- Hook: **Some of what upset the children in her study wasn't on a phone. It was on the family TV.**
- Proof: the Foundation stage line, shared screens and watching together.
- Tone: no blame. "We've all had the telly on with a little one in the room."

**K6. Rated 15, watched at 7**
- Her finding: a show rated 15 was among what the children described.
- Parent question: does the age rating actually mean anything?
- Hook: **The rating says 15. The child talking about it in the playground is seven.**
- Proof: `/dashboard/social-settings`: the age on the box is the platform's rule,
  never a readiness test. Pairs with story 15 in the Family IT Guy bank.

**K7. The seeking loop** (use sparingly, red team first)
- Her finding: after exposure, some children went back for more.
- Parent question: why would she watch it again if it scared her?
- Hook: **She was scared by it. Then she went looking for more. That's not as strange as it sounds.**
- Proof: modules 1 and 10, which teach children to see how the feed works.

**K8. Precision, not panic** (a founder voice Reel)
- The reconciliation: our earlier series said the average effect is small. She is
  about the tail. Both are true.
- Parent question: so is it bad or not?
- Hook: **The average effect of screens is small. For some children it isn't. Both are true.**
- Proof: the stage check, which starts from this child, not the average.

## Never make

- A case story of a real or identifiable child, including anything from her study
  beyond what she has published.
- A drawing, a quote or a figure from her reports presented as ours.
- "Screens cause trauma", or any line that turns sixteen children into a rate.
- Any claim that a child will be safer because of us.
