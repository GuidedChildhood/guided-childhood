# GDC-047 · Half of UK children have an AI toy (v3, 9 Nov)

**Job:** recruit strangers. **Plan:** Mon 9 Nov. **Card:** `content/packs/2026-11-09-cards/gdc-047-card.png`, "50%" with
"38% own two or more" underneath, worded to v3 ("bought for them", Focaldata).

## Post

Half of UK children have had an AI toy or learning device bought for them, according to a survey of 1,000 parents.

Thirty eight percent own two or more.

That comes from a May 2026 survey by Focaldata, published by the British Standards Institution. It's a parent survey, so it's what parents report, not what children say. And "AI enabled toy or learning device" covers a lot, from a spelling tutor to a bear that talks back.

So it doesn't mean half of children are in danger. It means a category most of us haven't thought hard about is already in the house.

The talking one is what I keep coming back to. Common Sense Media's January 2026 report says no child aged five or under should use an AI toy, and that parents of six to twelve year olds should exercise caution before buying one.

A toy that answers every question, never gets tired and never says "not now" is teaching a child something about what a relationship is.

A seven year old can't see that for themselves.

Nobody has told most parents what to look for. The BSI itself points to a growing gap between how quickly these toys are being adopted and the guidance available to families.

Four things a child should be able to tell you. Who's on the other side of this. What it remembers. What it can't do. When to go and find a person. We teach a short lesson on exactly that for younger children, and it starts from the idea that the child should be able to explain it to you.

An age on a box won't do that for us.

If a toy in your house could talk back tomorrow, what's the first thing you'd want to know about it?

# GDC-046 · The age is the easy part (unchanged, Justin's own register)

**Job:** start an argument. **Plan:** Wed 11 Nov. **Card:** `content/packs/2026-10-23-readiness-test/gdc-046-card.png` (13, 16, 18; re-rendered 9 Nov in the real Playfair Display and Source Serif 4).

Do not add contractions. He wrote it that way on purpose.

## Post

I have built a product around the word readiness and I still cannot tell you how to test it.

That is the uncomfortable centre of this, and picking a better age does not make it go away.

Everyone argues about the number. Thirteen for an account. Sixteen for social media. Eighteen for an AI companion. I understand why. An age is enforceable, auditable, the same for every child, and a regulator can write it down.

What an age cannot do is describe the child in front of you. I know this, having three very different children.

One child can turn thirteen without knowing that an AI which sounds caring does not care. Without understanding that a chatbot can be confidently wrong about something it has no business being confident about. Without noticing when a conversation is being kept going rather than finished. With no idea when to stop talking to a machine and go and find a person.

So what would a readiness test actually look like.

Not a quiz. A child can pass a quiz on Monday and hand over their address on Friday. Not a certificate either, because the whole point is that it moves as they grow and as the technology does.

The closest I have got is three things a parent can see for themselves. Can the child explain, in their own words, why this thing is showing them this. Can they tell you about a time they stopped. And do they come to you when something turns, which depends entirely on whether coming to you went well enough last time that they would do it again.

That last one is the real test, and it is not about the child at all.

Our stage check is an attempt at this and I will not dress it up as more. Three questions about your own child, and a stage with the things worth practising next. A starting point rather than a measurement. We have no outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently afterwards, and almost nobody in this field does.

An age tells you when the door opens. It tells you nothing about who walks through it.

If you had to judge whether a child was ready for something online, what would you actually look at?

# GDC-045 · Fear is the engine on both sides now (v3, 9 Nov)

**Job:** recruit strangers. **Plan:** Fri 13 Nov. **Card:** needs one, "52.4 to 42.1 percent", accounts held before and after the ban.

## Post

More than 85 percent of Australian teenagers were still on social media three months into the under sixteen ban, and a study from June has been quoted as proof they got round it.

It doesn't say that.

The paper is in the BMJ, from the University of Newcastle. It recruited 436 young people aged 12 to 17, mostly in New South Wales, and followed up with 408 of them three months after the ban began. More than 85 percent of the under sixteens were still using the platforms, mostly on accounts they already had.

Still using is not the same as broke in.

And the regulator's own follow up found the thing nobody is quoting. Among 803 children aged 10 to 15, the share holding an account on an age restricted platform fell from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent. eSafety calls that modest and statistically significant. Any use at all fell far less, so it's not a victory either. It's the one measurable thing the ban has done so far, and it's missing from both arguments.

None of this means the ban works. The BMJ paper says there's insufficient evidence of a real drop in use, the answers are self reported, and it's three months in.

Jonathan Haidt made the loudest case of the decade out of alarm. A generation rewired. An epidemic with a start date. A conclusion that keeps arriving ahead of the effect sizes that would carry it.

The people who spend their days correcting him have started doing the same thing. A Psychology Today piece cited that BMJ paper as proof the ban failed.

The paper doesn't say the ban failed. It says we can't tell yet.

So fear is the engine on both sides now. One side is frightened of the phones. The other is frightened of the ban. Both reach past what the study will carry, because a careful answer doesn't move anybody.

Britain has this argument next, with enforcement expected in 2027.

Which leaves a parent somewhere else entirely. The ban will do whatever it does. What happens in your house between now and the day it lands is the part nobody is legislating.

This is The Wrong Villain series continued.

What would actually change your mind about the ban?
