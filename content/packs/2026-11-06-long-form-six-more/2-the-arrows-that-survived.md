# Issue: The arrows that survived point the other way

**Venue: Substack.** From GDC-049. Evidence: the paper read in full from the
primary PDF, and the university's press release read in full.

## Subject lines

1. Twenty five thousand children, one question about hours, and nothing
2. The arrows that survived point the other way
3. The study that clears social media also shows why we keep measuring it wrong

**Preview line:** The best designed UK study on this found no evidence that social
media predicts later distress. It also measured social media with a single question
about hours. Both of those things are true and they belong in the same issue.

---

A study of 25,629 teenagers in Greater Manchester found no evidence that time spent
on social media predicted later emotional difficulties. Not for girls. Not for
boys. Gaming the same.

I want to take this one seriously, because it cuts against things I have said.

And then I want to show you the single question the study used to measure social
media, because it changes what the finding means without making it any less true.

## What they did, and why the design is the interesting part

The team is at the University of Manchester, working with colleagues at the
Norwegian Institute of Public Health, and they used the #BeeWell cohort: children
across Greater Manchester surveyed each autumn, in 2021, 2022 and 2023.

The sample is unusually good for this field. Average age twelve years and seven
months at the start. Fifty one percent girls. Seventeen percent with special
educational needs, twenty nine percent eligible for free school meals, thirty four
percent from minoritised ethnic groups. That last set of numbers matters, because
a recurring and fair complaint about this literature is that it runs on small,
comfortable, homogeneous samples. This one does not.

One correction before I go on, and it is a correction to myself.

I wrote earlier that the study followed the same children for three years. It is
more complicated than that, and the paper is straight about it. The team used what
they call a drop in approach, including any participant with at least one of the
three data points, and the methods section describes the final sample as 25,629
**observations** rather than 25,629 children measured three times each. Another
2,620 were excluded for missing covariates.

I took the looser version from the university's own press release, which says the
team "followed young people aged 11-14 over three school years". That is the
release being less careful than the paper. I will come back to that, because it
turns out to be the story.

Now the design. It is called a random intercept cross lagged panel model, which is
an ugly name for a genuinely clever idea.

Picture two questions you could ask about the same data. The first: do children who
use more social media tend to be more distressed than other children? The second:
when a particular child uses more social media than that child usually does, does
that child become more distressed afterwards than they usually are?

Almost every study you have read answers the first question and gets reported as
though it answered the second. They are not the same question. The first compares
children to each other, so it picks up everything that makes children different,
including the home they live in, the money in it, and what has happened to them.
The second compares a child to themselves, which strips most of that out.

This model separates the two. And when it does, here is what happens.

**At the between person level**, comparing children to each other, there is a
small but statistically significant positive correlation. Children who use more
social media do report more emotional difficulty. That finding survives.

**At the within person level**, following the same child over time, it goes. Social
media use in one year did not predict emotional difficulties the next, for girls or
for boys. Gaming did not either. The authors ran it again splitting active use from
passive scrolling, and got the same answer.

So the honest summary is not "no link". It is: the link between children is real,
and the link within a child, across a year, is not there. Which is exactly what you
would expect if something about a child's life were producing both.

## The two arrows that did survive, and they point backwards

This is the part almost nobody has reported, and it is the most interesting thing
in the paper.

Two effects did clear the bar the authors set for themselves.

For girls, more gaming in year two predicted **less** time on social media in year
three. For boys, more emotional difficulty in year two predicted **less** gaming in
year three, an effect the paper says met a lower bound of 0.10 and was therefore
"likely meaningful".

Read that second one again. Feeling worse came first, and the gaming went down
afterwards. The direction we spend all our time arguing about ran the other way.

The paper's co author Professor Neil Humphrey put it plainly in the release: young
people's choices around social media and gaming "may be shaped by how they're
feeling but not necessarily the other way around."

I do not think that means the worry is misplaced. I think it means a teenager who
goes quiet and stops playing the game they loved is telling you something, and a
parent watching the clock will read it as good news.

## The sentence that is doing the work, and the one question underneath it

Here is how the study measured social media use. One question, adapted from the
Millennium Cohort Study:

> On a normal weekday during term time, how much time do you spend on social media?

With an example, "sites or apps like TikTok, Instagram, and Snapchat", and answers
in hourly bands from zero to seven or more.

Gaming was one question too. How often do you play games on a computer or console,
with six options collapsed down to four: rarely, occasionally, sometimes, often.

That is it. That is the exposure.

Now, I am not sneering at it. Single item measures from a validated national cohort
are standard, the alternative is asking twenty five thousand children to install
tracking software, and the authors name self report as a limitation themselves:
social media and gaming here "do not capture an objective assessment nor a nuanced
reflection of these constructs".

But look at what that question can and cannot see.

It cannot see who the child was talking to. It cannot see whether the hour was
spent building something with a school friend or being slowly frozen out of a group
chat. It cannot see a feed narrowing. It cannot see one message at eleven at night
that the child is still carrying at breakfast. A child who had the worst evening of
their year and a child who had a lovely one both answer "two hours".

So the finding, stated as precisely as I can manage, is this: **self reported hours
on a normal weekday do not predict a child's emotional difficulties a year later.**

That is a real result and it is bad news for anybody whose argument runs on hours.
It is also not the same sentence as "social media is not affecting children", and
the gap between those two sentences is where the press release went.

## Where the release went further than the paper

The paper's conclusion reads: the findings "do not support the widely held view
that adolescent technology use is a major causal factor in their mental health
difficulties". In the discussion, the authors say these activities "may not play a
causal role".

May not. A lack of evidence for a thing, carefully stated.

The university's release, published on 14 January 2026, is headlined "Time spent on
gaming and social media not to blame for teen mental health issues". The opening
line says there is little evidence that social media and gaming "are causing" mental
health problems. Another line says technology habits "did not appear to drive"
difficulties.

Not to blame. Are causing. Those are claims about the world. "May not play a causal
role" is a claim about the evidence. The distance between them is small enough to
miss and large enough to matter, and it is the distance that the headline
travelled.

To be fair to the release, it does carry the caveats. It says plainly that this
does not mean online experiences are harmless, and that hurtful messages, online
pressures and extreme content can all have real impacts on wellbeing. It mentions
both of the backwards arrows. Lead author Dr Qiqi Cheng is quoted saying the results
do not support the idea that simply spending time on these things leads to mental
health problems, and then adds, "the story is far more complex than that".

That is a good release with a headline that undoes part of it. Which is how most of
them work, and why I would rather read the paper.

And I am in no position to be smug, since I repeated the release's description of
the sample without checking it against the methods. The thing I am complaining
about is the thing I did.

## What a parent actually takes from this

Three things, and then one to do this week.

**The hours are not the signal.** The best designed study we have, on the biggest
and most mixed UK sample, looked at hours and found nothing predictive. If you have
been keeping score on the clock and feeling like you are failing, you have been
grading yourself on a measure that the evidence does not support. You can put that
down.

**A withdrawal is a signal.** The one effect in this paper that was likely
meaningful was boys who felt worse playing less afterwards. Stopping is data.

**The fight was never really about the hours.** Mine wasn't. The argument in my
house was about the thing in my son's hand that kept asking for him back, and about
what happened in his head afterwards. No clock reaches that.

### The thing to do this week

Swap one question for three. Instead of "how long have you been on that", which the
evidence now says is close to useless as a predictor, ask the three things that
single survey question could not see.

**Who were you talking to.** Not an interrogation, a normal question asked
normally, the way you would ask who they sat with at lunch.

**What did it leave you with.** The study measured emotional difficulties a year
later. You can ask about ten minutes later, which is a thing no researcher can do
and you can do tonight.

**Did anything happen that you nearly told me about.** This is the one that
matters, and it is not really a question about the phone. It is a question about
whether coming to you went well enough last time that they would do it again.

Ask one of the three. Not all three, not as a list, and not tonight if tonight is
already difficult. One, asked lightly, and then stop talking.

If you want a starting point for where your own child is, our stage check is three
questions and gives you a stage with the things worth practising next. It is a
starting point and not a measurement, and I will not pretend otherwise: we hold no
outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently afterwards,
and almost nobody in this field does.

## The thing I keep coming back to

Twenty five thousand children, three years, one of the better designed studies in
the field, and the answer to "do the hours predict the misery" is no.

I believe that result. I also think it has cleared away the wrong question, which
leaves the real one standing in the open where everybody can see it.

Not how long. What, and who with, and what happened after.

## Sources, and what each one can carry

**The paper.** Qiqi Cheng, Margarita Panayiotou, Turi Reiten Finserås, Amanda
Iselin Olesen Andersen and Neil Humphrey, "How do social media use, gaming
frequency, and internalizing symptoms predict each other over time in early to
middle adolescence?", Journal of Public Health, volume 48, issue 1, March 2026,
pages 59 to 69, doi 10.1093/pubmed/fdaf150. Cheng, Panayiotou and Humphrey are at
the Manchester Institute of Education, University of Manchester; Finserås and
Andersen at the Norwegian Institute of Public Health in Bergen. **Read in full from
the primary PDF for this issue**, which is why this issue can quote the measures
and the methods rather than the summary of them.

Everything here comes from that document: the three waves in the autumns of 2021,
2022 and 2023; the drop in approach including any participant with at least one
data point; the 25,629 observations in the main analytical model and the 2,620
excluded; the demographic breakdown; the random intercept cross lagged panel model
and what it separates; the between person positive correlations; the absence of
within person prediction in either direction for either sex; the active versus
passive sensitivity analysis; the two surviving backwards effects and the 0.07 and
0.10 benchmarks they were judged against; and both quoted conclusions.

**The measures**, quoted exactly: the social media item and the gaming item are
both single items adapted from the Millennium Cohort Study, and internalising
symptoms were the ten item emotional difficulties subscale of the Me and My
Feelings measure, with internal consistency of 0.88, 0.89 and 0.90 across the three
waves.

**The limitation I lean on** is the authors' own, and I am not using it against
them. They write that social media and gaming are both self reported and "do not
capture an objective assessment nor a nuanced reflection of these constructs".

**The press release.** University of Manchester, "Time spent on gaming and social
media not to blame for teen mental health issues", 14 January 2026, read in full.
Every quoted phrase from it is verbatim. The release was published around thirteen
months after the paper first appeared online and in the run up to the March 2026
print issue, which is why coverage of this study clusters in January 2026 while the
paper is dated 2026.

**One date I will not pin.** The paper's own front matter and the journal's article
page give different online publication dates for it. I have not been able to
reconcile them, so this issue gives the print issue, March 2026, which both agree
on, and nothing more precise.

**What this issue does not claim.** That social media is harmless, which neither
the paper nor the release says. That the study measured anything other than
self reported hours and frequency. Any outcome for any child from anything we
build.
