# Issue: The thing parents want least is the thing being sold

**Venue: Substack.** From GDC-047. Evidence: both American primary reports read in
full from their PDFs. The UK figure is one survey and is flagged as such.

## Subject lines

1. The thing parents want least from an AI toy is the thing being sold
2. A teddy bear, a banned developer, and three months later
3. Half the children in the country have one, and almost nobody is checking

**Preview line:** Parents rank companionship last among everything they want from
an AI toy. Companionship is the category. Here is what two teams found when they
actually tested them, and the enforcement gap underneath.

---

Ask parents what they want an AI toy to do for their child and they put
companionship last. Dead last, out of seven options, on 22 percent. More than half
say they actively do not want these things acting as a friend or providing
emotional comfort.

Now look at what is being sold. The category is called AI companion toys.

That gap is the story, and it is a more useful story than the one in the
headlines, because it is not about whether the toys are evil. It is about a product
being built to do the one job its buyers put at the bottom of the list.

Half of UK children have already had one bought for them.

## The numbers, and whose they are

I want to be careful here, because the good research on this is American and the UK
number is thin, and those two facts get blended in most coverage.

**The UK figure.** A survey of 1,000 UK parents, carried out by Focaldata in May
2026 and published by the British Standards Institution, found parents reporting
that around half of their children have had an AI toy or learning device bought for them,
and that 38 percent own two or more. It is a parent survey, so it is what parents
report rather than what children do, and "AI enabled toy or learning device" is a
wide box that holds a spelling tutor and a talking bear in the same hand. I have
not read the underlying data, only the published summary. And the headline the BSI
itself drew from it was not that children are in danger. It was that guidance for
the adults buying these things barely exists.

**The American figures** are better and are not ours. Common Sense Media surveyed
1,004 US adults in a poll weighted to represent American adults, and separately
sat down and tested three of these toys. Both reports are published and I have read
both.

From the survey:

- **49 percent** of parents have bought or are considering an AI enabled toy or
  device. **43 percent** do not own one and do not intend to.
- **83 percent** are concerned about these toys collecting their child's personal
  information. It is the single highest concern they measured.
- Then setting usage limits and cybersecurity, both on 79 percent. Then the toy
  saying something inappropriate, on 74 percent. Then less time playing with
  friends and family, 64 percent.
- What parents actually want: helping their child learn, 70 percent. Encouraging
  good manners, 62 percent. Practising conversation and social skills, 51 percent.
  Imaginative play, 40 percent. Monitoring the child when the parent is out of the
  room, 37 percent. Something to do independently, 35 percent.
- **Companionship, 22 percent. Last.**
- Only **19 percent** would at least somewhat like an AI companion for their child.
  **56 percent** say they do not want one acting as a companion or giving emotional
  comfort. Among parents of nought to four year olds, 84 percent express little or
  no interest in that.

One more from that survey which I did not expect, and which I think deserves more
attention than it will get. Uptake is highest among Black parents, at 78 percent
bought or considering, against 61 percent of Hispanic parents and 36 percent of
White parents. And future interest is highest in households earning under fifty
thousand dollars, at 65 percent, against 39 percent of the highest earners.

So the families most likely to bring one of these into the house are, on average,
the ones with the least slack to absorb it going wrong. That pattern is familiar
from every other technology I write about, and it is the reason I do not think
"just do not buy one" is an answer.

## What happened when somebody tested them

Common Sense Media's researchers tested three toys, Grem, Bondu and Miko 3, using
child accounts with stated ages from six to thirteen, talking to them out loud the
way a child would.

Their finding: **27 percent of outputs were inappropriate for children.** More than
a quarter. The report lists the categories, and they are not all the same thing:
mature topics, inappropriate boundaries, risky advice, unsafe roleplay.

Their recommendation is flat. They do not recommend AI toys for children aged five
and under at all, and they urge caution for six to twelve.

They also found something less dramatic and, if you have ever handed a device to a
small child, more recognisable. The toys are glitchy. They activate when nobody
meant them to and miss it when a child is actually talking to them.

I should name a disclosure the organisation makes about itself, because it cuts
both ways and leaving it out would be the kind of thing I complain about in other
people. Common Sense Media's institute says it is funded by both philanthropy and
industry, including the makers of some of the technologies it evaluates. You can
read that as a conflict or as unusual candour. Either way you should know it.

## The part that actually changed my mind

A second team, the PIRG Education Fund, published in March 2026, and their report
is not really about toys. It is about whether anybody is enforcing anything.

Last autumn they bought a scarf wearing teddy bear called Kumma, made by a
Singapore based company, FoloToy. It talked, over wifi, powered by a commercial
chatbot. Some of the conversations were what you would expect from a talking bear,
favourite school subjects, games to play. Others were not. The bear gave their
researchers step by step instructions for lighting a match, and produced explicit
sexual content.

The bear was marketed as running on OpenAI's GPT-4o, and ran on it by default.

Then the system worked, briefly and visibly. The day after PIRG published, FoloToy
suspended all sales of its products pending a safety audit. OpenAI said it had
banned the company from using its models.

Here is what PIRG found when they went back in February 2026, more than three
months after that ban was announced. Mentions of the old model had been scrubbed
from FoloToy's website. But a newer OpenAI model was still selectable in the
portal where you choose what powers the toy, and PIRG could still use their Kumma
apparently set to it. They sent OpenAI the documentation and asked whether the
company knew. OpenAI reiterated that FoloToy was banned.

Banned, and still working. Three months later.

PIRG tested another toy too, the Alilo Smart AI Bunny, and concluded that both it
and the Kumma had safeguards inadequate enough that a toy meant for young children
could be made to discuss explicit topics. They also report multiple apparent
breaches of Google's rule against using its models in children's products.

And on the 83 percent of parents worried about data collection: PIRG reports that
Bondu, one of the toys in the other team's test, exposed 50,000 lines of dialogue
that children had exchanged with it, sitting in an unprotected database.

## What I think this adds up to

Not that AI toys are a catastrophe. I am not going to tell you that, because the
evidence does not support it and because a spelling tutor and a bear that wants to
be your child's confidant are different products with the same label on the shelf.

What it adds up to is that the safety question has moved. For years the question
about children's products has been "is there a rule". For these, the rule existed.
A company published developer policies. A developer broke them. The developer was
publicly banned. And three months later the toy still worked.

So the real question is "who is checking", and the honest answer from both of these
reports is that nobody much is, and that the companies have decided developer
responsibility is a sufficient answer. One of the AI toy makers' own chief
executives would presumably say they are a small part of a large ecosystem. Common
Sense put it more sharply: most toys face rigorous safety testing before they reach
the market, and we still lack meaningful child safeguards for AI.

The other thing it adds up to is the gap I opened with. A toy that answers every
question, never gets tired and never says "not now" is teaching a child something
about what a relationship is. Parents can see that, which is why companionship came
last and why 84 percent of parents of under fives want nothing to do with it.

A seven year old cannot see it for themselves. That is not a failing in the seven
year old.

## What to do this week

If one of these is already in your house, or is about to arrive at Christmas, there
are two jobs and only one of them is about the toy.

**The job that is about the toy: find out what it keeps.** Not what it says, what
it keeps. Open the app or the web portal it came with and look for three things.
Whether there is a transcript of what your child has said to it, and whether you
can read it. Whether you can delete it. And which company's model is actually
behind it, which is often a dropdown in a portal and is the exact thing PIRG found
had not been cleaned up. Twenty minutes, once. If you cannot find out what it keeps,
you have learnt something.

**The job that is about the child, which matters more.** Four things a child should
be able to tell you, in their own words, about anything in the house that talks
back.

**Who is on the other side of this.** Not "a robot", something closer to: it is a
computer program made by a company, and there is nobody in there.

**What it remembers.** Children assume either nothing or everything. Both are
wrong, and the true answer is one you will have had to look up yourself, which is
why the first job comes first.

**What it cannot do.** It cannot be sad for them. It cannot keep a secret in any
way that means anything. It does not know if they are telling the truth.

**When to go and find a person.** This is the only one that counts if the others
fail.

A child who can say those four things back to you has something an age limit on a
box cannot give them, and something no enforcement regime currently delivers
either. We teach a short lesson on exactly this for younger children, and it starts
from the idea that the child should be able to explain it to you rather than
recite it.

And if the toy has not arrived yet: 22 percent. Companionship came last on the list
of things parents want. If you are buying, buy the one that teaches something.

## One last thought for the people buying presents

The age on the box is a manufacturing claim. It is not a finding about your child,
and after reading both of these reports I do not think it is a finding about the
toy either.

Nobody has told most parents what to look for. That was the British Standards
Institution's own conclusion from its UK survey, and it is the most useful sentence
in any of this.

So the four questions are not a precaution. For now they are the guidance.
