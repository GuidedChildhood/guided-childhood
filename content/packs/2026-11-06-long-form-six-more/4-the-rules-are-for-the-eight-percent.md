# Issue: The rules are being written for the eight percent

**Venue: Substack.** From GDC-051. Evidence: weaker than the other issues in this
batch and the text says so. eSafety's own PDF has refused our tools four times
across three sessions.

## Subject lines

1. The rules are being written for the eight percent
2. Four in five children use the thing nobody is regulating
3. Homework help, and where it goes next

**Preview line:** Australia asked 1,950 children about AI. Almost all of them use
an assistant. Almost none use a companion. Nearly every law being drafted is about
the companion.

---

Australia's online safety regulator asked 1,950 children aged ten to seventeen
about artificial intelligence. Seventy eight percent had used an AI assistant.
Eight percent had used an AI companion.

Now count the laws.

New York has passed one limiting what a chatbot may do with a minor. California's
companion chatbot law took effect at the start of this year. Idaho, Oregon and
Washington have acted on chatbots claiming to be conscious and on sexual
conversations with minors. Georgia has restricted relationship simulation features
for under eighteens. A federal bill in the United States would bar minors from AI
companions altogether and has cleared the Senate Judiciary Committee.

Nearly all of it is aimed at the companion. The eight percent.

And the tool that four children in five are actually using is the ordinary
assistant, the one most parents have filed under homework help.

## Before I go further, what I can and cannot stand behind

I am going to be unusually blunt about the evidence in this issue, because it is
the weakest in this series and you should know that before you read the argument.

eSafety's report is called "Talking to machines", it was released on 3 August 2026,
and I have not read it. Its site has refused the tools I use four times now, across
three separate attempts over two weeks. So have the mirrors.

What that means in practice:

- The headline figures, the 78 percent and the 8 percent, are confirmed through
  eSafety's own published summary and media release and are consistent everywhere I
  find them. I am comfortable with those.
- The figure I would most like to use, that 54 percent of children who had used an
  assistant or a companion said they used it for personal or social reasons, I am
  giving you with a warning rather than confidence. **I have never seen the question
  the children were asked.** "Personal or social" is a wide bucket. It could be a
  chat about a game as easily as a worry. Until somebody reads me the question, treat
  that number as a direction and not a measurement.
- Figures I have seen reported but will not build an argument on: that about one in
  five children who used either tool reported a potentially inappropriate or harmful
  interaction, and that around one in three shared personal or sensitive
  information. Both come to me through summaries rather than the document. They are
  almost certainly roughly right. I just will not lean on a number I have not read.

These are also Australian children, not British ones, and I would not carry the
percentages across.

What does carry across is the shape. And the shape is the whole point.

## Two countries, two surveys, the same shape

Set the Australian figures beside an American one.

The Pew Research Center surveyed 1,458 American teenagers aged thirteen to
seventeen in the autumn of 2025. Fifty four percent had used a chatbot for help
with schoolwork. Twelve percent had asked one for emotional support or advice.

Different country, different question, different year. Same shape. Overwhelming use
of the general purpose tool, a much smaller slice using it for something personal,
and almost nobody describing a dedicated companion app as their main experience.

Two independent samples landing on the same structure is worth more than either on
its own, and it tells you something that neither headline does.

The exposure is not concentrated in a product category. It is spread across a tool
that looks harmless.

## Why that matters more than it sounds

If you are writing a rule, a product category is a wonderful thing to have. It has
a name. You can define it, require an age check on it, and tell a parent to look
for it. Everything about it is enforceable.

A general purpose assistant is the opposite. It is on the phone already. It is on
the laptop the school handed out. It is inside the search box, the word processor
and increasingly the operating system. There is no aisle it sits in.

So a rule about companion apps is not wrong, and I want to be fair about this. The
worst documented cases, the ones that have driven the legislation, involve
companion products designed to hold a young person's attention and simulate a
relationship. Those products deserve the scrutiny. A law that makes them harder for
a fourteen year old to reach is a reasonable thing for a legislature to do.

It just will not touch the thing most children are doing.

And here is the part I find genuinely difficult. A general purpose assistant is
also the one with the better guardrails, the bigger safety team and the stronger
commercial reason not to produce a disaster. The companion app with nobody checking
it is both more dangerous per child and used by far fewer of them.

Both things being true at once is why this is hard, and why I distrust anybody who
finds it easy.

## The part of this no ban can reach

There is one more reason the companion category is the easy target and the
assistant is the hard one, and it is the reason I think almost nothing being
drafted will touch the actual exposure.

A lot of children meet their first AI assistant at school.

Not through a product their parents bought or a app they downloaded, but on a
device the school issued, inside software the school chose, with an assistant built
into the word processor or the learning platform or the search box. In that setting
the use is not only permitted, it is instructed. The child is being taught to use
it, graded on work produced alongside it, and told by an adult that this is the
right tool for the job.

Every protective instinct we have about children and technology assumes a parent
can decide. Buy it or do not. Allow the app or do not. Set the limit or do not.

None of that applies to a tool that arrives with the homework.

And it means the conversation a child has with an assistant at four in the
afternoon, about a piece of coursework, on a school laptop, sits entirely outside
the reach of an age restriction, a parental control and a family rule. It is also
the single most common way children in this country are currently encountering
these systems.

I am not saying schools should not use them. The better ones are being thoughtful
about it, and a child who learns to use a powerful tool well under supervision is
in a better position than one who meets it alone at thirteen. I am saying that if
you are a parent trying to work out where to put your attention, the answer is not
the thing with an age rating. It is the thing with a login your child was given.

## The finding I keep returning to

Of the children who had used either tool, the Australian survey reports that
somewhere around half had taken it somewhere personal or social.

I have told you why I hold that number loosely. But even at half the size it would
still say something, and it fits what the American data shows at a lower number for
a narrower question.

A meaningful minority of children are not using these things as a calculator. They
are using them as somebody to talk to. And the one they are talking to is mostly
not the product built to be talked to. It is the homework tool.

There are two readings of that and I hold both on different days.

The first is that a child typing a worry into a machine late at night is a child
who did not have, or did not trust, a person to tell. On that reading the machine is
a symptom, and one that is built to keep a conversation going.

The second is that for some young people, saying a thing out loud to something with
no face is a way of practising. Rehearsing the sentence before they say it to a
person. That is not nothing, and I have watched an adult do exactly that.

No survey can tell you which. Nobody has followed these children long enough to
find out.

What I think it comes down to is not whether the tool is safe. It is whether the
conversation ends with a person, or ends with another conversation.

## The finding nobody is legislating

One more thing from the Australian work, reported in its summary rather than read
by me, and it is the most actionable item in this entire issue.

Very few children knew how to report a problem. And parents' awareness dropped
sharply for companions compared with assistants.

Think about what that means next to everything above. The regulatory energy is
going into keeping children away from one category. Meanwhile the children who are
already in the other category, in enormous numbers, mostly cannot tell you what to
do when something goes wrong inside it.

Engagement with companions was also reported as higher among children with a
disability or a diagnosed mental health condition. If that holds, the children most
likely to form an attachment to one are the children already carrying something,
which is the pattern that runs through every topic I write about and the reason I
keep saying the average child is not the story.

## What to do this week

Two things, and the second one takes ten minutes and almost nobody has done it.

**First, find out which one it is.** Not whether your child uses AI, they almost
certainly do. Which kind. The question that gets you a real answer is not "do you
use ChatGPT", it is "what did you last ask it". A homework answer and a question
about a friendship are different houses to be living in, and you cannot tell from
the outside.

Ask it in the car, or while doing something else, and then do not react to the
first answer. The first answer is a test.

**Second, find the report button together.** This is the one.

Open the assistant your child actually uses. Not a companion app, the real one, the
one on their phone or their school laptop. Then the two of you find out, together,
what happens when something it says is wrong or frightening. Is there a thumbs
down. Does it go anywhere. Is there a way to flag a conversation. Can you see the
history, and can you delete it.

You are not doing this to install a safeguard. You are doing it so that the answer
to "what do I do if this gets weird" is something your child has already done once,
with you, on an ordinary afternoon, rather than something they have to work out
alone at eleven at night.

If you cannot find a route, that is worth knowing too, and worth saying out loud to
your child: this thing you talk to has no good way for you to tell anybody when it
goes wrong, so I am the route.

That sentence is the whole lesson, and it does not depend on which country's survey
you believe.

## Where I have landed

A rule written for companion apps misses the tool nearly four children in five
actually use. That is not an argument against the rule. It is an argument against
mistaking the rule for the answer.

And the habit that carries across every country, every product category and every
version of the law is the same one: knowing who is on the other side, and knowing
when to stop talking to a machine and go and find a person.

We teach a short lesson on exactly that for younger children, and it starts from
the idea that the child should be able to explain it to you. If you want a starting
point for where your own child is, our stage check is three questions and returns a
stage with the things worth practising next. A starting point, not a measurement.
We hold no outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently
afterwards.

with AI assistants and companions"**, released 3 August 2026. An online survey of

  both of all children surveyed. Confirmed in multiple independent places and used

- **54 percent used it for personal or social reasons**, among children who had

**Pew Research Center**, 1,458 American teenagers aged 13 to 17, surveyed in autumn
