# Issue: The rule parents set least is the one they worry about most

**Venue: Substack.** From GDC-039. Evidence: Ofcom's report, 13 October 2026.
ofcom.org.uk refuses our tools, so the figures come through consistent reporting
and the text says so.

## Subject lines

1. The rule parents set least is the one they worry about most
2. I set a timer because it was the rule I could actually enforce
3. Two children, both an hour, nothing else the same

**Preview line:** Sixty four percent of parents of gaming children worry about
strangers. Forty three percent set a rule about who their child plays with. Fifty
six percent set a time limit. That ordering is the whole problem, and I did it too.

---

I set a timer because it was the rule I could actually enforce.

That is the honest reason, and it took me a while to admit it. A time limit is
checkable. I could look at a clock and know whether I had done my job. Who my child
was talking to was a much harder question, so I asked it less often.

Ofcom published a report on children and gaming last month, and the numbers have a
version of that in them. Not a version of my failing. A version of everybody's.

## The inversion

Among parents of three to seventeen year olds whose children game online, here is
what they say worries them, in order:

- Contact with strangers through games or chat: **64 percent**
- Bullying: **60 percent**
- Inappropriate content: **59 percent**
- Pressure to make in game purchases: **51 percent**

And here is what they say they actually do, in order:

- Rules about time limits: **56 percent**
- Rules about when their child can play: **51 percent**
- Rules about appropriate content: **50 percent**
- Rules about who their child can play with: **43 percent**
- No rules at all: **9 percent**

Read those two lists against each other.

The thing parents name as their top worry is the thing they are least likely to
make a rule about. And the rule they are most likely to set, a time limit, does not
appear in their stated worries at all. Nobody in that survey said their main fear
was that their child was gaming for too long. They said their main fear was who was
in there with them.

I am not going to turn that into a neat percentage point gap, because those figures
come from different questions put to different groups and the arithmetic would be
dishonest. The ordering is the finding. And the ordering is clear.

## Why we do it, which is not stupidity

There is a reason, and it is not that parents are foolish or lazy. It is that the
rules differ in one respect that has nothing to do with how much they matter.

A time limit is **verifiable by the person setting it.** You can see a clock. The
console reports hours. You know, at the end of the week, whether the rule held.

A rule about who your child plays with is not verifiable in that way, by anybody.
You cannot watch. The names are handles. The voices are in a headset. Even with
every parental control switched on, enforcement depends almost entirely on your
child telling you things.

So we are not choosing the rule that matches the risk. We are choosing the rule
that gives us feedback. Given a choice between a task you can complete and a task
you can only attempt, a tired adult at nine at night picks the one with an end.

I did. For months, and I would have told you at the time that I was being
responsible, because I was doing the thing I could check.

## What the hours cannot tell you

Two children, both an hour.

One is building something with a school friend whose mum you have met at the gate.
They are on a headset, laughing, and will do the same thing tomorrow.

The other is talking to someone they met inside the game three weeks ago, who has
been generous, who remembers details, and who has started suggesting they carry the
conversation on somewhere else, somewhere more private, just the two of them.

Same hour. Same console. Same number in the weekly report. Nothing else the same.

A timer cannot distinguish those two children. It was never built to. And the
numbers say that more than half of children who game online, **55 percent**, play
with people they have never met offline at least sometimes, with **20 percent**
doing so most or all of the time.

A fifth of gaming children, routinely playing with people they do not know
offline. That is not a fringe scenario to plan for. That is Tuesday.

## In defence of the timer, briefly

I have been hard on the clock, so let me give it its due, because there is one
thing a time limit does that nothing in my four questions does.

It protects sleep.

A time limit that ends an hour before bed is not really a rule about gaming. It is
a rule about a child being asleep, and sleep is one of the few things in this whole
field where the evidence is not contested and the mechanism is not mysterious. A
tired child is worse at everything, including judging who to trust in a game. The
Bradford trial that is testing social media limits right now has sleep as one of
the main roads it thinks any effect travels down, and its curfew runs from nine at
night.

So if your time limit is actually a bedtime wearing a different hat, keep it. It is
doing real work.

What it is not doing is the thing my 64 percent of parents are worried about. Those
are two different jobs and they need two different rules, and the mistake is not
having a timer. The mistake is thinking the timer covers both.

## The nine percent, and what I do not read into it

One figure I want to handle carefully, because it is the one most likely to be
turned into a stick.

Nine percent of parents said they set no gaming rules at all.

The easy reading is nine percent of parents are not bothering. I do not think that
reading survives contact with how these households actually work. Some of those
children are sixteen and seventeen, where no rules is a reasonable position and
arguably the right one. Some of those parents are doing shift work, or holding a
house together alone, or managing a child whose needs make a rule about a console
the least of it.

And some of them have a child who games with their cousins and their mates and
nothing has ever gone wrong, and so the subject has never come up.

The group I find more interesting is not the nine percent who set nothing. It is
the much larger group who set something, just not a rule about who. Most parents in
that survey set at least one rule and most of them did not set that one. Those are
engaged parents, paying attention, doing the work, and landing on the rule with the
scoreboard. That is not negligence. That is a design problem, and the design is
ours to fix rather than theirs.

## The part that costs me something to write

Here is where I have to be fair, because the argument I have just made can be
turned into something I do not believe.

Somebody your child met online is not automatically a threat.

Ofcom's own figures say **96 percent** of children who game online play with
friends or family at least sometimes, and its report describes gaming as a real way
for children to be social and to make things. Most of what happens in there is
children doing what children have always done, in a place adults find harder to
see.

My own son's gaming was mostly that. The friendships were real. Some of them were
with children he also saw at school and some were not, and I could not always tell
you which, and in most cases it did not matter.

So I am not arguing for fewer online friends. I am arguing that a timer cannot tell
a child who is trustworthy, cannot explain why somebody wants secrecy, and cannot
notice that a conversation has quietly changed shape under them. Those are the
things that decide how the hour goes, and no clock reaches any of them.

And none of it is a parent's job alone. The platforms carry a legal duty here under
UK law, and Ofcom's own gaming lead has said the buck stops with them. I believe
that, and I want it enforced. It is also true that enforcement arrives on a
regulator's timetable and your child is playing tonight.

## What to do this week

Ofcom's own advice is three words, check, chat and change, and I think it is good
advice in the wrong order for most families. Here is what I would do.

**Tonight, change one setting.** Not all of them. One. Open the game your child
actually plays most and find the setting for who is allowed to contact them. In
most games it is under privacy or social, and in most houses nobody has ever looked
at it. Set it to friends only, with your child in the room, so it is a thing you
did together rather than a thing they discover later and resent.

Twenty minutes. Then stop, because the settings are the small half of this.

**Then, over the week, four questions.** These are the ones a timer cannot answer,
and you do not ask them as a list. You ask one, at a time when nothing is wrong,
and then you let it go.

**Who are you playing with.** Asked the way you would ask who they sat with at
lunch. Names, not categories.

**How do you know them.** This is the one that does the work. The answer sorts the
whole thing into school friend, friend of a school friend, and somebody from
inside the game. Your child almost certainly knows which is which. Most parents
have never asked.

**Can they reach you outside the game.** Any route off the platform is the thing
worth knowing about, because it is the step that matters in almost every account of
this going wrong. Not because off platform means danger, but because moving
somewhere private is the move.

**What would make you leave and come and tell me.** Ask this one last and ask it
about them rather than about strangers. You are not warning them. You are finding
out whether they have a trigger at all, and whether the route back to you is one
they would actually use.

That fourth question is the real test, and it is not about the child. It depends
entirely on whether coming to you went well enough last time that they would do it
again. If the last time your child told you something about a game ended with the
console being taken away, you have already taught them the answer.

### Which of the four has your family actually had?

Most families, mine included for a long time, have had the first one and none of
the others. That is not a failure of love. It is what happens when the only rule
with a scoreboard is the clock.

If you want a starting point for where your own child is, our stage check is three
questions and returns a stage with the things worth practising next. It is a
starting point and not a measurement, and we hold no outcome evidence that a child
who finishes a stage behaves differently afterwards.

## The thing I would tell myself four years ago

The timer was not wrong. Sleep matters, and a limit on a weeknight is a reasonable
thing for a parent to want.

It was just not the rule I thought it was. I believed I was managing a risk, and I
was managing a number, and the number and the risk were different objects that
happened to live in the same room.

The parents in that survey know exactly what they are afraid of. Sixty four percent
of them named it. They are just setting the rule that answers back.
