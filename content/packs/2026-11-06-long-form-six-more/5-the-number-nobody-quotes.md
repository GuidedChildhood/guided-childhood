A study everyone was quoting a few weeks ago said that 85 percent of Australian
teenagers got round the under sixteen social media ban.

It doesn't say that.

What it says is that more than 85 percent of under sixteens were still using the
platforms three months after the ban began, mostly on accounts they already had.

Still using is not the same as broke in. One describes a door being forced. The
other describes a door that was never shut behind the people already inside.

That distinction is not pedantry, and I am going to spend this issue on it, because
the way this one paper has been used tells you almost everything about how the
argument over children and phones is now conducted. Both sides are quoting it.
Both sides are quoting it for things it does not say. And the single number in this
whole story that actually measures the effect of the ban is being quoted by
neither.

## What the paper is, and what it found

The study is in the BMJ, from a team at the University of Newcastle in Australia,
published on 24 June. It recruited 436 adolescents aged 12 to 17, most of them in
New South Wales, and followed up with 408 of them three months after the ban came
into force.

Its finding on continued use is the 85 percent figure, and the crucial detail is
the mechanism: most of those still using were on accounts that already existed
before the ban. The platforms did not let them back in. The platforms never
removed them.

The paper also says, in its own terms, that there is insufficient evidence of a
real drop in use. Which is not the same as saying nothing changed, and I want to
hold that distinction, because I am about to be hard on people who lose it.

One Australian state, mostly. Self reported answers. Four hundred and eight young
people, three months in.

A good, careful, small study of the first three months of a policy that will take
years to assess, which has been handed a job it cannot do.

## The number that measures something

Now the regulator's own follow up, which surveyed 803 children.

The share of children who actually held an account on an age restricted platform
fell from **52.4 percent to 42.1 percent.** eSafety describes that change as modest
and statistically significant.

Sit with that for a second, because it is the only figure in this story that
answers the question people think they are arguing about.

Did the ban change anything measurable? Yes. About ten percentage points of account
holding, which is roughly one in five of the children who had an account losing it.
Modest, in the regulator's own word. Real, by its own statistics.

And it is missing from both arguments. The people who want the ban do not quote it
because modest is not the word they are looking for. The people who oppose the ban
do not quote it because it is not nothing.

I find that genuinely clarifying. When a number is inconvenient to everybody, it is
usually the one worth knowing.

The same regulator's work also found the thing that should embarrass every platform
in this story: around half the children who still had accounts said the platform
had never checked their age, and 37 percent had simply entered an age of sixteen or
over. The ban did not fail because children are cunning. It is leaking because
nobody was checking.

## The design flaw hiding inside the 85 percent

Go back to the detail everybody skips. Most of the children still using were on
accounts **they already had**.

That is not children defeating a system. That is a system that was never pointed at
them.

A ban can be built two ways. You can stop new accounts being created by people
under the age, which is a gate on the way in. Or you can also require platforms to
find and remove the under age accounts already on their books, which is a sweep of
the people inside. The first is cheap, easy to announce and politically
straightforward. The second is expensive, requires a platform to admit roughly how
many under age users it has been carrying, and guarantees a wave of angry
fourteen year olds and their parents.

If you gate the entrance and do not sweep the building, then on day one of your ban
almost every child you were worried about is already inside, holding a working
account, and your headline circumvention figure will be enormous within three
months. Not because enforcement failed. Because enforcement was never asked to do
that job.

Which is exactly what the regulator's other finding suggests was happening anyway:
around half the children who still had accounts said the platform had never
checked their age at all.

So the 85 percent is not really a measure of whether bans work. It is a measure of
how this particular ban was built, and the answer to "did children get round it" is
mostly that they did not have to.

This matters for Britain specifically, because our regulations have not been laid
yet. The thing worth watching when they appear is not the headline age. It is
whether they say anything about existing accounts, and who has to go looking for
them. If they do not, you can write the three month story now.

## How the same paper proves two opposite things

Here is the part I did not expect when I started looking into this.

Jonathan Haidt made the loudest case of the decade out of alarm. A generation
rewired. An epidemic with a start date. A conclusion that keeps arriving ahead of
the effect sizes that would carry it. I have written about that at length and I
stand by it.

What I had not noticed is that the people who spend their days correcting him have
started doing the same thing with the same material. A Psychology Today piece
published on 30 June cited this BMJ paper as proof that the ban had failed.

The paper does not say the ban failed. It says there is not enough evidence yet,
three months in, on four hundred and eight young people in one state.

So you now have two camps, pointing at one small three month study of one
Australian state, each announcing that it settles a question the study explicitly
declines to settle.

One side is frightened of the phones. The other is frightened of the ban. Both
reach past what the study will carry, because a careful answer does not move
anybody.

And I want to be honest about my own position in that, because I am not a neutral
observer. I build a product that exists because I think a deadline is not a plan.
That gives me a reason to want the ban to look insufficient. Knowing that is the
only protection I have against it, and the reason I keep writing the regulator's
inconvenient ten points down rather than quietly leaving them out.

## The asymmetry that makes this worse

There is a structural reason the argument gets conducted this way, and it is worth
naming because it will happen to Britain next.

A ban produces its bad news immediately and its good news slowly.

Circumvention is visible within weeks. A journalist can find a fourteen year old
with an account in an afternoon. "They are all still on it" is available as a story
almost at once, and it is true.

Whatever benefit a protected period might deliver, if it delivers one, shows up in
mental health data years later, confounded by everything else that happened in
those years, and will be contested when it arrives.

So the first two years of any ban will be dominated by evidence of failure,
regardless of whether it works. Everyone who wants it to fail will have material.
Everyone who wants it to succeed will have to say "wait", which is the losing move
in public argument.

Britain's version lands in spring 2027, with the Culture Secretary having named
March and no exact date officially confirmed. The regulations that would fix it were
due before the end of this year and had not been laid when I wrote this.

You can therefore predict the next eighteen months of British commentary with some
confidence. Three months after the checks start, somebody will find the teenagers
who are still on it, and that will be reported as the ban failing. It will not be
evidence of the ban failing. It will be evidence that bans leak, which we already
know.

## The test I now apply, and would ask you to apply

When somebody hands you a claim about the ban, three questions. They take about
thirty seconds and they catch almost everything.

**What did it measure, use or accounts?** These are completely different things.
Children still using a platform tells you about doors left open. Children holding
an account tells you about enforcement. Time spent tells you about behaviour. Most
coverage swaps them mid sentence.

**Measured when, and for how long?** Three months into a policy is a measure of
implementation, not of effect. Anybody citing a three month figure as proof about
children's mental health is telling you about themselves.

**Does the finding point where the person quoting it is pointing?** This is the one
that catches the most. The BMJ paper is being cited by one camp for a conclusion its
authors refuse. Go and read what the authors say they found. It is usually in the
abstract, and it is usually more modest than the tweet.

## What to do this week

This one is not about your child. It is about you, and it takes ten minutes.

**Write down now what would change your mind about the ban.**

Actually write it. On paper, or in a note on your phone, with today's date on it.
Two sentences is plenty.

One sentence for each direction. What result, arriving in two or three years, would
persuade you the ban was worth having? And what result would persuade you it was a
distraction?

Be specific enough that you would recognise it. "Children are happier" is not an
answer. "A drop in anxiety that survives when you account for the children who were
already struggling" is.

Here is why this is the most useful thing in this issue. In two years the first
real results will arrive, and they will arrive inside a shouting match, framed by
whoever got there first. If you have never written down what would convince you, you
will do what almost everybody does, which is find the framing that fits what you
already thought and feel informed.

If you have written it down, you get to check.

I have done mine and I will tell you one half of it, so this is not a free
instruction. The thing that would most move me towards thinking the ban was worth
it is not a mental health number at all. It is evidence that the protected period
got used: that in the years the door was shut, children arrived at sixteen more able
to say what a feed was doing to them than the cohort before them. If the ban buys
time and nobody uses the time, I do not expect the numbers to move, and I would not
blame the ban for that.

That is a falsifiable thing to believe and I would rather be caught being wrong
about it than be vague.

## Where this leaves a parent

The ban will do whatever it does. Nobody writing about it today knows which, and
the most honest people in the field are the ones saying so rather than the ones
with a verdict three months in.

What happens in your house between now and the day it lands is the part nobody is
legislating, and it is the part that is entirely yours.

Our stage check is three questions and gives you a stage with the things worth
practising next. It is a starting point rather than a measurement, and we hold no
outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently afterwards.
I would rather say that plainly than sell you the thing I have just spent two
thousand words warning you about.
