# Issue: Two hours a day, and a nine o'clock curfew

**Venue: Substack.** From GDC-038. Evidence: the trial's own protocol, v1.3, 12
August 2026, read in full.

## Subject lines

1. Four thousand Bradford children, two hours a day, and a nine o'clock curfew
2. The trial that is running right now, while the rest of us argue
3. What a randomised trial can settle, and what it cannot

**Preview line:** The protocol is public. Here is what it actually measures, what
the children themselves asked for, and the question it was never built to answer.

---

While we have been arguing about whether to limit children's social media, somebody
has been quietly running the experiment.

The first wave of it finished about a week ago.

It is called the IRL Trial, it is based in Bradford, and its protocol is published.
I have read it, all of it, and it is the most interesting document I have come
across this year. Not because it settles the argument. Because of how carefully it
refuses to pretend it can.

Here is what it does.

School year groups, not individual children, are randomly assigned to one of two
groups. Both groups install the same app on their own phone. For one group the app
limits social media to two hours a day and blocks it between nine at night and
seven in the morning. For the other group the identical app only counts the hours
and never stops anything. Six weeks. Then everybody answers the same questionnaire
they answered at the start.

That design is the whole point, and it is worth slowing down on, because it is the
thing almost no study in this field has.

## Why this one is different from the studies we usually argue about

Nearly every number you have ever read about children and screens, including most
of the ones I have written about, comes from asking a lot of children two questions
at the same time and noticing that the answers move together. Children who use more
social media report more anxiety. That is a real finding and it is reported
honestly. It just cannot tell you which way round it runs.

It cannot tell you whether the scrolling made the child anxious, or the anxious
child reached for the scrolling, or whether something else entirely, a hard year at
home, a friendship that collapsed, a family under financial strain, produced both.

The trial's own protocol says this out loud, and I want to quote the sentence
because of who is saying it. These are researchers who have staked years on finding
an effect, writing in their own funding document:

> the current observational evidence has several methodological limitations,
> including likely residual confounding and reverse causality, meaning the true
> effect of social media use on adolescent mental health is uncertain.

And then the line after it:

> Scientific authorities in the UK and US have concluded that it is currently not
> possible to estimate the overall causal effect of digital technology on the
> wellbeing of children.

They are citing the National Academies consensus report and the UK Chief Medical
Officers' own commentary. Which means the people running the biggest UK trial of
social media limits began by writing down that nobody currently knows the answer.

That is not a weakness in their document. It is the reason the document exists.

A randomised trial fixes the direction problem by brute force. If you decide at
random which year groups get the limit, then the only systematic difference between
the two groups is the limit. Anything that shows up afterwards has an arrow pointing
one way.

## What it is actually measuring, which is narrower than the headlines will say

The primary outcome is anxiety. One thing. Measured with the anxiety subscale of a
questionnaire called the RCADS-25: fifteen questions, each answered on a four point
scale, producing a score from zero to forty five.

Depression and general wellbeing are secondary. Then there is a set of what the
protocol calls mechanisms, which is the part I find most interesting: social media
use itself, sleep, time spent with friends and family, and bullying. Those are the
roads by which an effect, if there is one, would have to travel.

So when the result lands, the honest question is not "did it work". It is: did
anxiety move, and if it did, did sleep or time with friends move with it in a way
that explains how.

Two more numbers from the protocol that tell you how to read whatever comes out.

The trial is powered to detect a standardised effect of **0.16**. That is the
smallest effect it can reliably see. If the true effect is smaller than that, this
trial will probably come back with nothing, and nothing will not mean zero. It will
mean smaller than this trial could catch. I would like everybody who is going to
write about the result to hold on to that sentence.

And the baseline anxiety score they are expecting, taken from Bradford data
collected in 2023 and 2024, is **9.9 out of 45**.

## The children designed the intervention, and that changed it

This is the part of the protocol I did not expect.

The team consulted young people three times. In July 2025 they put seven options in
front of 16 to 24 year olds, including phone abstinence and parental restrictions.
The preference was clear: a restriction rather than a ban, delivered by an app the
young person controlled. In December 2025 they asked 12 to 16 year olds about
engagement, and got a strong preference for streaks, peer competition and
notifications. In January 2026 another group said they would prefer an overnight
restriction from nine until seven.

Every one of those preferences is in the final design. The two hour budget rather
than a ban. The nine until seven curfew. The streaks and the leaderboards.

And one more thing, which I think is the most honest decision in the whole
document. **Participants can override the restrictions.** They can push past the
budget, and they can get in during the curfew. The app records it.

A lot of people will read that as a flaw. I read it as the researchers accepting
that a limit a child cannot escape is not a limit, it is a confiscation, and that
measuring what children do with a limit they can escape is the more useful
question anyway.

They also left Year 7 out on purpose, and the reason is quietly damning. Under
data protection law an eleven or twelve year old is not allowed a social media
account at all, because the minimum age is thirteen. The team decided it would be
uncomfortable for parents and teachers to enrol children in a social media study
at an age when social media is not legally available to them.

Which would be fine, except that Bradford's own data says **86 percent of ten to
twelve year olds already have a profile**. The age rule has already failed for
that group. The trial just cannot be the thing that says so.

## The number I keep coming back to

While looking into this I found the companion paper, published in May 2026 by most
of the same team, and it contains the most concrete estimate I have seen anybody
put on this question.

They surveyed 8,466 Bradford students aged 12 to 15, weighted to the city. The
median time on social media apps was **3.36 hours a day**. Then they modelled what
would happen if you capped it at three hours.

Their answer, and I want to give it to you exactly as they frame it: if the
association were causal, capping daily use at three hours would reduce the share of
children at clinical threshold for anxiety and depression symptoms from 10.7
percent to 9.5 percent. Which they then translate into the clearest sentence in
this entire field.

**Thirteen fewer cases in a typical school of a thousand pupils.**

Hold that up against how this argument gets conducted. It is not nothing. Thirteen
children in a school of a thousand is thirteen real children, and if your child is
one of them you do not care what the aggregate looks like. It is also not a
generation being rewired.

And the authors put the condition in the sentence themselves: "we cannot
demonstrate strong evidence of a causal relationship". That is what the trial now
running is for.

## One study suggests the curfew is the stronger half

Here is something I did not expect to find while reading around this, and it
makes me more interested in one half of the Bradford design than the other.

A team at King's College London surveyed 1,043 of their own students about phone
use and sleep. Published in 2021, data collected in early 2019. They measured
three things: how long people were on the phone, whether they scored as addicted
on a standard scale, and when they last used it at night.

Then they asked which of those predicted poor sleep.

**Daily hours did almost nothing.** Against a baseline of three hours a day, four
hours came out at 1.01, five hours at 0.73, more than five hours at 1.10. None of
those is statistically significant. Spending more than five hours a day on a phone
was not associated with worse sleep than spending three.

**The time of night did a great deal.** Last using the phone at one in the morning
or later, rather than before eleven, came out at 3.91. Nearly four times, and it
is the largest association in the paper by a distance.

Now the caveats, and they are serious enough that I nearly left this out.

**There are no children in this study.** The youngest person in it is eighteen.
They are university students at one London university, 73 percent of them women,
recruited by being approached on campus. The authors say plainly that their sample
is not representative of UK young adults, let alone of anyone's thirteen year old.

It is cross sectional, so it cannot tell you which came first. The authors say so:
no causal relationships can be drawn, and they cannot rule out reverse causality,
which here means a young person sleeping badly and reaching for the phone at one
in the morning because they are already awake. That reading fits the data exactly
as well.

And one more, which matters for the comparison I just made. The late night figure
is **not** adjusted for daily hours. The authors say the two could not go in the
same model together, so I cannot tell you that timing beats duration in a single
fair test. What I can tell you is that in the model where hours were tested, hours
did not predict poor sleep, and in a separate model, timing did, strongly.

I should also say that the paper's own discussion goes further than its design
allows, calling this a deleterious impact on sleep, which is a causal verb from a
study its own limitations section says cannot establish cause. That is the same
fault I spend most of my time pointing at. It does not make the numbers wrong. It
means read the tables and not the summary.

So I am holding this loosely. But it is a UK dataset, it points the same way as
the Manchester work on children, and it points at the curfew rather than the cap.

Bradford is testing both at once.

## What it cannot tell you, and this is the part that matters at home

Six weeks of a two hour cap will tell us whether anxiety scores shift.

It will not tell you what a child does with the time. It will not tell you whether
they moved to a different screen, which is in the protocol as a thing to watch and
is the outcome I would bet on. It will not tell you anything about a nine year old,
or about your child specifically, or about what happens in month seven.

And it cannot tell you the one thing I think actually decides how this goes for a
family: whether the child understands why the limit exists.

A limit changes access. It does not teach judgement. If a child gets six quieter
weeks and nothing happens in the quiet, they come out of it with the same instincts
and a worse temper. If something does happen in the quiet, the six weeks were worth
having whatever the anxiety score did.

Nobody can randomise that, which is exactly why it keeps falling out of the
argument.

## What to do this week

Borrow the design, not the result. The result is months away and may be modest.

Run the two hour budget and the nine until seven curfew in your own house for six
weeks, with three conditions taken straight from the protocol.

**One: the child can override it, and you both know they can.** Build the override
in deliberately. A limit your child has to break to escape teaches them to hide. A
limit they can lift, and tell you they lifted, teaches them to notice. The trial
made this choice on purpose and so should you.

**Two: measure one thing, at the start and at the end.** Not a feeling, something
you can write down. What time they actually fell asleep. Or how many times that
week they saw a friend in person. The trial picked sleep and time with friends and
family as its mechanisms for a reason: they are the roads anything else would
travel down. Pick one, write it on the back of an envelope now, look at it in six
weeks.

**Three: say out loud which of you chose this.** The young people in the
consultation did not ask for a ban. They asked for a restriction they controlled.
That preference is worth taking seriously in your own kitchen, because a rule a
teenager helped set is a different object from one that arrived.

Then at the end of the six weeks, ask the question the trial cannot: can they tell
you, in their own words, what changed. That answer is worth more to you than the
anxiety subscale, because it is about your child and the subscale is about four
thousand of them.

If you want a starting point for where your own child is before you begin, our
stage check is three questions and gives you a stage with the things worth
practising next. It is a starting point, not a measurement, and I will not dress it
up as more: we hold no evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves
differently afterwards.

## One last thing, about the waiting

The regulations that will restrict under sixteens in Britain are expected in spring
2027, with the Culture Secretary having named March, and no exact date officially
confirmed.

The trial that is testing the thing being legislated is running right now. Its
second wave finishes before Christmas. Nobody has published when it reports.

So we are going to legislate first and find out afterwards. I do not think that is
a scandal, because governments cannot sit still waiting for perfect evidence about
a risk they believe is real, and the trial team says as much themselves.

But it does mean something for you. The law will arrive on a date somebody chose.
The evidence will arrive on a date nobody has announced. What happens in your house
between now and then is not waiting on either of them.

## Sources, and what each one can carry

**The IRL Trial protocol, version 1.3, 12 August 2026**, published by Born in
Bradford. Read in full for this issue. Everything about the design comes from here:
the cluster randomisation of year groups, the two hour daily budget, the nine
until seven curfew, the six week period, the override, the anxiety primary outcome
measured on the RCADS-25 anxiety subscale, the secondary outcomes, the mechanisms,
the 0.16 powered effect size, the baseline score of 9.9 out of 45, the exclusion of
Year 7 and its reason, the three young people's consultations and their dates, and
the two quoted sentences about observational evidence and about what scientific
authorities have concluded.

**The trial's governance**, from the same protocol. Co principal investigators Dr
Dan Lewer at Bradford Teaching Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust and Professor Amy
Orben at the MRC Cognition and Brain Sciences Unit, Cambridge. Sponsor: Bradford
Teaching Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust. **Funded by both the Wellcome Trust and
the National Institute for Health Research**, which I had previously described as
Wellcome alone, and that was incomplete. A partnership with the University of
Cambridge and the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine Clinical Trials
Unit, which runs the primary analysis.

**Scale.** Born in Bradford's public page says 4,000 students in years 8 to 10
across secondary schools in the city. The protocol is more precise and slightly
larger: to detect its target effect the trial needs 30 year groups, about 10
schools and roughly 4,500 participants, and it aims to recruit 12 schools to allow
for one or two dropping out. Where the two differ I have used the protocol.

**Timeline.** The protocol gives two waves: 1 September 2026 to the autumn half
term, and after half term finishing before Christmas 2026. **No published reporting
date for the results exists.** I have now looked for one across three sessions and
in the protocol itself. The protocol says the team will pre register their analyses
but gives no registration number. If you see anybody claim a results date, ask them
where it came from.

**The Bradford survey**, for the three hour cap estimate: Pickavance, O'Nions,
Hammad, Jackson, Lightfoot, McEachan, Orben, Ryan, Shire, Wood, Wright and Lewer,
"Adolescent social media use and its association with mental health: a cross
sectional study in Bradford, England", BMC Public Health, 7 May 2026. 8,466
students aged 12 to 15 from Born in Bradford: Age of Wonder 2023 to 2024, weighted
to the city's 12 to 15 year old population. Median 3.36 hours a day, interquartile
range 1.88 to 5.44. The reduction from 10.7 percent to 9.5 percent, and the
thirteen children per thousand, are the paper's own figures **under its own stated
assumption that the association is causal**, which the paper says it cannot
demonstrate. It is a cross sectional study. It cannot establish cause, and it says
so.

**The King's College London sleep study**, for the section on the curfew. Sei Yon
Sohn, Lauren Krasnoff, Philippa Rees, Nicola J. Kalk and Ben Carter, "The
Association Between Smartphone Addiction and Sleep: A UK Cross Sectional Study of
Young Adults", Frontiers in Psychiatry, volume 12, 2 March 2021, doi
10.3389/fpsyt.2021.629407. Read from the article page, including its tables.

1,043 participants aged 18 to 30, students at King's College London, recruited
opportunistically on campus, 73.2 percent female, surveyed between 21 January and
4 February 2019. Smartphone addiction prevalence 38.9 percent. Poor sleep 61.6
percent.

From Table 2, outcome poor sleep, adjusted for age, gender, ethnicity, site,
addiction, daily hours and where the phone sleeps: addicted 1.41 (1.06 to 1.87, p
= 0.018); against a reference of three hours a day, four hours 1.01 (0.68 to
1.49), five hours 0.73 (0.47 to 1.12), more than five hours 1.10 (0.72 to 1.69),
none significant. From Table 4, outcome addiction, adjusted for age, gender,
ethnicity and site: last use at 1am or later versus before 11pm, 3.91 (2.32 to
6.61, p < 0.001).

**Four limits on it, all of which the issue states in the text.** There are no
children in it, the youngest participant is eighteen. It is a convenience sample
from one university and the authors say it is not representative of UK young
adults. The data are pre pandemic and the authors flag that. And it is cross
sectional, so, in their own words, no causal relationships can be drawn and they
cannot ignore the possibility of reverse causality.

**A fifth limit, which I have not seen anybody note.** The late night figure is
not adjusted for daily hours. The authors say the two could not be fitted in the
same model because of collinearity, so this paper does not contain a single fair
test of timing against duration, and the issue does not claim one.

**And the paper overclaims in its own discussion**, calling this a deleterious
impact on sleep, which is a causal verb its limitations section rules out. Its
discussion also says a three fold risk where its table says 3.91. The issue says
so rather than quietly using the tables and praising the paper. Its funding
statement and its conflict of interest statement also contradict each other, one
saying there was no funding source and the other naming institutional support,
which I have not resolved and am not reporting either way.

**The 86 percent** of ten to twelve year olds in Bradford with their own social
media profile is cited in the trial protocol to Ofcom's Children and Parents: Media
Use and Attitudes Report 2025. I have not read the Ofcom report directly, because
Ofcom's site refuses the tools I use.

**The ban date.** The Culture Secretary has named March 2027 and the Commons
statement and Hansard say spring 2027. Smartphone Free Childhood's own parent
guidance says no exact date is officially confirmed. The regulations that would fix
it were due to be laid before the end of 2026 and had not been laid when I wrote
this.

**What I have not claimed.** No result from this trial, because there isn't one.
No reporting date. No outcome for any child from anything we build.
