# Issue: He is describing me

**Venue: Substack.** Part of The Wrong Villain series. A response to Christopher
Ferguson's piece of 17 August 2026. Read in full before drafting.

**Justin: this one is about a researcher you know, so the temperature is yours to
set.** It is written generous and it concedes a great deal, but it does say where
his argument slips, and it says out loud that he is describing your business. If
you would rather it were softer, or not published at all, that is a reasonable
call and the work is not wasted either way.

## Subject lines

1. A researcher I rate says people like me are part of the problem
2. He is describing me, and he is partly right
3. The word I keep using, and the business I happen to run

**Preview line:** Christopher Ferguson argues that if you oppose social media bans
you should stop saying "harms". One of his asides is about people who run
businesses like mine. Here is what I think he gets right, and the one place his
own standard slips.

---

Christopher Ferguson has written a piece arguing that people who oppose social
media bans should stop using the word "harms".

His case is that the word is vague, that it implies a cause nobody has
established, that it makes social media sound like a major driver of children's
problems when he does not believe the data support that, and that it therefore
feeds the argument for the very bans those people are trying to resist.

Then, early on, he makes an observation about who tends to be making the "do
something, but not a ban" argument. Many of them, he notes, run media literacy,
youth advocacy or tech wellness businesses. He is careful about it. He says it
does not disqualify them, only that it is worth noticing.

I run a staged digital parenting platform. I have been making the do something but
not a ban argument in public for a year.

He is describing me.

So this is not a rebuttal. It is what I think after sitting with a criticism that
has my name on it, from somebody whose meta analytic work I have cited approvingly
when it suited me, which is the test.

## What he is right about, and what I checked

Start with the free speech point, because it is the strongest thing in the piece
and the part a fair opponent has to answer.

To keep under sixteens off a platform you have to know who is over sixteen. That
means checking everybody. His phrase for what follows is that we would be handing
governments the ability to track not just young people but adults online.

I have written two posts about Ofcom's four methods and what each costs a family,
and I reached the same place by a different road. A passport, a bank check, a face
scan or a reusable digital ID, and every one of them is a thing you hand over.
Ferguson gets there faster than I did and states it more plainly.

Now the word itself.

His argument is that "harms" does a particular kind of work: it sounds specific
while naming nothing, and it carries causation inside it without ever having to
defend one. Say harms and the reader fills in the blank with whatever frightens
them most, which is usually something the evidence does not support.

I think that is correct, and I think it is a better criticism than the people it
is aimed at will want to admit.

So I went and counted. About fifty uses of the word across everything we have
published in the last two months.

Here is what I found, and I was relieved rather than vindicated. Almost all of
them are the word being used to mark a limit rather than to gesture at a fear. A
committee that refused to conclude social media causes population level harm. A
note at the foot of an issue saying this does not claim AI toys harm children. A
line saying I will not give you a figure for how often contact from a stranger
leads to harm, because I do not have one I trust.

That is the word doing the opposite of what he describes. But I would not have
been able to tell you that before I looked, and the counting is the point. If you
use a contested word fifty times in two months you should know how.

The third thing he is right about is subtler. He says that if you have a specific
concern, name the specific thing. Adult content reaching a child's feed.
Catfishing. Harassment that never gets moderated. A child sharing an address.
Those are nameable, checkable, and arguable. "Harms" is none of those.

I agree, and it is roughly what this newsletter already tries to do. Which is
presumably why the criticism stung.

## Where the argument slips, and it is one sentence

Now the part I think is wrong, and it is not a small thing, because it is the same
fault this whole series exists to point at.

Ferguson dismisses the graphs in The Anxious Generation, the ones that line up a
rise in teenage distress against the arrival of the smartphone. His objection is
that they are correlational and out of date. That objection is sound. I have made
it myself.

A few paragraphs later he offers his own account of where children's problems
actually come from: families under strain and schools that are not working. And
his evidence for the schools half is that youth suicides track the academic year.

He knows what he has just done, and to his credit he says so in the sentence
itself. His words are that this is correlational data, sure, but that there is no
such correlational data now for social media.

That is the whole argument in one line, and I do not think it holds.

The problem is not that he used correlational evidence. Correlational evidence is
most of what any of us have. The problem is that he rejected a correlation when it
pointed at screens and accepted one when it pointed at schools, in the same piece,
a few hundred words apart.

If seasonal patterning is good enough to locate the problem in schools, then the
same class of evidence cannot be disqualifying when somebody else points it at
phones. Either correlation can locate a cause or it cannot.

I think it mostly cannot, which is why I am unconvinced by both sets of graphs.

## The claim I can answer with a number

One more. He writes that so far, evidence suggests bans just do not work, and the
support he offers is that children and some parents get around them.

There is now evidence about this, from the only country that has run the
experiment, and it is in neither camp's mouth.

Australia's regulator went back three months after its ban began and surveyed 803
children aged ten to fifteen. The share holding an account on an age restricted
platform fell from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent. The regulator's own description
is modest, and statistically significant.

About one in five of the children who had an account lost it.

I have written a whole issue on the fact that nobody quotes that number, and here
is a careful researcher, writing at length about whether bans work, not quoting
it. Not because he is hiding it. I think because it is genuinely inconvenient to
everybody: too small for the people who want the ban to feel vindicated, too real
for the people who want it dismissed.

Modest is not nothing. A ban that moves account holding ten points is doing
something, and whether that something is worth what it costs is exactly the
argument we should be having instead of the one about whether anything happened.

## The gap where a policy should be

There is one more thing, and it is an absence rather than an error.

Ferguson names real concerns. Adult content reaching a child's feed. Catfishing.
Harassment that nobody moderates. Children being pushed into fights, or handing
over an address. He is explicit that these are legitimate, and he is right.

Then he says what to do about them, and the answer is good media literacy, plus
presenting the data patiently and being calm.

Look at that pairing for a second. Four of those five problems are things a
company does, or fails to do, inside a product it designed. A feed that serves a
thirteen year old something nobody chose. A reporting route that goes nowhere. An
account that lets a stranger move a child somewhere private.

No amount of literacy in the child reaches the design of the feed. A well taught
fourteen year old and a badly taught one are both downstream of what the
recommender decides to show them, and neither of them can moderate a platform.

So a piece that opposes a ban, names the real problems, and then offers only
education has left the field. If the only two proposals on the table are a blunt
ban and better conversations at home, the ban wins, because it is the one that
asks something of the companies.

I would rather argue about design duties, reporting routes that work, and what a
platform owes a child who flags something. That argument is available and almost
nobody is having it, which is the thing I find genuinely sad about this whole
debate.

And yes, I notice that teaching children is also what I sell. The difference I
would claim is that I do not think it is sufficient, and I say so.

## The part about me

Back to the aside, because dodging it would be the cowardly version of this issue.

He is right that I have an interest. If the public conversation lands on teach
them rather than ban them, I am better off. That is true whatever my motives are,
and it would be true if I had never thought about it. An interest is a fact about
a position, not an accusation about a person.

Two things follow and I want both on the record.

The first is that it cuts at my confidence and should. It is a reason to check my
own work harder than I check anybody else's, which is why this newsletter keeps
publishing the numbers that are awkward for it: that we hold no outcome evidence
for our own stage check, that the strongest UK study on children found nothing
where I might have liked it to find something, that the one measurable thing the
Australian ban did is a point against the argument I make for a living.

The second is that the argument still has to be answered on its merits, and I
notice Ferguson agrees with me about that in principle. His own recommendation for
what to do instead of banning is good media literacy. Which is, as near as makes
no difference, the thing the media literacy businesses he had just flagged happen
to sell.

I do not think that discredits his recommendation. I think it shows the test does
not work the way it looks like it works. Everybody in this argument has an
interest, including the people with no product at all, who have a reputation and a
book and a position to defend.

Nobody gets a free pass. Including me. Especially me, since I am the one with the
checkout page.

## What a parent takes from this

Nothing in this issue changes what happens in your house tonight. It is an
argument between adults about how to argue.

But there is one habit in it worth stealing, and it is the most useful thing
Ferguson says.

### The thing to do this week

**Next time you read something frightening about children and screens, find the
noun.**

Not the headline. The specific thing the piece says is happening. Is it a feed
showing a thirteen year old something nobody chose for them? Is it a stranger
moving a conversation somewhere private? Is it a child awake at one in the
morning? Is it a girl comparing herself to two hundred people before breakfast?

Those are different problems with different answers, and three of them have
nothing to do with how long the phone is in her hand.

If you cannot find the noun, if the piece only has a mood, you have learnt
something about the piece rather than about your child.

Then do the same to anybody who tells you there is nothing to worry about,
including me. Ask what specific thing they are saying does not happen.

That test cuts both ways on purpose. It is the only one I know that does.

## Sources, and what each one can carry

**The piece.** Christopher J Ferguson, "If You Worry About Social Media Bans Stop
Saying 'Harms'", Secrets of Grimoire Manor, 17 August 2026. Read in full. Every
characterisation of his argument above comes from that reading, and I have tried
to give the strongest version of it before disagreeing with any of it. The
subtitle is his: critics of social media bans often feed directly into the
narrative propping them up.

**I have not checked his sources.** The piece links four items on its central
evidence claim, two of which are his own meta analyses, plus a CDC report on youth
mental health. I have not read any of them for this issue, so nothing here
endorses or disputes his evidence base. The disagreement above is about the
internal consistency of the argument, which does not require me to adjudicate the
literature.

**The correlational point** rests on his own words, which concede the issue in the
sentence that makes the claim. I am not putting anything in his mouth, and the
reason I quote him conceding it is that it is to his credit that he did.

**The Australian figures.** The eSafety Commissioner's follow up, 803 children aged
10 to 15, account holding falling from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent, described by
eSafety as modest and statistically significant. Our register records these as
checked against the source in an earlier pass. **esafety.gov.au has refused our
tools four times across three sessions**, so I could not re read them while
writing this, and that is a weaker position than I would like for a number I am
using to answer somebody.

**The Anxious Generation.** Ferguson's piece names the book and not its author. It
is Jonathan Haidt's, and I have said so because leaving it unnamed would be coy. I
have deliberately not repeated his characterisation of it, which is sharper than
anything I would write about another person's work.

**Our own fifty uses of the word.** Counted across `content/packs` for October and
November 2026. The count is mine and anybody with the repository can repeat it.

**What this issue does not do.** It does not question Ferguson's motives. He
raised an interest of mine, which was fair and which I have answered; returning
fire on his would be the move this whole series argues against. It does not claim
his evidence is wrong, only that one step in his argument applies a standard he
does not apply to himself. And it makes no outcome claim for any child, from our
product or from anything else.
