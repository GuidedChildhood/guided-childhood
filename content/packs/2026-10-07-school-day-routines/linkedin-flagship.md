## The post
355,358 adolescents. Sleep and breakfast beat the screen in every dataset.

I went looking because of two moments every parent knows. The television on at 7.30 with the shoes not on. The child who falls apart in the car at 3.30.

Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski at Oxford ran one analysis across three large adolescent datasets, two American and one British. Nature Human Behaviour, 2019.

Technology use explained at most 0.4 percent of the variance in wellbeing, a correlation of about 0.06. Small on average, real and concentrated in the children already struggling. Not the same as harmless. That is every screen for every teenager; girls on heavy social media is a bigger number.

Same datasets, same units: sleep plus 0.07 to plus 0.25, breakfast plus 0.12 to plus 0.17, technology medians minus 0.005 to minus 0.035. Larger in all three datasets, by the least in the British one.

The phone in the bedroom is the right villain for the night, and the bedroom rule comes first. The television at 7.30 is the wrong villain for the morning.

Among 6,616 London pupils aged 11 and 12, a phone in the hour before sleep meant 1.82 times the odds of too little sleep on a school night, 2.13 in the dark.

Sleep loss shows up fast: 32 children aged 8 to 12 sent to bed an hour later for four nights, then an hour earlier; mood and control over feelings moved with it.

The big numbers are teenagers. The primary age evidence is the Millennium Cohort: 10,230 British children, irregular bedtimes at three, five and seven predicting behaviour at seven, improving when the bedtime did.

The only before school study I could find is one French case control: 276 children aged three to six, about language delay, screen time from parents' memory. Minutes before school have never been tested against lateness or mood.

Two children, same twenty minutes of television before school. One was in bed by eight and has toast inside her. One was up till ten and has nothing inside him. One North Yorkshire district asked its Year 6 in 2024: 8 percent had eaten nothing before school and 57 percent had felt tired at school the day before. Half of its Year 2, aged six and seven, have a television in their bedroom.

Children do fall apart at 3.30. The internet calls it after school restraint collapse, and the idea under the name is that self control drains like a tank. 23 labs got 0.04 in 2016 against a claimed 0.62, and only small effects since. A school day does tire a child. Keep the pattern, drop the tank. For a child with ADHD, autism or a school worry, the 3.30 has its own cause and its own professional.

A regular bedtime. Something to eat before any screen. The end agreed before the screen goes on.

This is The Wrong Villain series continued. The school day.

Which one is yours, the 7.30 or the 3.30, and what have you tried that actually helped?

## The first comment, posted the second it is live
The sources, in order. Orben and Przybylski, Nature Human Behaviour 2019, three datasets (Monitoring the Future and the Youth Risk Behavior Survey in the United States, the Millennium Cohort Study in the UK), n 355,358, and their 2020 reply to Twenge, Haidt, Joiner and Campbell, which reran the analysis with the critics' choices and found the girls and heavy user numbers larger and the average still small. Mireku and colleagues, Environment International 2019, the SCAMP cohort, 6,616 London pupils, cross sectional at baseline. Vriend and colleagues, Journal of Pediatric Psychology 2013, 32 children, bedtime moved an hour each way. Baum and colleagues, Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 2014, 50 teenagers. Kelly, Kelly and Sacker, Pediatrics 2013, Millennium Cohort Study, 10,230 children. Collet and colleagues, Acta Paediatrica 2019, 167 cases and 109 controls. Lillard and Peterson, Pediatrics 2011, for what is on in the twenty minutes. Growing Up in North Yorkshire 2024, the Selby district report, Year 6 n 829 for the breakfast and tiredness figures, Year 2 for the television in the bedroom. Hagger and colleagues, Perspectives on Psychological Science 2016, 23 labs, 2,141 adults, d 0.04 against the 0.62 the literature had claimed; Vohs and colleagues 2021 and Dang and colleagues 2021 for the small effects since. The UK Chief Medical Officers' 2019 commentary, which said the evidence was not conclusive and recommended precaution; the two lines I lean on in replies, phones outside the bedroom at bedtime and screen free meals, are what their precaution looked like.

Three things before anyone asks. The experiments are small, 32 and 50 children, and the second one, Baum 2014, is 6.5 against 10 hours in bed in 14 to 17 year olds; they are why I trust sleep, because sleep and breakfast come from the same surveys as the screen number and I have no experiment for toast. What is on in the twenty minutes matters for the next hour in a four year old (Lillard and Peterson 2011), which is a content point, not a minutes point. The 23 lab replication tested adults on lab tasks, so it rules out the tank, not the tired child. And this post is about minutes and routines, not what a feed shows an eleven year old; that is a different harm and I have never called it small.

## Replies ready for the first hour
- "So the screen is the upstream cause and you have proved it." For the night, yes, and the bedroom rule comes first in the post. The claim is narrower than the headline you are arguing with: the television at 7.30 and the tablet at 3.30 are not where the evidence puts the morning and the afternoon.
- "0.4 percent is variance, your sleep figures are correlations, you switched units." Fair, and the post gives both: 0.4 percent of variance is a correlation of about 0.06, and the technology medians sit between minus 0.005 and minus 0.035 next to sleep at plus 0.07 to plus 0.25. Same paper, same units, larger in all three datasets, by the least in the British one.
- "That pooled measure hides social media and girls." It does, and Orben's own 2020 reply found the girls and heavy user numbers larger, which is why the post says so and why it is a different post. Nothing here rests on the average being zero.
- "Ferguson's 0.061 was contested and now you are back with the same average." Both are averages across all screens and all teenagers, the subgroup numbers are larger, and nothing here rests on either being zero.
- "Ego depletion replicated later." Small effects, Vohs 2021 and Dang 2021, nowhere near the 0.62 claimed, and the tank was never the thing parents mean. A school day tires a child; the post says so. The name is the only part I would drop.
- "My child eats breakfast and sleeps fine and still melts down." Then the morning is not where the trouble is. A fight that happens only on school days, with a stomach ache, is worth a word with the school before it is worth a rule about the television; the Tavistock and Portman's note on emotionally based school avoidance is the plain language guide I would send a parent.
- "For an autistic child the collapse is masking, and the name gets them a SENCO." Agreed, and that is the line in the post: for a child with ADHD, autism or a school worry the 3.30 has its own cause and its own professional. Keep whatever word gets you the meeting. The heads up before an ending stays for the children who need it; agreeing it in advance is the move for the rest.
- "You sell a parenting app, you would say this." I sell a parenting pathway, not a screen, and everything in this post costs nothing and needs no app. The product's version of this is untested, and I have said so in writing with a kill line attached.
- "This minimises what the feed does to an eleven year old." This post is about minutes and routines. What a feed shows a child is a different harm and a different argument, and I have never called it small.
- "The CMOs said the research was insufficient, you cannot have it both ways." They said it was not conclusive and recommended precaution, and the two lines I quote are what their precaution looked like. I am not asking for less precaution; I am asking for it in the bedroom rather than at the breakfast table.
