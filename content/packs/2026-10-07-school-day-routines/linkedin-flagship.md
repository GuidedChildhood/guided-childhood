## The post
355,358 adolescents. Sleep and breakfast beat the screen, several times over.

I went looking for this because of two moments every parent knows. The television on at 7.30 with the shoes not on. The child who falls apart in the car at 3.30.

Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski ran the same analysis across three large adolescent datasets, two American and one British. Nature Human Behaviour, 2019.

Technology use explained at most 0.4 percent of the variance in wellbeing.

In the same datasets, getting enough sleep carried an association of plus 0.07 to plus 0.25. Eating breakfast, plus 0.12 to plus 0.17.

Several times the size, in the same paper.

None of this makes the screen innocent. The screen is often what moves the bedtime.

Among 6,616 London pupils aged 11 and 12, the ones on a phone in the hour before sleep had 1.82 times the odds of too little sleep on a school night. In the dark, 2.13 times.

And an hour of sleep is not a small thing. In a randomised crossover experiment, 32 children aged 8 to 12 lost one hour a night for four nights. Their mood and their control over their feelings dropped. Fifty teenagers given 6.5 hours in bed against 10 showed the same. Their parents saw it too.

Now the case against the morning screen itself. The only study I could find that isolates the before school slot is a French case control, 276 children aged three to six, about language delay, with the screen time taken from parents' memory.

That is the whole case against the morning screen on its own. The sleep and food case has 355,000 adolescents, a cohort of 10,230 British seven year olds and two experiments behind it.

Two children, same twenty minutes of television before school. One was in bed by eight and has toast inside her. One was up till ten and has nothing inside him. When one North Yorkshire district asked its Year 6 in 2024, 8 percent had eaten nothing before school and 57 percent had felt tired at school the day before.

Children do fall apart at 3.30, often on the parent who collects them. The internet calls it after school restraint collapse, and the explanation under the name is that self control runs out like a tank. 23 labs tested that idea on 2,141 adults in 2016. The original claim was 0.62. They got 0.04, with the interval straddling zero. The meltdown is real. A psychotherapist named it in 2016, I could find no study that has tested it, and the idea underneath it did not hold.

A regular bedtime. Something to eat before any screen. The end agreed before the screen goes on, not announced when it is over. The television is the least of it.

This is The Wrong Villain series continued. The school day.

Which one is yours, the 7.30 or the 3.30, and what have you tried that actually helped?

## The first comment, posted the second it is live
The sources, in order. Orben and Przybylski, Nature Human Behaviour 2019, three datasets (Monitoring the Future and the Youth Risk Behavior Survey in the United States, the Millennium Cohort Study in the UK), n 355,358; the median association for technology use sat between minus 0.005 and minus 0.035. Mireku and colleagues, Environment International 2019, the SCAMP cohort, 6,616 London pupils, cross sectional at baseline. Vriend and colleagues, Journal of Pediatric Psychology 2013, 32 children. Baum and colleagues, Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 2014, 50 teenagers. Collet and colleagues, Acta Paediatrica 2019, 167 cases and 109 controls. Growing Up in North Yorkshire 2024, the Selby district report, Year 6 n 829. Hagger and colleagues, Perspectives on Psychological Science 2016, 23 labs, 2,141 adults, d 0.04 against the 0.62 the literature had claimed. Kelly, Kelly and Sacker, Pediatrics 2013, Millennium Cohort Study, 10,230 children, where irregular bedtimes predicted behaviour difficulties and the effect reversed when bedtimes became regular. The UK Chief Medical Officers' 2019 commentary for the two lines of advice I lean on in replies: phones outside the bedroom at bedtime, screen free meals.

Two things before anyone asks. The experiments are small, 32 and 50 children, and I have said so. And the 23 lab replication tested adults on lab tasks, so it rules out the mechanism, not the meltdown. Children do fall apart at 3.30. Feed them first, ask later.

## Replies ready for the first hour
- "So screens do not matter?" They matter where the evidence puts them: the phone in the bedroom moves the bedtime, and the bedtime moves the morning. The Chief Medical Officers' advice is two lines, phones outside the bedroom at bedtime and screen free meals. That is where I would start too.
- "My child eats breakfast and sleeps fine and still melts down." Then the morning is not where the trouble is. A fight that happens only on school days, with a stomach ache, is worth a word with the school before it is worth a rule about the television; the Tavistock and Portman's note on emotionally based school avoidance is the plain language guide I would send a parent.
- "Where is the restraint collapse study?" I could not find one. The idea it rests on, willpower as a tank, was the one tested in 23 labs. The pattern parents describe is real, the name is the only part I would drop.
