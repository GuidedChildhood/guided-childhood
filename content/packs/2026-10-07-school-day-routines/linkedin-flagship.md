## The post
7.30. The television is on and the shoes are not. 3.30. The car, and a child who held it together all day falls apart on you.

If that is your house, this is for you. It has been mine: the argument in the car, the fall apart when the screen went off, the timers, the rules.

I have three, aged four, nine and twelve. There is work to get ready for and breakfast to cook, and the screen helps with that. Nobody here is being told to get rid of it. This is the routine around it, and seeing the two flashpoints coming.

I went looking at what the evidence says about those two moments. Most of it is not about the screen.

The morning is decided the night before. In 10,230 British children in the Millennium Cohort, an irregular bedtime at three, five and seven predicted behaviour at seven, and improved when the bedtime did. Send 32 children aged 8 to 12 to bed an hour later for four nights and their mood moves with it. That one was a randomised experiment.

The phone is part of the night. Among 6,616 London pupils aged 11 and 12, a phone in the hour before sleep meant 1.82 times the odds of too little sleep on a school night. So the bedroom rule comes first, and it is a night rule, not a morning one.

The after school hour is the one the screen actually fills. Ofcom's meter puts 8 to 14 year olds' online time at 25 to 32 minutes a day in the breakfast window, and an hour and a half to two hours after school, the largest block of the day. In the one diary study of how screen time ends, 380 endings in 28 families, the endings that went well were the routine ones and the ones the programme itself ended. Warned children were more upset, not less.

The case against the morning screen itself is one French case control: 276 children aged three to six, about language delay, screen time from parents' memory. Even in the datasets built to study screens, 355,358 adolescents, Orben and Przybylski at Oxford found sleep and breakfast carried larger associations with wellbeing than technology use in all three. Same surveys, same units, so take it as proportion, not proof.

Children do fall apart at 3.30. A school day tires a child, and hunger looks like defiance. The name the internet gives it rests on an idea 23 labs could not find in 2016, and only small effects since. Keep the pattern, drop the tank. For a child with ADHD, autism or a school worry, the 3.30 has its own cause and its own professional.

So, tonight. A regular bedtime, and the phone charging in the kitchen. Something to eat before any screen, at 7.30 and at the gate. The end agreed before the screen goes on, not announced when it is over.

That is the whole of it, and it costs nothing. Part two, next, is the thing we built so you can log how it went and tell me what I got wrong.

This is The Wrong Villain series continued. The school day.

Which one is yours, the 7.30 or the 3.30, and what have you tried that actually helped?

## The first comment, posted the second it is live
The sources, in order. Kelly, Kelly and Sacker, Pediatrics 2013, Millennium Cohort Study, 10,230 children. Vriend and colleagues, Journal of Pediatric Psychology 2013, 32 children, bedtime moved an hour each way. Mireku and colleagues, Environment International 2019, the SCAMP cohort, 6,616 London pupils, cross sectional at baseline. Ofcom, Children's Online Experiences 2026, the metered panel of 701 children aged 8 to 14, Figure 4 (phones, tablets and computers, weekdays and weekends averaged, television sets not counted). Hiniker, Suh, Cao and Kientz, CHI 2016, 28 families, 380 endings, children aged one to five, parent rated; the authors think the warning result is partly parents warning when they already expect trouble. Collet and colleagues, Acta Paediatrica 2019, 167 cases and 109 controls. Orben and Przybylski, Nature Human Behaviour 2019, three datasets (Monitoring the Future and the Youth Risk Behavior Survey in the United States, the Millennium Cohort Study in the UK), n 355,358: technology use explained at most 0.4 percent of the variance in wellbeing, a correlation of about 0.06, medians between minus 0.005 and minus 0.035; sleep plus 0.07 to plus 0.25 and breakfast plus 0.12 to plus 0.17 in the same datasets, larger in all three, by the least in the British one. That is every screen for every teenager; their 2020 reply to Twenge, Haidt, Joiner and Campbell found the girls and heavy social media numbers larger and the average still small. Hagger and colleagues, Perspectives on Psychological Science 2016, 23 labs, 2,141 adults, d 0.04 against the 0.62 the literature had claimed; Vohs and colleagues 2021 and Dang and colleagues 2021 for the small effects since. Lillard and Peterson, Pediatrics 2011, for what is on in the twenty minutes: nine minutes of a fast cartoon and 60 four year olds did worse on four executive function tasks, a content point, not a minutes point. The UK Chief Medical Officers' 2019 commentary, which said the evidence was not conclusive and recommended precaution; the two lines I lean on, phones outside the bedroom at bedtime and screen free meals, are what their precaution looked like.

Three things before anyone asks. The experiments are small, 32 children and, for the teenage one I did not quote, 50; they are why I trust sleep, because sleep and breakfast come from the same surveys as the screen number and I have no experiment for toast. The 23 lab replication tested adults on lab tasks, so it rules out the tank, not the tired child. And this post is about minutes and routines, not what a feed shows an eleven year old; that is a different harm and I have never called it small.

## Replies ready for the first hour
- "So the screen is the upstream cause and you have proved it." For the night, yes, and the bedroom rule comes first in the post. The claim is narrower than the headline you are arguing with: the television at 7.30 and the tablet at 3.30 are not where the evidence puts the morning and the afternoon.
- "0.4 percent is variance, your sleep figures are correlations, you switched units." The first comment gives both: 0.4 percent of variance is a correlation of about 0.06, and the technology medians sit between minus 0.005 and minus 0.035 next to sleep at plus 0.07 to plus 0.25. Same paper, same units, larger in all three datasets, by the least in the British one. The post calls it proportion, not proof, on purpose.
- "That pooled measure hides social media and girls." It does, and Orben's own 2020 reply found the girls and heavy user numbers larger, which is why the comment says so and why it is a different post. Nothing here rests on the average being zero.
- "Ferguson's 0.061 was contested and now you are back with the same average." Both are averages across all screens and all teenagers, the subgroup numbers are larger, and nothing here rests on either being zero. For the record, Rausch and Haidt's critique is of Ferguson's 2024 experiments meta analysis, not the 2025 correlational one.
- "Ego depletion replicated later." Small effects, Vohs 2021 and Dang 2021, nowhere near the 0.62 claimed, and the tank was never the thing parents mean. A school day tires a child; the post says so. The name is the only part I would drop.
- "My child eats breakfast and sleeps fine and still melts down." Then the morning is not where the trouble is. A fight that happens only on school days, with a stomach ache, is worth a word with the school before it is worth a rule about the television; the Tavistock and Portman's note on emotionally based school avoidance is the plain language guide I would send a parent.
- "For an autistic child the collapse is masking, and the name gets them a SENCO." Agreed, and that is the line in the post: for a child with ADHD, autism or a school worry the 3.30 has its own cause and its own professional. Keep whatever word gets you the meeting. The heads up before an ending stays for the children who need it; agreeing it in advance is the move for the rest.
- "You sell a parenting app, you would say this." I sell a parenting pathway, not a screen, and everything in this post costs nothing and needs no app. The product's version of this is untested, and part two says so with a kill line attached.
- "This minimises what the feed does to an eleven year old." This post is about minutes and routines. What a feed shows a child is a different harm and a different argument, and I have never called it small.
- "The CMOs said the research was insufficient, you cannot have it both ways." They said it was not conclusive and recommended precaution, and the two lines I quote are what their precaution looked like. I am not asking for less precaution; I am asking for it in the bedroom rather than at the breakfast table.
- "Ofcom's minutes exclude the telly." They do, and the first comment says so. The point stands without the television set: the online time sits after school, not before it.
