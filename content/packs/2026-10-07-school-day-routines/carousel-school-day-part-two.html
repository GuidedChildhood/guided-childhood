One. Your child's clock

A card on Home, fifty minutes before school starts and fifteen after they are home.

Your child's day, set once in Settings. Morning advice is useless once the shoes are on, and the first quarter hour home is coat off, bag down. The numbers are a guess we will measure.

2

Two. One window a day

Three texts a week beat one and five.

In the one direct test of how often to message parents (3,473 families), five a week raised opt out by 58 percent and helped nobody. Two microrandomised trials put the right minute at single digit points. So we cut the count.

3

Three. The words

Sleep and food first. The end agreed before the screen goes on.

Breakfast first, the screen after shoes. Snack in your hand at the gate, no questions for twenty minutes. 355,358 adolescents on sleep and breakfast, two experiments on an hour of sleep, 380 logged screen endings. The card never names a syndrome.

4

Four. Three taps, and what each one does

The logging is the product. A tap is a moment, a moment becomes a worry we can watch, and a week later we ask whether what you tried worked. That is how the advice gets better than my reading of the literature.

5

To be fair

Yes, the card is a screen in a parent's hand.

At the exact moment I say the screen is beside the point. One card, one window, and every rule on it needs no app. With three children and a job, the screen in our house is staying; this is the routine around it.

6

What we do not know

Whether anyone taps.

Under ten percent and we redesign or kill it. Whether fifty minutes is right. Whether the no warning finding, from 28 American families with children under five, holds for a nine year old, or for a child with ADHD, where the NHS says the sequence takes longer.
