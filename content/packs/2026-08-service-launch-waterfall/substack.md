## The year the ground moved

Three things changed this year, and I have spent it building a company for the day they did.

The data changed. The best studies stopped blaming the hours a child spends and started pointing at the pattern underneath them. The law changed. The UK committed to an under 16 ban, made school phone bans statutory this September, France passed its own, and Australia's world first ban went live and promptly sprang leaks. And something genuinely new arrived that almost no parent has a plan for. AI companions.

Put those together and an ordinary parent is standing in the loudest year we have ever had on children and screens, told that change is coming, with almost nothing to actually do about it. The market offers two answers. Block it, or watch their every move. I think both are wrong, and I want to walk you through why, using five findings that landed in 2025 and 2026, and the thing we built for each.

### One. It was never the hours

A study in JAMA followed more than four thousand children for four years, and the number of hours on a screen barely predicted anything. What predicted harm was a pattern, the compulsive kind, cannot put it down, anxious without it, using it to fill a gap. Children on that rising trajectory carried a much higher risk of the outcomes every parent fears. Children on the same hours without the pattern did not. Hold it honestly, it is association not proof and the average effect is modest, but the one experimental handle we have points the same way, and the signal keeps arriving in the same place. It is the shape of the use, not the size of it.

So when we built our balance report, I refused to build a screen time counter. Everyone builds the counter, the dashboard of hours that goes red after the fact. We built the opposite, a weekly read that shows the pattern, the making and connecting on one side and the passive scrolling on the other, and flags a young child on the phone at an age where that should be near zero. A counter tells you what the clock did. We wanted to show you what the child did.

### Two. The thing no parent has a plan for

Seventy two percent of teenagers have now used an AI companion, a chatbot they talk to like a friend, and it went from niche to normal in about twelve months. The first careful studies, from Stanford, Drexel and Aalto, land the same way. For lonely or vulnerable young people, heavy use tracks with more loneliness over time, not less, and a quiet pull away from human connection. This is the newest front line, and unlike the older social media argument there is very little pushback in the evidence yet. It is also the one place the industry has answered with lawsuits and settlements rather than teaching a single child how to handle it.

So it is the first thing we are building next. A short, staged strand that teaches a child what an AI companion actually is, why it is built to feel like a friend, where the danger sits in that feeling, and how to tell when a bot is not a person, plus the words for the parent's my chatbot said conversation. Not a ban on it, because bans do not survive contact with a curious teenager. Judgement, which does.

### Three. Australia banned it, and most kids stayed online

Australia did the thing everyone is asking for. It banned social media for under sixteens. Eight in ten of those children are still online, on VPNs, borrowed accounts and fake ages, within weeks, on the government's own research. Beside it, an independent audit this summer found only forty percent of the safety features platforms tell parents to trust actually work and can be found by a child. So the two things a worried parent is handed, the ban and the toggles, are the two things the evidence says leak most.

This is the whole reason we build a pathway and not a wall. Guided Childhood is a staged road from age four to sixteen, where a child grows the judgement to handle each thing before they meet it, so that when the app finally arrives, and it will, they are prepared rather than simply blocked. The ban takes the apps for a while. We use that while to build the child. If you have delayed brilliantly, and hundreds of thousands of families have, this is the part nobody sold you, what you do the day the delay ends.

### Four and five. The guilt, and the belief under all of it

Eighty four percent of parents feel guilty about their child's screens, and the average parent loses the equivalent of forty eight nights of sleep a year worrying, with technology the biggest single cause. Here is the kind thing the guilt never lets you feel. The evidence on how to actually do this favours neither the strictest home nor the most permissive one, but the guided middle. A 2026 study found the hands off families, not the structured ones, had children who felt they had the least freedom, and the paediatricians now weight content, co use and conversation over the number on the clock.

So the whole of what I am building is one thing, a calm, evidence led alternative to a market that sells fear on one side and surveillance on the other. And underneath it, a belief I will say plainly, once. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are poverty, the things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with too little help. Social media takes the blame because it is visible and rich. The real drivers sit in the dark. I cannot fix them from an app, but I can refuse to build one more thing that points at a tired parent and calls it a solution. What I can build is connection, because connection is the protection.
