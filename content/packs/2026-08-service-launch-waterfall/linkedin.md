## MONDAY — The year the ground moved

**Post text:**

Three things changed this year, and I have spent it building a company for the day they did.

The data changed. The best studies stopped blaming the hours and started pointing at the pattern of use. The law changed. The UK committed to an under 16 ban, made school phone bans statutory this September, and France passed its own. And something new arrived that almost no parent has a plan for yet. AI companions.

Put those together and a parent is standing in the middle of the loudest year we have had on children and screens, being told change is coming, with almost nothing to actually do about it. The shelf offers two things. Block it, or spy on it. That is the whole market.

I think both are wrong, and this week I want to show you why, using five findings that landed in 2025 and 2026, and the thing we built for each one.

Not to sell you a panic. To do the opposite. The evidence, read honestly, is calmer and more useful than the headlines, and it points somewhere neither the ban camp nor the surveillance camp wants to look. At preparation. At teaching. At the child, and the family around them.

One study a day this week. One piece of what we built beside it. By Friday you will have the whole argument, and the whole of what I am building, in your hands.

Here is the frame for all five, in one line. The ban delays the apps. We build the judgement.

If someone told you change was coming for your child online, but not what to do about it, what would you actually want handed to you?

## TUESDAY — It was never the hours

**Post text:**

A study in JAMA followed more than four thousand children for four years. The number of hours on a screen barely predicted anything.

This is the finding that should have changed the whole conversation, and mostly has not. What predicted harm was not the total time. It was a pattern, the compulsive kind, cannot put it down, anxious without it, using it to fill a gap. Children on that rising trajectory carried a risk ratio of two point one four for the outcomes every parent fears. Children on the same hours without that pattern did not.

Be careful with it, because it is association not proof, and the effect is real but modest. But the direction is now backed by the one experimental handle we have, a meta analysis where reducing use lowered depression. The signal keeps arriving at the same place. It is the shape of the use, not the size of it.

So when we built our balance report, I refused to build a screen time counter. Everyone builds the counter, the dashboard of hours that goes red. We built the opposite. It reads the pattern. It shows the making and the connecting on one side and the passive scrolling on the other, and it flags a young child on the phone at an age where that should be near zero, not a big number on a Saturday that balances out by Sunday.

A counter tells you what the clock did. We wanted to show you what the child did.

If the hours are the wrong thing to measure, what do you actually watch for in your own house?

## WEDNESDAY — The thing no parent has a plan for

**Post text:**

Seventy two percent of teenagers have used an AI companion. Almost no parent knows.

That number is from this year, and it went from niche to mainstream in about twelve months. Two thirds of teens now use AI chatbots at all, and a large share use one as a friend, someone to talk to. And the first careful studies are landing, from Stanford, Drexel and Aalto, and they point the same way. For lonely or vulnerable young people, heavy use of an AI companion tracks with more loneliness over time, not less, and a quiet pull away from human connection.

This is the newest front line, and unlike the older social media argument there is very little pushback in the evidence yet. It is also the one place the whole industry has responded with lawsuits and settlements rather than teaching a single child how to handle it.

So it is the first thing we are building next. A short, staged strand that teaches a child what an AI companion actually is, why it is built to feel like a friend, where the danger sits in that feeling, and how to tell when a bot is not a person. And it hands the parent the words for the conversation they did not know they needed to have, the my chatbot said one.

Not a ban on it. Bans do not survive contact with a curious teenager. Judgement does.

I built this because my own instinct, the first time I watched a child talk to one of these, was fear, and fear is not a plan. A plan is teaching them to see it.

Has your child mentioned an AI they talk to, and did you know what to say back?

## THURSDAY — Australia banned it. Eight in ten kids stayed online.

**Post text:**

Australia did the thing everyone is asking for. It banned social media for under sixteens. Eight in ten of those children are still online.

That is Australia's own research, months after the ban went live. Kids moved to VPNs, borrowed accounts, fake ages and fringe apps. The government is now talking about strengthening a law that only just started. It is the clearest natural experiment we have, and it says something the ban camp does not want to hear. A ban is a start line, not a finish line.

Beside it, an independent audit this summer tested the safety features the platforms tell parents to rely on. Only forty percent actually worked as advertised and could be found by a child. So the two things a worried parent is handed, the ban and the safety toggles, are the two things the evidence says are leakiest.

This is the whole reason we build a pathway and not a wall. Guided Childhood is a staged road from age four to sixteen, where a child grows the judgement to handle each thing before they meet it, so that when the app finally arrives, and it will, they are prepared rather than simply blocked. The ban takes the apps for a while. We use that while to build the child.

If you have delayed brilliantly, and hundreds of thousands of families have, this is the part nobody sold you. What you do the day the delay ends.

If a ban buys you two years, what would you want your child to have learned by the time it lifts?

## FRIDAY — Most parents feel guilty. Almost none should.

**Post text:**

Eighty four percent of parents feel guilty about their child's screens. Almost none of them should, and the guilt is the problem.

The average parent now loses the equivalent of forty eight nights of sleep a year worrying about their child, and technology is the biggest single cause. Meanwhile the evidence on how to actually do this quietly favours something the guilt never lets you feel. Not the strictest home, and not the most permissive one. The guided middle. A 2026 study found the hands off families, not the structured ones, had children who felt they had the least freedom, and the paediatricians now weight content, co use and conversation over the number on the clock.

So the whole of what I am building is really one thing. A calm, evidence led alternative to a market that only sells you fear on one side and surveillance on the other.

And underneath it a belief I will say plainly, once, to close the week. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are poverty, the things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with too little help. Social media takes the blame because it is visible and rich. The real drivers sit in the dark. I cannot fix those from an app. But I can refuse to build one more thing that points at a tired parent and calls it a solution. What I can build is connection, because connection is the protection.

That is Guided Childhood. A staged pathway, the scripts for the hard moments, a family agreement, a balance that reads the pattern not the clock, an honest AI guide for you and not your child, and now the AI companion strand the week began with.
