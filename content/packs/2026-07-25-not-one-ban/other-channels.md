## MUMSNET
**Board:** Parenting

**Title:** I watched a panel of six child and tech experts this week and not one of them wanted a ban. It has changed how I am thinking about this.

I have a nine year old and a thirteen year old and I have been quietly in favour of just banning most of it, if I am honest. Then I watched a recording of a panel from UC Irvine, six people who have spent their whole careers studying children and technology, and not one of them reached for a ban. It has genuinely shifted me, so putting it here.

The thing that landed hardest was one of them, Mimi Ito, saying that the harm is not really in the device, it is in what the child is doing with it and what they are carrying when they open it. She called banning the phone to fix depression magical thinking, because the sadness or the bullying was never inside the app, that is just where it showed up. You delete the app and you have only moved the problem somewhere you can no longer see it.

Another one, Michael Levine, asked the question that stuck with me. When we get a ban wrong, who actually pays. And the answer is not the family with four spare devices and good wifi, they are fine. It is the kids for whom the school laptop was the only laptop. That made me think about it completely differently, because I had only been thinking about my own two.

The bit I have actually started doing is from a story one of them told about her gamer son and family dinner. They kept having screaming matches because dinner was ready and he could not leave his game without letting his teammates down. Nothing worked until she stopped issuing time limits and they just talked about it, and eventually he started asking how long till dinner and picking his game to fit. Connection, not control. I tried a version of that this week instead of just yelling turn it off, and it went better than a year of me nagging.

I am not saying it is all fine. The same panel wanted the tech companies held properly to account, and one of them said AI toys trying to befriend little kids are just inappropriate, which I agree with. But for the everyday stuff in my house, watching what my kids are doing and staying close to them is beating banning hands down. Anyone else come round to this, or am I being talked into something.

## REDDIT
**Subreddit:** r/ScienceBasedParenting

**Title:** A UC Irvine panel of six children-and-technology researchers this month, and the consistent position was staged guidance over bans. Notes and the evidence they leaned on.

I watched the UC Irvine panel on children and technology (July 2026, moderated by Prof Gillian Hayes, with Mimi Ito, Katie Salen, Michael Levine, Michael Preston and Roblox's Tammy Balik) and wanted to write up the throughline, because it is a useful counterweight to the ban discourse and the panellists were careful about their claims.

A few things up front so no one over reads this. It is a panel, so most of what follows is expert argument and framing, not new trial data. Where they cited specific evidence I have flagged it. And it was not a pro tech panel, they repeatedly said the companies have serious things to answer for.

The main threads.

1. It is the activity, not the device. Mimi Ito's framing, that most headlines run on technology determinism (social media as the independent variable causing an outcome) when the actual driver is how a child uses a general purpose tool and what they bring to it. She called ban-and-expect-the-harm-to-vanish magical thinking.

2. Bans have an equity cost that rarely gets stated. Michael Levine's point that removing devices from schools does not touch the well resourced child, it hits the child for whom the school device was the only device and the classroom was where mentorship and media literacy happened. Around one in five US school age children lack reliable home broadband (the homework gap), which is the group a blunt ban hits hardest. His frame was for which child, at which age, within which community, and do not treat children and screens as one undifferentiated problem.

3. Play and creativity are a quiet casualty of AI. Katie Salen's argument that when a single prompt generates the world, the level, the story, the character, it takes over developmental work that open play is supposed to do. Her healthy counter example was her daughter's friend group chat, healthy precisely because the kids control it.

4. Connection over control. The connected learning position, that top down blanket bans erode adult child trust when you most need it. Illustrated by Ito's family dinner and gaming story, and Roblox's guidance of a contract the child writes themselves.

5. Safe by design is doing real work where it is tried. Tammy Balik described Roblox's facial age estimation, communication off by default for the youngest cohort, and AI chat rephrasing that intercepts bullying patterns, plus neurodivergent family research done with Internet Matters and autism charities.

6. The blunt tool is underperforming on its own terms. On the Australia under 16 ban, follow up research this year found more than 85% of under 16s still using the covered platforms, most via self declared age, with daily use among 12 to 13 year olds barely changing. https://boingboing.net/2026/06/27/australias-social-media-ban-not-working-study-finds.html and the Molly Rose Foundation briefing https://mollyrosefoundation.org

Two caveats I would flag. The one in five broadband figure is a longstanding homework gap estimate and varies by population and year, so treat it as order of magnitude. And the Australia follow up is early, one year in, so it speaks to circumvention and enforcement failure more than to long run wellbeing effects.

My read. The panel is not saying tech is harmless, they are saying the device is the wrong unit of intervention. Target the activity, the child and the platform's design, and you get further than targeting the object. Interested in where people think the staged-guidance position is weakest, especially on enforceability.
