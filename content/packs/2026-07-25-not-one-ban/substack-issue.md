# Six experts, one stage, and not a single ban

## The issue

Six people who have spent their whole working lives studying children and technology sat on one stage at UC Irvine this month. A cultural anthropologist who has followed young people and tech for thirty years. A game designer who built a public school around digital learning. The person at Roblox whose entire job is making a children's platform safer. Two directors of research centres named after the woman who founded Sesame Street. Between them, more than a century of looking directly at this question.

And not one of them reached for a ban.

I want to sit on that, because it is not what you would guess from the headlines. If you only knew this debate from the news, you would think the serious, child protecting position was obvious. Get the phones out of schools. Bar the under sixteens. Draw the line and hold it. That is the story that photographs well and feels like courage. What you would not know is that the people who study the actual children mostly think it aims at the wrong target, and quietly hurts the children it claims to protect.

Let me walk you through what they said instead, because it is more useful than anything a ban can offer, and it is the whole of what I believe put better than I usually manage.

### It was never the device

Mimi Ito, the anthropologist, named the mistake underneath most of the fear. She calls it technology determinism. The headline says social media, the cause, produces depression, the effect. The device is treated as a force that arrives in a child's life and knocks them off balance. It is clean, it is frightening, and she has spent thirty years watching it not be true. What actually drives the harm, she argued, is how a child is using the thing. These are general purpose tools, and the same app builds one child up and wears another down depending entirely on what they are doing with it and what they are carrying when they open it.

The alternative she called magical thinking. Ban the phone and you expect the depression to lift, the bullying to stop, the children to look up and talk to each other again. It is seductive because it is simple, and it is a fantasy, because none of those problems lived in the device in the first place. You cannot delete a child's loneliness by deleting an app. You have only removed the place it was showing up.

If you take one habit from this issue, take that one. Before you fear the screen, ask what the child is doing on it, and what they brought to it. The screen is almost never the story. It is where the story becomes visible.

### The question that should end the ban debate

Michael Levine, who has spent decades on children and media, asked the question I have not stopped turning over since. Not is a ban good or bad. Who bears the cost when we get it wrong.

Because someone always does, and it is never who you would hope. Strip the screens out of a school, and the child in the comfortable home with four spare devices and fast broadband is completely fine. Nothing changes for them. The cost lands on the child for whom the school device was the only device, and the classroom was the only place the mentorship, the media literacy, the guidance ever happened. Around one in five school age children in the richest country on earth still do not have reliable broadband at home. A blunt ban does not touch the well resourced child. It takes the ladder away from the one already furthest down.

So Levine offered three questions in place of the single blunt one. Not ban or no ban, but for which child, at which age, and within which community. A rule that genuinely helps a fourteen year old in one house can quietly harm a nine year old in another. The most dangerous move in this whole debate, he said, is to treat children and screens as one undifferentiated problem with one solution. That is precisely the move a ban makes. It treats every child as the same child, and the children who are not the average child are the ones it fails.

### The thing quietly being taken away

Katie Salen, the game designer, pointed at a loss almost nobody is even worried about yet, because we are all busy worrying about screen time. Play. The open, invented, child made kind.

Her example was a summer AI camp where children played noughts and crosses by writing instructions for an AI agent to follow. They played the game not as a child plays, but as a machine would. And she watched, in that, the shape of a much bigger shift. With a single prompt now, AI will build the whole game world, finish the story, fill out the character from the barest outline a child gives it. It feels like magic. It also quietly does the exact work that play is meant to do, the improvising, the deciding, the making it up as you go, the developmental labour that turns a child into someone who can create rather than only choose.

Then she described her own daughter living in two worlds on the same afternoon. In one, AI curates and optimises everything she touches, a smooth menu of things chosen for her. In the other, a group chat with five school friends that Katie called one of the most playful spaces she had ever seen. Not because AI was absent from it, but because the girls own it completely. They make the rules. They decide who is in and who is out and what is funny. That, she argued, is the space worth protecting, and it is precisely the one no optimisation engine can ever hand a child, because the entire value of it is that they built it themselves.

I want to be fair to the other side of this, because the panel was, and because it is where I actually live. AI can be wonderful for a child. Michael Preston framed the real test cleanly. Does a given product diminish the capacities we want for children, their creativity, their relationships, their agency, or does it feed them. Some AI genuinely feeds them. The question is never the technology in the abstract. It is what the thing does to the child's own creative work.

### Connection, not control

The most useful parenting moment of the whole evening was not a study. It was a story about pasta going cold.

Mimi Ito described the fight she kept having with her gamer son. Family dinner was sacred in their house. But he played long team games, the kind where leaving mid match lets down five other people who have invested two hours. So dinner would be ready, he could not come without betraying his team, the pasta went cold, everyone was cross, and her only tool was shouting. Until one day, unprompted, he started asking how long until dinner, and choosing his game to fit. The fix was never a screen time limit. It was connection and conversation, and it was mostly led by him.

That is the heart of what these researchers call connected learning. Blunt, top down, one directional bans, Ito argued, erode trust between adults and children at the very moment connection matters most. Tammy Balik of Roblox told the same truth from inside the industry. Their guidance to parents is a contract the child writes themselves, taking the lead on what is fair, because it treats the child as someone with a stake rather than a suspect. When a child feels seen, you get the conversation. When they feel policed, you get the closed door and the hidden phone.

This is exactly the principle we built DiGi on, our AI guide for children. DiGi never allows and never denies. Faced with a child, it does not lay down the law and it does not look away. It stays curious, keeps the child talking, and returns them to a trusted adult with a calmer way in. I will be careful about the claim, because the panel was careful too. This does not make a child happier and it does not fix anything, and I will not pretend it does. What connection actually buys you is narrower and more honest. A child who brings you the problem while it is still small, instead of hiding it until it is not. In a world your child can climb out of the second your back is turned, that is worth more than any wall you can build.

### The ban that is not even working

And here is the fact that should give the ban's supporters real pause, because we can now measure it. Australia barred under sixteens from social media in December. By the follow up research this year, more than eighty five percent of under sixteens were still using the platforms the law was meant to close to them. Most through their own accounts. Most getting in by simply typing a false birthday. Daily use among twelve and thirteen year olds barely moved. The biggest, boldest ban in the world, and the children walked around it in an afternoon. Ito's image for why was the sharpest of the night. Social media and phones are not something you can cleanly extract from a child's life. They are more like a circulatory system, the good and the bad running through the same vessels. You cannot cut it out without cutting the child off from the connection they need.

I have spent this whole issue arguing that guidance beats banning, so let me say plainly what guidance is, because I do not want to be mistaken for someone who thinks the answer is to do nothing. It is two things the panel returned to again and again. Education, preparing the child for what does not go away, real digital literacy taught in schools, which Balik said her own teen council keeps pleading for and mostly cannot get. And safe by design, forcing the platforms to actually build for children. Facial age estimation. Communication switched off by default for the youngest. Systems that catch and rephrase bullying before it lands. Roblox is doing versions of all of it. Most platforms are doing none.

And this is where proportion cuts the other way, because none of this was an apology for big tech, and neither am I. Levine said flatly that the companies have a great deal to answer for. Some AI products are now trying to build relationships with very young children, which he called plainly inappropriate, and he is right. The point was never that the industry is fine. It is that punishing the child by confiscating the tool, while the company that designed the tool faces nothing at all, gets the target backwards. The child is the one part of this with the least power and the most to lose.

### What this actually asks of you

Carry this out of the noise. A ban aims at the child and misses, and the research now shows it missing. Education aims at the child and equips them for the thing that is not going anywhere. Accountability aims at the platform, where the power actually sits.

So the thing to do tonight is not to build a wall your child clears with a fake birthday. It is smaller and it lasts longer. Sit with them while they do something real online, and get curious rather than suspicious. Ask what they are making, not just what they are watching. Let them teach you the thing they are good at, because a child who has taught you once will come to you when it goes wrong. That is the whole job, and it is the one thing that travels with them into every new app and past every birthday a ban was ever going to draw.

I cannot promise you it makes childhood easier. Nobody honestly can. What it builds is a child who is prepared instead of merely blocked, and a house with the door open instead of a wall your child has already learned to climb.
