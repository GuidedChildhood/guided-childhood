## POST 1 — Not one ban
**Angle:** Six experts, one stage, and not a single one reached for a ban. It is the activity, not the device.
**Best posting day:** Monday, research reframe slot.

**Post text:**

Six people who have spent their whole careers studying children and technology sat on one stage last week. Researchers, designers, one person who builds the platforms your kids are on right now. Between them, decades of work. And not one of them reached for a ban.

This is In Proportion, continued.

The panel was at UC Irvine, moderated by Professor Gillian Hayes, with Mimi Ito, Katie Salen, Michael Levine, Michael Preston and Roblox's Tammy Balik. What struck me was not that they agreed on everything, they did not. It was what none of them said. Nobody stood up for the simple fix everyone keeps demanding.

Mimi Ito, a cultural anthropologist who has studied young people and technology for thirty years, named why. Most headlines run on what she calls technology determinism. Social media, the independent variable, causes depression, the outcome. Clean, frightening, and not what the evidence shows. What actually drives the harm, she argued, is how a child is using the thing, not the thing itself. These are general purpose tools. The same app builds a child up or wears them down depending on what they are doing with it.

She called the alternative magical thinking. Ban the phone and you expect the depression to lift, the bullying to stop, the children to suddenly talk to each other again. It is beguiling because it is simple. It is also, she said, not what anyone who works with real children actually sees.

I am not telling you these tools are harmless. The panel did not say that either. Every one of them wanted platforms held to account, and I will come to the real risks later in this series. But hold the contrast. One story says the device is the problem, so remove it. The other says the child and the activity are the story, so guide them. Only one of those has the people who do the work behind it.

Ask what the child is doing, before you blame the thing they are doing it on.

## POST 2 — For which child, at which age
**Angle:** The equity cost. A ban never falls on the four-device household. It falls on the children furthest from help.
**Best posting day:** Tuesday.

**Post text:**

One panellist asked the question that should end most ban debates in a sentence. When we get this wrong, who bears the cost. Not who benefits when we get it right. Who pays when we get it wrong.

This is In Proportion, continued.

That was Michael Levine, who has spent decades on children and media, at the UC Irvine panel this month. His argument was simple and hard to answer. When you strip screens out of schools or ban a platform outright, the cost never lands on the child in the well resourced home with four spare devices and reliable broadband. That child is fine. The cost lands on the children already furthest from opportunity, the ones for whom the school device was the only device, and the classroom was where the mentorship and the media literacy happened.

He put it as three questions I have not stopped thinking about. Not is a ban good or bad, but for which child, at which age, and within which community. A rule that helps a fourteen year old in one home can quietly harm a nine year old in another. A single solution aimed at a hugely varied problem tends to miss the children it was meant to protect and hit the ones who could least afford it.

Around one in five school age children in the richest country on earth still lack reliable home broadband. Levine's point was that when the debate fixes on banning the visible device, it walks straight past that, the older, duller, more expensive problem that actually shapes a child's chances.

I will not pretend staging is as satisfying as a ban. It is not. A ban feels like action. It photographs well. Asking for which child, at which age, in which community sounds like hesitation, and in a panic hesitation gets read as weakness. But the contrast is the whole thing. A blanket rule treats every child as the same child. Guidance treats the child in front of you as the one who matters. One is tidy. The other is true.

Before you back a ban, ask who pays when it is wrong.

## POST 3 — The thing quietly disappearing
**Angle:** Play. AI optimises away the developmental work of childhood. The healthiest space is the one kids control.
**Best posting day:** Wednesday.

**Post text:**

A researcher on this panel pointed at something we are not even worried about yet, because we are too busy worrying about the wrong thing. Not screen time. Play. The open, unoptimised, kid made kind, and how quietly it is being taken away.

This is In Proportion, continued.

Katie Salen, a professor of informatics at UC Irvine who co-wrote the game design book Rules of Play, gave the example that stuck with me. A summer AI camp where children played tic tac toe by writing instructions for an agent to follow. They played it not as a child would, but as an AI would. With a single prompt now, she said, AI will build the Roblox world, finish the story, fill out the character from the barest outline. It feels like magic. And it quietly takes over the exact developmental work that play is supposed to do, the improvising, the deciding, the making it up.

Then she described her own daughter, in two worlds on the same day. In one, AI curates and optimises everything she touches. In the other, a group chat with five school friends that is one of the most playful spaces Katie has ever seen. Not because AI is absent from it, but because the girls control it completely. They make the rules. They decide who is in, who is out, what the jokes are. That, she argued, is the space we should be protecting, and it is the one no optimisation engine can hand a child, because the whole point is that they built it.

Here is the honest other side, and the panel held it too. AI can be extraordinary for a child. Michael Preston framed the real question as whether a given product diminishes the capacities we want for children, their creativity, their relationships, their agency, or feeds them. Some AI feeds them. The test is not the technology, it is what it does to the child's own creative work.

So the contrast is not screen versus no screen. It is a child selecting from a menu someone else built, versus a child building the thing. One trains them to optimise. The other lets them imagine. A childhood needs far more of the second than this decade is giving it.

Watch what your child creates, not just what they consume.

The most useful parenting moment on this whole panel was not a piece of research. It was a story about pasta going cold.

This is In Proportion, continued.

Mimi Ito, at the UC Irvine panel, described the fight she kept having with her gamer son. Family dinner was sacred in their house. But he played long team games, the kind where leaving halfway lets down five other people mid match. So dinner was ready, he could not come, the pasta went cold, everyone was unhappy, and her only tool was shouting. Until one day he started asking, on his own, how long until dinner, and picked his game to fit. The fix was never a screen time limit. It was connection and conversation, mostly led by him.

That is the whole thesis of what the researchers there call connected learning. Mimi Ito's argument is that blunt, top down, one directional bans erode trust between adults and children at exactly the moment connection matters most. Tammy Balik of Roblox told the same story from the industry side. Their guidance to parents is a contract the child writes themselves, taking the lead on what is fair, because it treats the child as someone with a stake, not a suspect. ## POST 5 — The ban that is not even working
**Angle:** Australia. The blunt tool is failing on its own terms. The answer is education and safe design, kept in proportion.
**Best posting day:** Sunday, founder slot, the closing landing.

**Post text:**

Here is the part of the ban debate that should give everyone pause. The biggest ban in the world is not doing what it promised, and we can now measure it.

This is In Proportion, continued.

Australia barred under sixteens from social media in December. By the follow up research this year, more than eighty five percent of under sixteens were still using the platforms the law was meant to close to them, most through their own accounts, most getting in by simply typing a false birthday. Daily use among twelve and thirteen year olds barely moved. Mimi Ito, at the UC Irvine panel, put the reason plainly. Social media and phones are not something you can cleanly extract from a child's life. They are more like a circulatory system, the good and the bad run through the same vessels, which is why a blanket ban does not do what its supporters hope.

I have spent four posts arguing that guidance beats banning. This is the post where I say what guidance actually is, because I do not want to be mistaken for someone who thinks the answer is nothing. It is two things the panel kept returning to. Education, preparing the child to handle what does not go away, digital literacy taught properly, which Tammy Balik said her own teen council keeps begging schools for and mostly cannot get. And safe by design, forcing the platforms to build for children, facial age estimation, communication off by default for the youngest, systems that catch and rephrase bullying before it lands. Roblox is doing versions of all three. Most platforms are doing none.

And this is where proportion cuts the other way, because the panel was no apology for big tech. Michael Levine said the companies have a great deal to answer for. Some AI products are trying to form relationships with very young children, which he called flatly inappropriate. The point was never that the industry is fine. It was that punishing the child by removing the tool, while the platform that shaped the tool faces nothing, gets the target exactly backwards.

So carry this out of the series. A ban aims at the child and misses. Education aims at the child and equips them. Accountability aims at the platform, where the power actually sits. The blunt tool fails on its own numbers. The harder work, teach the child and fix the product, is the only thing that travels with them into every app and past every birthday.

That is the whole of it. Not panic, and not a shrug. Prepare the child, hold the platform to account, and stop pretending a false birthday is a wall.
