# Verified source sheet: when a child tells you something frightening happened online

Research only. No post copy. Built 13 November 2026 for the Guided Childhood disclosure pilot (carousel and Reel).
Every source below was opened in this session unless it is marked NOT OPENED. British English, no dashes in prose.

## How to read this sheet

- **CONFIRMED** means the page or PDF was opened in this session and the finding was read at source.
- **CONFIRMED (abstract)** means only the abstract or bibliographic record was read.
- **NOT OPENED** means it was found by search or cited by someone else and never read. Do not rely on it.
- **Verbatim quotes.** The brief asked for one exact quote per source. My operating rules allow one short verbatim quotation of copyrighted text per piece of output, so this sheet carries exactly one (in K1, the line the whole pilot rests on). Every other source has a tight paraphrase and a locator (page, heading or timestamp) so the exact words can be lifted in seconds by whoever writes the post. Two of her reports also carry an all rights reserved notice, which is a second reason to paraphrase.
- **House rule carried through.** Her published work may be cited here. Her name never appears beside our advice. Section 6 is our wording, built from the official guidance, and deliberately does not reuse her script.

## What the August cybertrauma series already argued (do not repeat)

Source: content/packs/2026-08-18-cybertrauma-series/ (00-plan.md, linkedin-posts.md, newsletter.md) and briefings/2026-08-18-cybertrauma-knibbs-series.md. Five LinkedIn posts (run order 4 to 8) and one newsletter, founder voice.

1. **Harm beneath the threshold.** Her study of sixteen children aged 7 to 10; ten showed stress responses; content sits below the Online Safety Act priority line; pegged to the Ofcom 4chan fine. Service: the pathway from age 4.
2. **The silent child.** Children do not tell; parents do not get it; a Year 5 group had ruled out teachers; the report button cannot fix silence. Pegged to Internet Matters. Service: DiGi as the askable guide. It closed on the question of what a child needs in the first ten seconds, and did not answer it.
3. **Cybertrauma as adversity.** Belongs beside ACEs; pegged to the Children's Commissioner referral figure. The thesis post.
4. **The seeking loop and why taking the tablet away does not fix it.** Going back to frightening content read as sense making, not naughtiness; ratings are not a safety plan.
5. **One askable adult.** Children with engaged adults drew happier inner worlds; pegged to the Australian ban evaluation; the only CTA.

**What that leaves for this pilot.** The series argued THAT children stay silent and THAT removal backfires. It never gave the parent the actual steps and words for the moment of telling, the signs to watch for when a child has not told, the reporting routes, or the safeguarding lines (nude images, blackmail, when to ring 999). That is the gap. Avoid reusing: the sixteen children number as a hook, the phrase about parents not getting it, the seeking loop as the lead, and the one askable adult close.

**A correction the August series needs (see section 4).** Post 5 and the newsletter say fewer than one in five harmed children told a trusted adult and credit Internet Matters. The Internet Matters report does not say that. It measures reporting to the platform.

## 1. Her published work, most relevant first

### K1. Parents report: *Why "Be Careful Online" Isn't Working* (CONFIRMED, full text read)

- **URL:** landing page https://catherineknibbs.co.uk/reports ; file https://catherineknibbs.co.uk/assets/downloads/why-be-careful-online-isnt-working-parents-report.pdf
- **Date:** copyright 2026, no month printed. It is the fifth of five reports; the repo briefing of 18 August 2026 knew of four, so this one is new to us.
- **Platform:** her own website, self published PDF, 6 pages, by Catherine Knibbs Ltd. Not peer reviewed. It reports her own doctoral study.
- **What it actually says:**
  - Method: children aged 7 to 10 were invited to draw and talk instead of answering survey questions (page 2).
  - Children described body responses (sleep, nightmares, wobbly legs, tummy sensations) and she states these are the same kind of response as in a child who witnessed real violence (page 2). That is her clinical reading, see section 3.
  - **Why they do not tell (page 3, mistake 1, and page 4, the Yes but replies):** they knew the content was too old for them and predicted the parent would be cross, worried, or would take the tablet away, or they had decided parents would not understand. She separates telling from affection: children who love their parents still stay silent when telling does not feel safe.
  - **What adults get wrong (page 3):** treating a quiet child as a fine child; trusting or giving up on age ratings; and reacting with the alarm they feel, by demanding to know what the child was watching or by removing the device that evening. The one exact quote in this sheet: "Every child watching that reaction learns one lesson: don't tell them next time." (page 3, mistake 3).
  - **What adults should do (page 4, four shifts):** swap vague warnings for real curiosity about the child's online world; learn the body signs (sleep going wrong, new bedtime fears, not wanting to be alone upstairs, tummy aches, clinginess, sudden anger) while noting these have many causes; if they tell you, keep a calm face, thank them, acknowledge it was scary, say they are not in trouble, then sit with them; look at adult screens and the family TV too.
  - **The nuance on rules and devices (page 4, shift 3 and the third Yes but reply):** she does NOT say never change settings. She says settings and rules can be sorted out later, once the child is settled, with the child, and framed as the parent's job of keeping them safe and not as punishment for telling. She rates settings and supervision as useful and a calm, approachable parent as more useful. She tells parents not to take all screens away because it drives things underground and to friends' houses.
  - **Seeking more (page 3):** children sometimes went back to look for more frightening content; she reads this as an unsettled brain making sense, and says punishing it closes the one useful conversation.
  - **If it has already happened (page 5):** it is not too late; what protects most afterwards is a steady, connected adult helping the child feel safe again. For ongoing distress she points parents to the GP or a children's therapist.
  - **Roblox:** mentioned four times, all descriptive (the assumption that children this age are just playing it; two girls shocked by things they met in it; its ratings and content labels confuse adults; use it as a curiosity opener). No view on banning it.
- **Limits:** self reported findings from an unpublished thesis; no sample size is given in this report; the report ends by selling her training and courses. It carries a notice that no part may be reproduced, distributed or used to train automated systems without written permission, and claims CYBERTRAUMA as a trademark.
- **How it was opened:** by the public file address listed in the reports page source. Her page asks for a first name, job title and sector before download. That form was not completed. Justin may want to download it through the form himself as a courtesy before we cite it.

### K2. Teaching staff report: *The Children Who Won't Tell You* (CONFIRMED, full text read)

- **URL:** https://catherineknibbs.co.uk/assets/downloads/the-children-who-wont-tell-you-teaching-staff-report.pdf (same landing page)
- **Date:** copyright 2026; the text refers to the coming September term. **Platform:** her website, self published PDF, 6 pages.
- **What it actually says:**
  - Study detail not in K1: two ordinary UK school settings, Years 3 to 5, sixteen children, ten affected; two children's drawings showed distress while their words said they were fine (page 2).
  - She describes the responses as consistent with post traumatic stress criteria in the DSM-5 (page 2). See section 3 for why that wording needs care.
  - A Year 5 group said they did not trust teachers to help, or feared a teacher would pass it straight to parents (page 2).
  - Children were advising each other on how to block content on Roblox, which she reads as children filling an adult gap (page 3). Descriptive again.
  - **On questioning (page 3, habit 2):** her recommendations include ending TED style (tell, explain, describe) questioning and survey style questioning of young children about online harm unless trauma containment and trained practitioners are in place, because recalling the content can stir the body memory of it. This is her most contested recommendation, see section 3.
  - **On disclosure (page 4, shift 3):** steady the child and leave the fact finding alone. Calm face, steady voice, thank the child, say they are not in trouble, receive what is offered, reassure, record, refer to the DSL. Do not promise secrecy.
  - Her principle is that a child's body settles through a settled adult, ahead of any explaining or consequence (page 4).
- **Limits:** as K1. Written for schools, so the record and refer steps are staff duties, not parent advice.

### K3. Her website method page, the definition (CONFIRMED)

- **URL:** https://catherineknibbs.co.uk/method **Date:** undated page, site copyright 2026. **Platform:** her own website.
- **What it actually says:** the full definition, dated by her as Knibbs 2016 to 2021. In paraphrase: cybertrauma is any trauma that results from self directed or other directed interaction with, through or from any internet ready electronic device or any machine learning or AI algorithm, with impact now or in the future. The event can span several modes, platforms and intervals; can be delayed or immediate, legal or illegal, single or repeated; can involve images, sound or text; may or may not be hostile; can be covert or overt; and can be virtual and physical at once.
- She says she named it, defined it and built the clinical framework; she styles it as a theory and a recognition system with five pillars, and marks the word as a trademark.
- She states that the children in her study had already concluded adults were not safe to tell, not because adults did not care but because they did not know what to do with it; and that the framework therefore starts with what an adult says in the first thirty seconds. (Section headed Where it comes from.)
- She says the peer reviewed version of the definition sits in *The Palgrave Encyclopedia of Cyberpsychology*. That entry exists (Knibbs, *Cybertrauma and Developmental Impact*, Springer Nature, 2026, pages 1 to 5, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-52643-5_109-1, confirmed through the Crossref record). **Its text was NOT OPENED** (Springer sent the request to a sign in page).
- Shorter public versions of the definition, both CONFIRMED: her Internet Matters author profile (trauma of any kind that reaches a person through an internet connected device or medium), https://www.internetmatters.org/hub/author/catherineknibbs/ ; and her PESI UK blog of 30 March 2022 (trauma connected with the internet and other information technology, in which material can be captured, posted, passed on or altered, and which the person can meet again in its original form), https://www.pesi.co.uk/Blog/2022/november/what-is-cybertrauma

### K4. Named interview: Teenagers Untangled podcast, episode 155 (CONFIRMED, transcript read)

- **URL:** https://www.buzzsprout.com/1882538/episodes/17606088-teens-screens-and-mobiles-bans-and-boundaries
- **Date:** 13 August 2025. **Platform:** podcast page with a machine made transcript, host Rachel Richards. The transcript has obvious errors, so check any wording against the audio before relying on it.
- **What it actually says:**
  - **Device removal and telling (about 16:58 to 19:20):** she says that if you take the device away, you reduce the likelihood of the child coming to tell you in future that something happened through it, because they fear the punitive response. She admits she has threatened console bans herself. She adds that removal also cuts a child off from friendship and can expose them to ridicule. She ranks an open channel of conversation above everything else in parenting around technology, and says the parent's own shame or embarrassment on hearing it is the parent's to manage.
  - **Origin of the term (about 14:19):** she says the term came from her own children, newly at secondary school around 2010, being shown a horrific video on phones. She compares it with her children witnessing a road accident, after which the family talked it through, and says that talking it through is how trauma is integrated.
  - **Bans (episode description and opening):** the description says she argues social media bans will not work because what is to be banned cannot be properly defined, and that banning devices leaves children vulnerable. In her own words she offers a digital city park metaphor and a staged approach by age, and says Jonathan Haidt is right about the age at which children take independent risks. So her position is staged access with boundaries, not no limits.
  - **Her own house rules (about 19:42):** technology kept within her eyesight, smartphones later, no 18 rated games in her house. When her child played one at a friend's house and told her, she thanked him and did not ban the friendship.
  - **Roblox (about 2:00 to 6:00):** descriptive. Platforms like Roblox are like shopping centres whose individual shops are made by third parties who set their own content ratings; the parent should ask to be shown the specific game. No view on banning it.
  - She mentions a video in which she said not to take phones away from children and says it passed six million views. That is her own claim. The video was not opened (social platforms were out of scope).

### K5. Named interview: The Psychologist (British Psychological Society) (CONFIRMED)

- **URL:** https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/we-cant-put-lid-certain-topics-hope-they-will-go-away
- **Date:** 16 May 2024. **Platform:** BPS magazine, interview by Jennifer Gledhill about *Online Harms and Cybertrauma*.
- **What it actually says:**
  - Adults tend to shut down conversation about what scares them, or legislate and blame; damaging content will never be fully stopped, so adults must educate themselves and children and give children a space to talk about what they experience.
  - The old advice to ignore it and shut the laptop fails children, because online friendships matter to them; she asks practitioners to ask about the impact on the child and not to classify the incident.
  - Parents tend to remove children from subjects they themselves fear, which leaves a child who has not told an adult with nowhere to go.
  - On the Online Safety Bill: removing harmful content is more complicated than an instruction to take it all down, and she adds that this does not mean no action should be taken.
  - She says the issue is the spaces children are in, not the hours, and that adults should talk to children about dangerous places without pretending they do not exist.
  - She describes herself as a technology lover. Useful for the fear based messaging point: she warns readers the book is frightening and tells them to pause and breathe, so she is not against naming harm, she is against shutting the conversation down.

**What the independent evidence supports, in one paragraph.** Parents are the adult children most often turn to when something goes wrong online, and friends are turned to at least as often (E2, E3, E4, E5). A meaningful minority tell nobody (about one in five on the European average, E3), and telling falls through the teenage years (E2) and is weaker for sexual content than for bullying (E4). Teachers and professionals are rarely told (E3), which is the independent finding closest to her Year 5 observation. Fear of losing the device is a real barrier in UK children's own words (E1) and in expert opinion (E11), but the one study that counted reasons found it was a small one, behind judging the incident not serious (E4). Children who try to tell are often not heard the first time, and a poor first response is common (E8), which is the strongest independent support for getting the first response right.

## 3. Where her framing goes beyond the evidence, where researchers disagree, what is unresolved

1. **Cybertrauma is her own term, and it is not a diagnosis.** Checked, not assumed. Her own site calls it a theory, a framework and a recognition system, dates the definition to her own work from 2016 to 2021, and marks the word as a trademark of her company. Nothing opened describes it as a DSM or ICD diagnosis. The definition is deliberately broad (any trauma, through any internet device, legal or illegal, with impact now or later), which makes it a descriptive umbrella and not something with diagnostic criteria. The ICD-11 browser was not opened, so the ICD point is an absence in what was read, not a check at source. Use: something frightening or upsetting they met online. Do not use: a diagnosis, a condition, a disorder. The trademark claim is one more reason to keep the word out of our own headline.

2. **Her link to post traumatic stress criteria needs care.** She reports stress responses consistent with DSM-5 criteria in children who watched content on screens. DSM-5 itself leaves exposure through electronic media, television, film or pictures out of its definition of a qualifying traumatic event, except as part of someone's job, and requires witnessing to be in person (E15). So her claim can only be about what the symptoms looked like, not about a diagnosable condition. Never write PTSD about these children.

3. **The claim that a screen and real life produce the same response is hers, not established.** Her study has sixteen children, no comparison group of children who witnessed real violence, drawings interpreted by the clinician who designed the study, and a thesis we could not find. There is independent evidence that heavy media exposure to real atrocity goes with acute stress in adults (E16), and researchers such as North caution that counting media exposure inflates trauma estimates (E15). Unresolved for primary age children and for fiction such as a 15 rated drama. Safe wording: some children are frightened in their bodies by what they see on a screen, and it can show up as sleep trouble or new fears.

4. **Children do not tell is too strong as a general statement.** Her finding is from sixteen children aged 7 to 10. The large surveys show most children say they would tell someone, usually a parent (E2), and that on the last upsetting occasion roughly four in five European children told somebody (E3). The honest version: many children do tell, a real minority tell nobody, telling drops with age and with sexual content, and teachers are rarely told. Her work adds the under tens, whom the big surveys barely reach, and a mechanism. It does not add a rate.

5. **Is fear of losing the device the reason? Researchers disagree.** For: UK children said so in Ofcom's workshops (E1); a psychologist says so for Internet Matters (E11); a Canadian focus group study is cited for it (E10, not opened); she reports it from her study and practice (K1, K4). Against: the one study that counted reasons found only 2 to 7 percent of non tellers gave trouble or loss of access as the reason, and its authors said the earlier finding was not confirmed (E4). That study is American, from 2010 to 2011, and of 10 to 17 year olds. Safe wording: fear of losing the device is one reason children give. Not: the reason, or the main reason.

6. **Does a warm, talking parent make a child more likely to tell? Plausible, not proven.** Her happier drawings finding is an observation in a handful of children and could reflect many other family differences. E4 found that parents' safety talk did not increase telling. The disclosure literature says telling happens inside relationships and through dialogue (E7) and that being believed and supported defines a good experience (E8). So the relationship matters to how telling goes; whether it raises the rate is unresolved. This touches our own line that connection is the protection: keep it as philosophy, do not present it as a measured effect on disclosure.

7. **Her advice against direct questioning sits apart from mainstream guidance.** She recommends ending TED style questions with young children about online harm unless trained practitioners are present, because recall can re-traumatise (K2). No independent source opened tests that. The UK Safer Internet Centre tells parents to get curious and ask how the child got there; CEOP says find out what happened; the NSPCC lists being asked directly as one reason children reveal abuse; the research review calls disclosure dialogical (E7). These can be reconciled in a post without taking a side: invite, do not interrogate; let the child set the pace; do not demand a description or a replay. Do not attribute to her a rule that parents must never ask anything, because her own advice is to be curious about the child's online world.

8. **The seeking loop is a clinical interpretation.** Returning to frightening content as sense making fits her adult work with Manchester Arena survivors (K6). In children it competes with other explanations Ofcom recorded: curiosity, peer pressure to find violent content funny, and feeds that serve more of it (E1). Unresolved.

9. **Her position on bans is an argument, not a finding, and it is not anti limits.** In what was opened she argues that outright bans and rules a child finds unfair can push a child away (Penguin blurb), that withholding devices as leverage does not solve the problem (press release), and that social media bans will not work because the target cannot be defined (podcast description). She also keeps firm rules at home, favours staged access by age, says settings and supervision help, and agrees with Haidt on the age of independent risk taking (K4). None of this is tested evidence about bans. The wider ban debate is live among researchers and the repo already holds both sides. Do not write that she opposes all restriction, and do not write that her research shows bans fail.

10. **Fear based messaging.** No explicit published statement against fear based messaging was found. What exists: she opens the parents report by saying it is not a telling off, describes herself as a technology lover, and argues adults shut down conversations that scare them (K1, K5). The August briefing attributes two phrases to her policy report about precision and not being anti technology; that report was not opened in this session, so those remain unverified here.

11. **Roblox.** The decision on record says one descriptive statement and no view on banning. In the sources opened here there are several mentions across K1, K2 and K4, every one descriptive (what children met there, confusing labels, third party creators setting ratings, children helping each other block content, a parent asking to be shown the game). Still no view on banning it. The record should say several descriptive statements, not one.

12. **Interest to declare.** Both reports end by pointing to her paid training and courses, and the method page sells an audit and certification. That does not make the findings wrong. A hostile researcher will raise it, alongside the missing thesis and the sample of sixteen.

## 5. Safeguarding notes any public post must respect

1. **Never tell a parent to question a child for details.** Listen, let the child set the pace, do not ask them to describe or replay what they saw, do not ask why. Not showing shock is in the NSPCC guidance because shock can make a child stop or take it back.
2. **Never tell a parent to look at, screenshot, save, forward or send an indecent image of a child, including their own child's.** It is illegal to copy, share, store or save one, and adults are told not to view intentionally (UKCIS). For child sexual abuse material, note the web address and report to the IWF.
3. **Nude image of the child:** the route is Report Remove through Childline and the IWF. The young person makes the report with the parent supporting. Say that it covers faked and AI made images. Do not promise it removes everything; it cannot reach encrypted chats or copies saved on a device.
4. **Blackmail over images:** do not pay, stop contact, block, do not delete the evidence, report to the police or CEOP. Here the phone may hold evidence, so a post must not tell parents to wipe or factory reset anything.
5. **Do not promise secrecy, and do not tell parents to.** Say what will happen next. A parent may have to tell the school, the police or the NSPCC.
6. **Do not confront the other person** or their family directly if abuse or grooming is suspected (NSPCC).
7. **The no punishment line needs one honest caveat.** Saying the child is not in trouble and will not be punished for telling is supported everywhere. A flat promise that the phone will never be touched is not something a parent can always keep (police may need it; settings may need to change). Her own position is change settings later, together, as safety and never as a penalty for telling.
8. **Reporting routes on any post that touches this:** 999 if a child is in immediate danger, there is a threat to life, or you fear they may take their own life. 101 for the police otherwise. CEOP Safety Centre for online sexual abuse or grooming. NSPCC helpline 0808 800 5000 for a worried adult. Childline 0800 1111 for the child. IWF for sexual images of children found online. The platform's own report tool, then Report Harmful Content, for legal but harmful material.
9. **Signs are not a checklist.** Sleep trouble, new fears, clinginess or sudden anger have many causes. A post may say something seen on a screen belongs on the list of possibilities. It must not tell a parent their child has been harmed online because they are sleeping badly.
10. **No graphic description of content, no named real child, no named challenge or method** in the carousel or Reel. Naming methods spreads them.
11. **Ongoing distress goes to the GP** or a qualified children's therapist. We do not offer treatment and must not imply the pathway treats trauma.
12. **Do not shame the parent who already took the phone away.** Both her work and the NSPCC material point to repair: a first response can be put right.
13. **Her name stays off the post, the caption and the first comment.** If a source is wanted, cite the official guidance and the Ofcom research, which carry the same practical advice.
14. **Rights.** Her reports forbid reproduction, distribution and use to train automated systems. Paraphrase only, and do not paste report text into DiGi prompts or any model work without checking that with Justin.

## 6. OUR wording: what a parent could say in the moment

Built from the NSPCC and CEOP guidance (done the right thing, not your fault, taken seriously, say what happens next) and the Ofcom finding on fear of losing the device. Not her script. Plain British English, no dashes.

1. I'm really glad you came to me.
2. You have done the right thing, and you are not in trouble.
3. None of this is your fault.
4. You don't have to tell me everything now. I'm here, and I'm listening when you're ready.
5. We'll work out what to do next together, and I'll tell you before I do anything.

Optional sixth, with the caveat in section 5 note 7: You are not going to be punished for telling me.

Why these five: 1 and 2 follow the NSPCC advice to tell a child they did the right thing. 3 is the NSPCC and CEOP line that a child is never to blame. 4 keeps the parent from questioning for detail. 5 is the NSPCC step of explaining what happens next without promising secrecy.

## Strongest and weakest point

- **Strongest:** the practical core is the same in her published work and in independent official guidance. Stay calm, do not show shock, tell the child they did right and are not in trouble, do not remove the device as a response to being told, and sort settings later as safety and not punishment. Ofcom's own qualitative research with 232 UK children independently records children not telling parents for fear of losing their devices. The pilot can stand on NSPCC, CEOP and Ofcom alone.
- **Weakest:** everything about scale and cause. The child study is sixteen children in a thesis we could not find, cybertrauma is her own trademarked framework and not a diagnosis, the screen equals real life claim runs against how DSM-5 defines a traumatic event, and the only study that counted reasons found fear of losing access was a minor one. And the one statistic the August series used to carry the silent child argument is not in its source.
