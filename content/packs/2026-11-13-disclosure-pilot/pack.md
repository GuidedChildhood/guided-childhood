## The story in one line

When a child says something scary happened online, the first response decides
whether they keep talking. Steady face, five sentences, settings later and
together, and know where to report.

## Do not quote

- Fewer than one in five children told a trusted adult. It is not in the Internet Matters report it was credited to. See the first open item.
- Most children do not tell their parents. The large surveys say otherwise.
- That losing the phone is the main reason children stay silent. It is one reason children give. The one study that counted reasons found it a small one.
- Ten of sixteen, or any rate made from a small unpublished study.
- That a screen affects the body exactly as real life does, as fact.
- PTSD, traumatised, diagnosis or disorder about children who saw content. The word cybertrauma stays out of our headlines: it is one author's trademarked term and not a diagnosis.
- That it takes seven years to tell. That figure is about sexual abuse in sixty adults.
- Nine in ten do not tell an adult. American, self selected, cyberbullying only.
- Any promise that the phone will never be touched. Police may need it, and settings may need to change.
- Any instruction to question a child for details, or to look at, save, screenshot or forward an image.
- Any graphic description, named child, named challenge or method.
- Any clinician's name, script or view on Roblox or bans.

## Safeguarding lines every asset keeps

1. Listen. Never tell a parent to ask for details or a replay.
2. Never tell a parent to view, save or send a nude or sexual image of a child. It is illegal to copy, share or save one.
3. If someone is threatening a child over images: do not pay, stop contact, do not delete the messages, report to the police or CEOP.
4. No promise of secrecy. Say what will happen next.
5. Signs such as poor sleep or new fears have many causes. Never tell a parent their child has been harmed because they are sleeping badly.
6. Ongoing distress goes to the GP. We do not treat anything.
7. The reporting routes appear on the carousel, in the caption and under the Reel.

## 1. LinkedIn post

Your child comes to you and says something scary happened online.

The NSPCC's guidance for that moment starts with your face. Listen, and try not to show shock, because shock can make a child stop or take it back.

Here is what the UK data says about telling.

In Ofcom's 2025 research, 92 percent of 8 to 17 year olds said they would tell someone if they saw something worrying or nasty online. Most of those said a parent.

That is the good news, and it is what they say they would do.

Only 56 percent said they would always tell. It falls as they get older, from 65 percent of 8 and 9 year olds to about four in ten at 16 and 17.

Why not always? In Ofcom's workshops with 232 UK children, few wanted to tell a parent about violent content they had seen. One reason they gave was that the device would be taken away.

It is not the only reason, and one older American study found it was a small one. But it is the one a parent controls.

So here is what I would have ready.

"I'm really glad you came to me."

"You have done the right thing, and you are not in trouble."

"None of this is your fault."

"You don't have to tell me everything now."

"We'll work out what to do next together, and I'll tell you before I do anything."

Then look at the settings later, together. As safety, never as the price of telling.

If a child is in immediate danger, call 999. For grooming or online sexual abuse, report to the CEOP Safety Centre.

## 4. Parent resource: when your child tells you

Built from the NSPCC and CEOP guidance. Our own wording.

Say:

1. "I'm really glad you came to me."
2. "You have done the right thing, and you are not in trouble."
3. "None of this is your fault."
4. "You don't have to tell me everything now. I'm here, and I'm listening when you're ready."
5. "We'll work out what to do next together, and I'll tell you before I do anything."

Hold back from:

1. Showing shock, or asking why they were on it.
2. Asking for every detail, or asking them to show you again.
3. Taking the device away that evening as the response to being told.
4. Promising to keep it secret.
5. Looking at, saving or forwarding any sexual image of a child, including your own child's.

Afterwards: look at the settings together, as your job of keeping them safe
and never as a penalty for telling. If you already took the phone away, it is
not too late to go back and put it right.

Where to report:

- A child in immediate danger, a threat to life, or you fear they may harm themselves: 999.
- Grooming, or the way someone has been talking to your child online: the CEOP Safety Centre.
- A nude image of your child has been shared: Report Remove, through Childline. It covers faked images too. It cannot reach every copy.
- Someone is threatening them over images: do not pay, stop contact, keep the messages, report to the police on 101 or to CEOP.
- You are worried and want to talk it through: NSPCC helpline, 0808 800 5000.
- For your child: Childline, 0800 1111.

What this does not claim: that these words guarantee a child will tell you, or
that they treat anything. If your child stays upset, speak to your GP.
