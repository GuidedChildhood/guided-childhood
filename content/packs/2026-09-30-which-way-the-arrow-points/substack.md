## The issue

Almost everything you have read about children and screens rests on asking people how much they used their phone.

People are famously bad at that. The correlation between what someone says their screen time was and what their phone actually recorded is weak enough that a great many studies are, in effect, measuring how people feel about their phone use rather than their phone use.

A study published a few days ago did it differently, and I want to walk you through it, because the method is the story.

Researchers at Masaryk University followed 197 Czech teenagers, aged 13 to 17, for a year. Four two week bursts. An app on the phone passively recorded what they actually did, minute by minute, by category. Alongside that, four times a day, the teenagers were asked how they felt right then, on a scale from not at all to extremely.

Just over 17,500 moments.

Then they asked the question that almost never gets asked.

### Not whether. Which way

Everybody asks whether phone use and low mood go together. They do, a bit, and we have known that for years.

Almost nobody asks which comes first.

For entertainment apps, YouTube, Netflix, games, the answer ran backwards from what most of us assume. A teenager feeling better than usual was followed by less time on those apps. Time on those apps did not reliably change how they felt in either direction.

Read that again, because it is the opposite of the story we tell. The good mood came first, and the phone went down after.

Which makes a kind of obvious sense once you see it. A teenager who is having a decent day goes and does something else.

### The finding that does not help me

Social apps were different, and I am going to give you this before the part that supports what I usually argue.

When a teenager used social apps more than they usually do, they then reported slightly lower positive feeling and slightly higher negative feeling. Both effects were real and statistically credible. Both were small.

The researchers had predicted the opposite. Their starting theory was that teenagers use media to manage their mood and that it works. It did not work, and they reported that plainly.

So there is something there, in the social apps specifically, and it points the way the worried version says it does. I am not going to pretend otherwise.

Hold that, and now look at the next bit.

### Almost nobody is the average

Here is where it turned for me.

Rather than reporting one average effect for the whole group, they looked at each teenager individually and asked whether the pattern held for that person.

For whether a teenager's mood predicted reaching for social apps, **90.4 percent showed no detectable association at all**. For negative feeling specifically, it was 97.5 percent. For entertainment apps, 89.8 percent.

Nine in ten. Nothing measurable.

The average effect for the group is real. It is just that it is being carried by a small number of teenagers, and then spread thinly across everyone when it gets reported.

This is the thing I would most like every parent to understand, and it has nothing to do with phones. When you read that screens are associated with worse mood, that sentence is about a group. It is not about your child. It may be describing a handful of children very well and almost everybody else not at all.

### The number that puts it all in proportion

One more, and it is the one I keep thinking about.

Every measure in the study showed what statisticians call an autoregressive effect, which is a technical way of saying that how you were predicts how you are. Those effects were several times larger than any of the phone effects. Roughly six to ten times.

The best predictor of a teenager's mood was their mood an hour earlier.

Not their screen time. Not which app. Their day.

### What this study cannot tell you

I want to be careful here, because this result is convenient for me and that is exactly when to slow down.

It is 197 teenagers in one country. The sample was a convenience sample, partly recruited through an agency and partly by advert, and it was Android only. The researchers say themselves that teenagers who barely use their phones are probably underrepresented, and that generalising to all adolescents should be done with caution. I am passing that on rather than quietly dropping it.

It measured how somebody felt at that moment, in one hour windows. It says nothing about sleep. Nothing about depression. Nothing about what any of this does over years, which is the thing parents actually worry about.

And it could not see what they were watching or posting. Only how long, and what kind of app. The authors flag this as a limitation and it is a serious one, because the difference between a teenager watching something funny with friends and a teenager watching something that makes them feel small is the entire question, and this study could not see it.

What it does say, quite firmly, is that the number we have all agreed to stare at is not where the action is.

### So what do you actually do

Nothing that needs buying, and nothing dramatic.

**Ask about the day before you look at the number.** The study says a teenager's mood mostly comes from their day and arrives at the phone already formed. If you open with the screen time report you are starting the conversation at the wrong end, and they know it.

**Watch the direction, not the total.** The useful question is not how many hours. It is whether the phone goes up when something has gone wrong. That is a pattern you can notice without an app, and it is the thing the study suggests actually distinguishes one child from another.

**Assume your child is one of the nine, until you see otherwise.** Most teenagers in this study showed no link at all. Treating a child as if they are in the worrying minority, when they are not, does its own damage to the relationship you will need later.

**And if they are the one in ten, that is worth knowing properly.** Not through a weekly total. Through the thing you notice: the phone coming out after a bad day, every time.

## Deliberately not here

**Any claim that phones are harmless.** The study found a real negative effect
for social apps and it is in the issue, before the findings that help.

**Anything about sleep, depression or long term development.** This measured
momentary mood over one hour windows, and nothing else.

**Any suggestion this describes British children.** 197 Czech adolescents,
Android, convenience sample, and the authors' own caution is quoted.

**The word addiction.** It appears nowhere in the study and nowhere here.
