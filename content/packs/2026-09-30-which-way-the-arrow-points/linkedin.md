## Post one · Which way the arrow points

1,958 characters.

Researchers logged 197 teenagers' phones for a year and asked them how they felt four times a day. 17,580 moments, with the phone use recorded rather than remembered.

Then they asked the question almost nobody asks. Not whether phone use and mood go together. Which one comes first.

For entertainment apps, YouTube, Netflix, games, the answer ran backwards. A teenager feeling better than usual was followed by less time on them, not more. Time on them did not change how they felt in either direction.

The phone was reading the mood, not setting it.

Social apps were different, and I am going to give you the bit that goes against me before the bit that helps. More social app use than usual was followed by slightly lower positive feeling and slightly higher negative feeling. Small, but real, and measured properly. The researchers had predicted the opposite and said so.

So there is something there. Hold that.

Now the number I cannot get past. When they looked at each teenager individually rather than at the group average, about nine in ten showed no detectable link at all between how they felt and whether they reached for their phone. For negative feeling and social apps it was 97 percent.

The average effect is real. Almost nobody is the average.

And one more thing, which reframes the lot. How a teenager felt an hour ago predicted how they feel now roughly six to ten times more strongly than anything their phone did. The best predictor of a teenager's mood was their mood.

This is 197 Czech adolescents on Android, a convenience sample, measuring how someone felt at that moment. It says nothing about sleep, or depression, or next year. The authors say plainly that generalising to all teenagers needs caution, and I am not going to do it for them.

But it does say the thing we keep measuring, hours, is not where the action is.

If you have a teenager, what do you actually notice first, the phone going up or the mood going down?

### Prepared first comment, post one

Post this the second the post is live.

Source, and it is open access so nobody has to take my word for it. Šaradín Lebedíková and colleagues, "Does Smartphone Application Use Impact Adolescents' Affect? Or Vice Versa? An Experience Sampling Study", Media Psychology, published online 27 September 2026. Masaryk University. It carries the Center for Open Science Open Data badge, so the data are posted.

The numbers, since I rounded in the post. Entertainment apps: higher than usual positive affect was followed by lower use, b = −.034, credible interval −.066 to −.005. Social apps: use was followed by lower positive affect, b = −.024, and higher negative affect, b = .030. Individual level: 90.4 percent showed no credible association between positive affect and later social app use, 97.5 percent for negative affect, 89.8 percent for entertainment. Autoregressive effects ran .198 to .287.

The limits, which are real. 197 adolescents, Czech, aged 13 to 17, Android only, recruited partly by agency and partly by advert, and the authors note low use teenagers are probably underrepresented. One hour windows and momentary mood. No data on what was actually watched or posted, only how long and which category.

## Post two · The question underneath, only if post one lands

1,532 characters.

Yesterday I posted about a study that logged teenagers' phones and asked which way the arrow points. The thing that got the most replies was not the finding. It was one word: average.

So here is the bit worth its own post.

The same study found two things that sound contradictory and are not.

Teenagers who used social apps more than other teenagers did report feeling worse than other teenagers. That is a real finding.

But when they followed one teenager over a year, and asked whether that teenager felt worse on the days they used more than they usually do, it mostly was not there. About nine in ten showed no detectable link.

Those are two different questions and we treat them as one.

The first compares children to each other. The second asks what happens inside a child. A policy, a school rule, a bedtime, a ban, all of them act on the second. Almost all of the evidence we argue about is the first.

I do not think that makes the worry silly. I think it means a real effect concentrated in a small number of children gets averaged into a weak effect spread across all of them, and then we design for the average child, who does not exist.

That is the part that bothers me. Not whether phones are bad. Whether we would know which child needed something, if we only ever look at the mean.

This is one study, 197 teenagers, and it measured momentary mood over one hour windows. It cannot carry more than that.

If you work with children, how do you spot the one who is actually struggling, when the group looks fine?

### Prepared first comment, post two

Same study as yesterday, open access. Šaradín Lebedíková et al., Media Psychology, 27 September 2026.

The two levels, in their numbers. Between person: adolescents who generally used social apps more reported higher negative affect, b = .177, credible interval .021 to .323. Within person, positive affect predicting later social app use: 90.4 percent of participants showed no credible association, 4.1 percent negative, 5.6 percent positive.

The statistical name for treating the first as if it answered the second is the ecological fallacy, and it is most of the screen time conversation.

## Why these are shaped this way

**The concession is in the middle of post one, not the end.** The social app
finding genuinely cuts against our position. Putting it before the nine in ten
is what makes the nine in ten believable. It is the honest pivot from the
viral-post formula, used on the side it costs us.

**The hook is a method, not a claim.** "Logged, not remembered" is the thing
that makes this study different from the hundred a parent has skimmed. No
adjective to argue with.

**One physical comparison per post.** Post one: mood predicts mood six to ten
times more strongly than the phone does. Post two: the average child does not
exist.

**Neither post asks for anything.** No link, no CTA, no follow ask. The question
at the end is a real one that only a parent or a teacher can answer, which is
what earns the long comments that carry reach.

**Post two exists to be skipped.** If post one does not earn a thread, post two
is a worse post than whatever else is in the queue. Do not run it out of
completeness.
