# Which way the arrow points. A waterfall on one paper.

Built 30 September 2026 from a single study Justin sent through, read in full
from the PDF rather than from an abstract.

## The paper

Šaradín Lebedíková, M., Lacko, D., Tkaczyk, M., Blahošová, J., Elavsky, S., and
Šmahel, D. (2026). "Does Smartphone Application Use Impact Adolescents' Affect?
Or Vice Versa? An Experience Sampling Study." *Media Psychology*, published
online 27 September 2026. doi 10.1080/15213269.2026.2729979

Open access under CC BY, and it carries the Center for Open Science Open Data
badge. Both matter for us: Justin can link it without a paywall wall, and the
data are posted, which is the single strongest answer to "who funded this".

## Why this one is worth a whole waterfall

Nearly every screen time study asks how many hours, from memory, once. This one
logged what the phone actually did, asked 197 Czech adolescents how they felt
four times a day, and ran it for a year in four two week bursts. 17,580 moments.

Then it did the thing almost nobody does. It asked which way the arrow points,
and it separated two questions that are usually mashed together:

- **Between people.** Do teenagers who use more feel worse than other teenagers?
- **Within one person.** When this teenager uses more than they usually do, do
  they then feel worse than they usually do?

Those are different questions. The paper found the first and mostly did not find
the second, and that gap is the whole content spine.

## The three findings the pack rests on

1. **For entertainment apps the arrow runs the other way.** Higher than usual
   positive affect was followed by *less* entertainment app use, b = −.034, 95%
   CrI [−.066, −.005]. Entertainment use did not credibly change affect in
   either direction. Feeling good predicted the phone going down.

2. **For social apps there is a real effect, and it is tiny and unwelcome.**
   Higher than usual social app use was followed by slightly lower positive
   affect, b = −.024 [−.038, −.010], and slightly higher negative affect,
   b = .030 [.016, .044]. The researchers had predicted the opposite. We report
   this first and plainly, because it is the finding that cuts against us.

3. **About nine in ten adolescents showed nothing at all.** For whether positive
   affect predicted later social app use, 90.4 percent showed no credible
   association. For negative affect, 97.5 percent. For entertainment, 89.8
   percent. The average effect is carried by a small minority.

## The comparison that makes it physical

Every variable had a positive autoregressive effect, from b = .198 to b = .287.
How a teenager felt an hour ago predicted how they feel now roughly six to ten
times more strongly than anything their phone did.

The best predictor of a teenager's mood is their mood.

## What this pack must never say

- **That this proves phones are harmless.** It does not. It found a real, small,
  unwanted effect for social apps, and it measured only short term momentary
  mood over one hour windows.
- **That it says anything about depression, sleep, self harm or development.**
  It measured how someone felt at that moment. Nothing longer.
- **That it describes British children.** 197 Czech adolescents, aged 13 to 17,
  Android only, a convenience sample, recruited partly through an agency and
  partly by advert. The authors say plainly that generalising to all adolescents
  should be taken with caution and that low use teenagers are probably
  underrepresented. We say that too, in the body, not in a footnote.
- **That the researchers set out to defend phones.** They predicted phones would
  *improve* mood, per mood management theory, and did not find it.

## The one brick this carries

Per the 1 in 10 rule, this pack does not state the thesis. It lays one brick:
the average is not the child. Nine in ten showed nothing, so the effect lives in
a minority, and a policy built on the average will miss the children it is for.
Readers get to the rest themselves.
