## POST 1, The law just issued its first fine. A seven year old is hurt by what sits underneath it.
**News peg:** Ofcom's first Online Safety Act fine (4chan, 450,000 pounds, March 2026).
**Service:** the pathway starts at age 4, teaches before exposure.

**Post text:**

This year the Online Safety Act issued its first fine. 450,000 pounds.

Good. It should. That fine was for failing to keep children away from pornography, and the law is right to bite there.

But here is the finding that has been sitting on my desk all week, and it is about a different child.

A trauma psychotherapist called Catherine Knibbs studied children aged 7 to 10, letting them draw what they could not say. She reports that of sixteen children, ten showed stress responses that a clinician would recognise from the trauma manual. From Squid Game watched alone. From things inside Roblox. From content on the family telly that a parent put on.

Almost none of it is the kind of content the Act fines anyone for. It sits underneath the threshold.

Now the honest part, because I will not oversell it. This is a small study, sixteen children, and she says so plainly. It cannot tell you how common this is. It is not a headcount. It is a clinician showing you a mechanism the big surveys are too blunt to catch, in the exact age group they miss.

And that is the gap I built for. Everyone is fighting over the top of the pyramid, the illegal, the fineable, the worst. Meanwhile a seven year old is carrying a nervous system response to something no regulator will ever categorise, and carrying it silently.

Our pathway does not start at 13 when the apps arrive. It starts at four, and it teaches a child what they are going to meet before they meet it, because the law was only ever going to reach the worst of it.

The fine is the floor. It is not the ceiling of harm.

If the law only fines the worst content, who is preparing your child for the rest of it?

**First comment:** The study is Catherine Knibbs's doctoral research, and she is careful, it is qualitative, sixteen children, mechanism not prevalence. That is exactly why it is useful, it reaches the seven year olds the surveys cannot safely ask. The Ofcom fine figure is the 450,000 pound child protection penalty, March 2026.

## POST 2, You cannot build a report button for a child who will not tell you.
**News peg:** Internet Matters, 7 in 10 children harmed online, fewer than 1 in 5 tell a trusted adult.
**Service:** DiGi as the askable guide, the family agreement, and Knibbs already inside it.

**Post text:**

Seven in ten children have met something harmful online. Fewer than one in five told a trusted adult.

That is Internet Matters, a survey of a thousand UK children. Sit with the second number. The harm is happening, and the child is not telling you.

Catherine Knibbs found the same thing from the other side, in the therapy room. The 7 to 10 year olds in her study had worked out that parents "don't get it, they're not kids." And one group of Year 5s had quietly ruled out teachers too, or feared that telling a teacher just meant it went straight to mum and dad.

So here is the uncomfortable thing for anyone building in this space, me included. The entire online safety industry is built on the child reporting. The button. The flag. The worry box. And the children have already decided not to use it.

An absence of complaints is not an absence of harm. It is a measurement failure.

I could not build my way around that with a better button. So I built the opposite. DiGi, the guide we made, is for the parent, not the child, because the child is not going to type it into anything. It hands a parent the calm words for the moment a child does start to talk, and it is built on one rule I took straight from Knibbs's own work, and wrote into the product months ago. Speak to the state under the behaviour. Keep yourself the safe person to tell. Never make the child the problem.

You do not fix silence with a form. You fix it with one adult a child decides is safe.

When your child finally tells you something hard, what do they need you to do in the first ten seconds?

**First comment:** The stat is Internet Matters, 2025, a thousand UK children, fewer than one in five who were harmed told a trusted adult. The line I built into DiGi is Catherine Knibbs's, she has been in our research bank since before these reports came out, which is either good judgement or a lucky guess, I will take either.

## POST 3, There is a kind of childhood trauma that is on nobody's checklist.
**News peg:** Children's Commissioner, over a million children referred to mental health services (Jun 2026).
**Service:** the whole child, trauma informed pathway and the hidden thread. The thesis post.

**Post text:**

Over a million children were referred to mental health services in England last year. The highest number ever recorded. More than sixty thousand have been waiting over two years.

We keep asking what is driving that number. Here is one piece almost nobody is counting.

Catherine Knibbs, a trauma psychotherapist of twenty years, argues that some children are carrying a trauma response to what they have witnessed on a screen, and that it belongs alongside the adverse childhood experiences we already screen for. Not a screen time worry. Adversity. She reports children aged seven whose drawings mapped the body's stress cascade with the precision of an adult in a therapy room.

And it is on nobody's checklist. Not the safeguarding frameworks, not the ACEs lists, not the teacher training. So it shows up as a behaviour problem, a sleep problem, a child who suddenly will not go upstairs alone, and it gets a behaviour chart aimed at the wrong target.

Here is the belief underneath everything I build, said plainly, once. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are poverty, the hard things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with too little help. The screen is rarely the whole story. But when a child is harmed, it is almost never the hours. It is what they met, whether anyone saw it, and whether they had a safe adult when they did.

That is why I did not build a screen time counter. I built a pathway that treats the whole child, because a million referrals is a system meeting this too late, and the honest work is upstream.

If a child's distress is being read as bad behaviour, what are we missing in the millionth referral?

**First comment:** The referral figure is the Children's Commissioner for England, 2024 to 25 data, over a million children, published this summer. Knibbs frames screen based trauma as a form of childhood adversity, and I think she is right that it is missing from the checklists. It is qualitative work, mechanism not prevalence, held honestly. The point stands either way, the driver is adversity, not hours.

## POST 4, A face scan that a teenager beats in an afternoon is not a safety plan.
**News peg:** Roblox mandatory facial age scans, Jan 2026, and the BBC test that showed it leaking.
**Service:** teaching kids to see the machine (modules), DiGi never rubber stamps a rating, the balance report reads the pattern.

**Post text:**

From January, Roblox made every user pass a facial age scan to chat. Then the BBC ran its own test, and a 27 year old and a 15 year old could still message each other.

I am not knocking the effort. I am pointing at the pattern.

Catherine Knibbs found two things in her research that a face scan does not touch. First, the children were playing games they cannot legally buy in a shop, because ratings are misapplied, misunderstood, or ignored at home, and platform labels like Roblox's make it murkier, not clearer. Second, and this is the one that should stop you, after a child met frightening content, they went back to seek more of it. The technology shapes the child, and the child shapes what the technology serves next.

A gate does nothing about either. It does not fix a rating a parent cannot read, and it certainly does not reach a child whose own nervous system is pulling them back toward the thing that scared them. Through an adult lens that looks like naughtiness. Through a trauma lens it is a brain trying to make sense of something.

So I built the thing the gate forgets. We teach children how the machine actually works, why it serves them the next thing, and how to feel the pull and step back from it. Our guide never rubber stamps an age rating as a yes. And our balance report does not count hours, it reads the pattern, because the seeking loop is a pattern, and a clock will never see it.

You cannot age gate your way to a child who understands the machine. You have to teach them.

If a child goes back to the thing that frightened them, is that a discipline problem, or a signal?

**First comment:** The Roblox age scan rolled out worldwide in January 2026, and the BBC's own test of the age banding is worth reading before anyone calls the gate solved. The seeking loop is from Catherine Knibbs's research, qualitative and clinical, and it is the single most useful thing in it for a parent, because it explains why taking the tablet away does not resolve what is already inside.

## POST 5, The most hopeful line in the research was about one adult. [carries the CTA]
**News peg:** Australia's under 16 ban evaded, 8 in 10 still online (Aug 2026).
**Service:** the whole platform, preparation plus trust, connection is the protection.

**Post text:**

Australia banned social media for under 16s. Three months later, eight in ten of those teenagers were still on it.

Gates leak. Bans get walked around. You know my view on this by now.

So let me end this series on the most hopeful thing I have read all year, and it is not a policy. It is one line from Catherine Knibbs's work.

The children in her study with an engaged, present adult drew happier inner worlds. More awareness, more limits, more joy. The adult engagement was visible inside the child. And her conclusion, after twenty years in the therapy room, is almost too simple to trust. One askable adult changes the arithmetic for a child. It does not have to be a perfect parent. It has to be one calm person who does not flinch when the hard thing is finally said.

That is the whole of what I have spent this year building. Not a wall, and not a better lock. A pathway that prepares a child before they meet the world online, a guide that helps a parent be that calm askable adult, and an agreement you write together so the trust is there before it is needed.

Because the evidence, the careful kind, keeps landing in the same place. A ban delays the apps and prepares no one. A gate leaks. A face scan is beaten in an afternoon. But a child who has one adult they trust is safer than a child with every parental control ever made. Connection is the protection.

That is not soft. On this evidence, it is the strongest thing we have.

If you want the calm version of all of this, we made a free place to start, at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack.

Who was the one askable adult in your childhood, and what did they do that made them safe to tell?
