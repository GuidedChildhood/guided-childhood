## POST 4 (run order), The law fined the worst content this year. I want to talk about the rest.
**News peg:** Ofcom's first Online Safety Act fine (4chan, 450,000 pounds, March 2026).
**Service:** the pathway starts at age 4, teaches before exposure. Follows trio post 2's rule.

**Post text:**

This week I said you cannot build a ban on sixteen children, but you can build a lesson. Here is the sixteen children I meant, and the lesson.

A trauma psychotherapist, Catherine Knibbs, studied sixteen children aged 7 to 10, letting them draw what they could not say. She reports that ten of them showed stress responses a clinician would recognise from the trauma manual, from content that sits below the line the law cares about.

Let me be precise, the way I promised to be. Sixteen children is a small, careful study. It shows a mechanism, not a prevalence. It cannot tell you how common this is, and it is nowhere near a reason to ban anything. I am not going to pretend otherwise to make you click.

But it is a perfectly good reason to prepare a child. And that is the whole point.

This year the Online Safety Act issued its first fine, 450,000 pounds, for failing to keep children from pornography. The law is right to bite there. But the content in Knibbs's study sits underneath that line, and some of it, a film a parent put on the family telly, sits outside any platform duty at all. The law was only ever going to reach the worst of it.

So I did not build an alarm. I built a pathway that starts at four and teaches a child what they might meet before they meet it, because the gap the law leaves is not closed by fear, it is closed by preparation.

The fine is the floor. Preparing a child is the part no regulator will do for you.

If the law only reaches the worst content, what prepares a child for the rest of it?

**First comment:** The study is Catherine Knibbs's doctoral research, sixteen children, qualitative, mechanism not prevalence, and I would rather say that plainly than dress it up. The fine figure is the 450,000 pound child protection penalty, Ofcom, March 2026.

## POST 5 (run order), You cannot build a report button for a child who will not tell you.
**News peg:** Internet Matters, 7 in 10 children harmed online, fewer than 1 in 5 tell a trusted adult.
**Service:** DiGi as the askable guide, the family agreement, Knibbs already inside it.

**Post text:**

Seven in ten children have met something harmful online. Fewer than one in five told a trusted adult.

That is Internet Matters, a survey of a thousand UK children. Sit with the second number. The harm is happening, and the child is not telling you.

Catherine Knibbs found the same thing from inside the therapy room. The children in her study had worked out that parents "don't get it, they're not kids." One group of Year 5s had quietly ruled out teachers too, or feared that telling one just meant it went straight home.

This is the uncomfortable part for everyone building in this space, me included. The whole online safety industry is built on the child reporting. The button. The flag. The worry box. And the children have already decided not to use it. An absence of complaints is not an absence of harm. It is a measurement failure.

You do not fix silence with a better button. So I built the opposite. DiGi, the guide we made, is for the parent, not the child, because the child is not going to type it into anything. It hands a parent the calm words for the moment a child does start to talk, and it runs on one rule I took straight from Knibbs's own work and wrote into the product months ago. Speak to the state under the behaviour. Keep yourself the safe person to tell. Never make the child the problem.

Silence is not solved by a form. It is solved by one adult a child decides is safe.

When your child finally tells you something hard, what do they need you to do in the first ten seconds?

**First comment:** The stat is Internet Matters, 2025, a thousand UK children, fewer than one in five who were harmed told a trusted adult. The rule I built into DiGi is Catherine Knibbs's, and she has been in our research bank since before these reports came out.

## POST 6 (run order), There is a kind of childhood adversity that is on nobody's checklist.
**News peg:** Children's Commissioner, over a million children referred to mental health services (Jun 2026).
**Service:** the whole child, trauma informed pathway and the hidden thread. The thesis post.

**Post text:**

Over a million children were referred to mental health services in England last year. The highest ever recorded. More than sixty thousand have been waiting over two years.

We keep asking what is driving that number. Here is one piece almost nobody is counting, held honestly.

Catherine Knibbs argues that some children carry a trauma response to what they have witnessed on a screen, and that it belongs alongside the adverse childhood experiences we already screen for. Not a screen time worry. Adversity. It is qualitative, clinical work, so treat it as a mechanism, not a headcount. But the mechanism is enough to notice, and right now nobody is. Not the safeguarding frameworks, not the ACEs lists, not the teacher training. So it shows up as a behaviour problem, a sleep problem, a child who will not go upstairs alone, and gets a chart aimed at the wrong target.

Here is the belief underneath everything I build, said plainly, once. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are poverty, the hard things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with too little help. The screen is rarely the whole story. And when a child is harmed, it is almost never the hours. It is what they met, whether anyone saw it, and whether they had a safe adult when they did.

That is why I did not build a screen time counter. I built a pathway that treats the whole child, because a million referrals is a system meeting this too late, and the honest work is upstream.

If a child's distress is being read as bad behaviour, what are we missing in the millionth referral?

**First comment:** The referral figure is the Children's Commissioner for England, 2024 to 25 data, published this summer. Knibbs frames screen based trauma as a form of childhood adversity, and I think she is right that it is missing from the checklists. Qualitative work, mechanism not prevalence, and the point stands either way, the driver is adversity, not hours.

## POST 7 (run order), Why "just take the tablet away" does not fix what is already inside.
**News peg:** age ratings and platform labels (Roblox), refocused on the seeking loop, not the gate.
**Service:** teaching kids to see the machine, DiGi never rubber stamps a rating, the balance report reads the pattern.

**Post text:**

Here is the single most useful thing I have read for a parent all year, and it is not scary, it is clarifying.

Catherine Knibbs found that after a child met frightening content, some of them went back to seek more of it. Through an adult lens that looks like naughtiness, or an obsession you should punish. Through a trauma lens it is a brain trying to make sense of something it could not process the first time.

That one reframe changes what you do. Because if it is naughtiness, you take the tablet away and feel you have solved it. If it is a nervous system looping on something unresolved, taking the tablet away does nothing about what is already inside, and the child learns only that you are not the person to tell.

This is also why the age rating on the box is not the safety plan people think it is. Children play games they cannot legally buy, because ratings are misread at home and platform labels confuse more than they clarify. A rating tells you a category. It tells you nothing about what this particular child does with it.

So I did not build a stricter gate. I built the thing the gate cannot do. We teach children how the machine actually works and why it serves them the next thing, so they can feel the pull and step back from it. Our guide never rubber stamps an age rating as a simple yes. And our balance report does not count hours, it reads the pattern, because the seeking loop is a pattern, and a clock will never see it.

A child who understands the loop can step out of it. A child who is only punished for it learns to hide it.

If a child goes back to the thing that frightened them, is that a discipline problem, or a signal to read?

**First comment:** The seeking loop is from Catherine Knibbs's research, qualitative and clinical. It is the finding I would hand every parent, because it explains why removing the device does not resolve what is already in the child, and points at the thing that does, which is understanding, not punishment.

## POST 8 (run order), The most hopeful line in the research was about one adult. [carries the CTA]
**News peg:** Australia's under 16 ban evaded, 8 in 10 still online (Aug 2026).
**Service:** the whole platform, preparation plus trust, connection is the protection.

**Post text:**

Australia banned social media for under 16s. Three months later, more than eight in ten of those teenagers were still using one. Not mostly by clever evasion, the government's own report says the gate was largely never switched on, half the children were simply never asked to prove their age. Either way, the wall did not do the job.

You know my view on this by now, and this whole week has been me arguing that the answer was never a wall.

So let me end on the most hopeful thing I have read all year, and it is not a policy. It is one line from Catherine Knibbs's work.

The children in her study who had an engaged, present adult drew happier inner worlds. More awareness, more limits, more joy. The adult engagement was visible inside the child. And her conclusion, after twenty years in the therapy room, is almost too simple to trust. One askable adult changes the arithmetic. It does not have to be a perfect parent. It has to be one calm person who does not flinch when the hard thing is finally said.

That is the whole of what I have spent this year building. Not a wall, and not a fright. A pathway that prepares a child before they meet the world online, a guide that helps a parent be that calm askable adult, and an agreement you write together so the trust is there before it is needed.

Because the careful evidence keeps landing in the same place. A ban delays the apps and prepares no one. A gate leaks. But a child who has one adult they trust is safer than a child with every parental control ever made. Connection is the protection. That is not soft. On this evidence, it is the strongest thing we have.

If you want the calm version of all of this, we made a free place to start, at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack.

Who was the one askable adult in your childhood, and what did they do that made them safe to tell?

**First comment:** The Australia figures are the eSafety Commissioner's own three month evaluation, July 2026, self report, and it says plainly it is too early to judge outcomes. The main reason children kept accounts was platforms not checking at all, 50.2% never asked to prove their age, not the VPNs and fake ages the headlines assume. The one askable adult finding is Catherine Knibbs's, and it is the thread through this whole run, the fixes that work are not gates, they are relationships. Thank you for reading it.
