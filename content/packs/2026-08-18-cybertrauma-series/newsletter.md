## The trauma nobody was trained for

This year the Online Safety Act issued its first fine, 450,000 pounds, for failing to keep children away from pornography. The law is right to bite there. But I have spent this week with a set of reports about a different child, and a different kind of harm, and I want to walk you through what they say and what I have built for it, because the two have turned out to be the same story.

The reports are by Catherine Knibbs, a child trauma psychotherapist of twenty years. She gave children aged 7 to 10 a way to show, through drawing, what they could not say out loud. And she reports that of sixteen children, ten showed stress responses a clinician would recognise from the trauma manual, from Squid Game watched alone, from things inside Roblox, from content a parent had put on the family telly.

Let me be honest about the evidence before I lean on it, because that is the whole of my brand. This is a small, qualitative study, sixteen children, and she says so plainly. It cannot tell you how common this is. It is not a headcount. What it is, is a clinician showing you a mechanism the big population surveys are too blunt to catch, in the exact age group they cannot safely ask. Held that way, it is not panic. It is precision. Her own words are that precision is the opposite of panic, and that her work is not anti technology. That is my register too.

Here is what her work, read alongside the news of the last few months, actually says.

### The harm sits beneath the threshold

Almost none of the content that traumatised these seven year olds is the kind the Act fines anyone for. It sits underneath the priority threshold, and some of it, the family television, sits outside any platform duty at all. So the entire regulatory fight is happening at the top of the pyramid, the illegal and the fineable, while a young child carries a nervous system response to something no regulator will ever categorise.

That is the gap I built for. Our pathway does not start at 13 when the apps arrive. It starts at four, and it teaches a child what they are going to meet before they meet it, because the law was only ever going to reach the worst of it.

### The child will not tell you

Internet Matters surveyed a thousand UK children and found seven in ten had met something harmful online, and fewer than one in five told a trusted adult. Knibbs found the same from inside the therapy room. Her 7 to 10 year olds had decided parents "don't get it," and one group of Year 5s had quietly ruled out teachers too, or feared that telling one just meant it went straight home.

This should stop everyone building in this space, me included, because the whole industry is built on the child reporting. The button, the flag, the worry box. And the children have already decided not to use them. An absence of complaints is not an absence of harm. It is a measurement failure.

You cannot fix silence with a better form. So I built the opposite, a guide for the parent, not the child, because the child is not going to type it into anything. And I built it on one rule I took straight from Knibbs's own work and wrote into the product months before these reports landed. Speak to the state under the behaviour. Keep yourself the safe person to tell. Never make the child the problem.

### It is adversity, and it is on nobody's checklist

Over a million children were referred to mental health services in England last year, the highest ever recorded, more than sixty thousand of them waiting over two years. We keep asking what drives that number. Knibbs argues that some children are carrying a trauma response to what they witnessed on a screen, and that it belongs alongside the adverse childhood experiences we already screen for. Not a screen time worry. Adversity. And it is on nobody's checklist, so it presents as a behaviour problem, a sleep problem, a child who will not go upstairs alone, and gets a chart aimed at the wrong target.

Here is the belief underneath everything I build, said once. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are poverty, the hard things that happen at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with too little help. The screen is rarely the whole story. But when a child is harmed, it is almost never the hours. It is what they met, whether anyone saw it, and whether they had a safe adult when they did. That is why I did not build a screen time counter. I built a pathway that treats the whole child, because a million referrals is a system meeting this too late, and the honest work is upstream.

### A gate is not a plan

From January, Roblox made every user pass a facial age scan to chat. Then the BBC ran its own test, and a 27 year old and a 15 year old could still message each other. Knibbs found two things a face scan does not touch. Children were playing games they cannot legally buy, because ratings are misapplied and platform labels confuse rather than clarify. And after a child met frightening content, they went back to seek more of it. Through an adult lens that looks like naughtiness. Through a trauma lens it is a brain trying to make sense of something.

A gate does nothing about either. So I built the thing the gate forgets. We teach children how the machine works and why it serves them the next thing, our guide never rubber stamps an age rating as a yes, and our balance report does not count hours, it reads the pattern, because the seeking loop is a pattern and a clock will never see it.

### The most hopeful line was about one adult

Australia banned social media for under 16s. Three months later, more than eight in ten of those teenagers were still using one, and the government's own report says the gate was largely never switched on rather than cleverly beaten, half the children were simply never asked to prove their age. Either way, the wall did not do the job.

So I will end on the most hopeful thing I read all year, and it is not a policy. The children in Knibbs's study who had an engaged, present adult drew happier inner worlds, more awareness, more limits, more joy. The adult engagement was visible inside the child. Her conclusion, after twenty years, is almost too simple to trust. One askable adult changes the arithmetic. It does not have to be a perfect parent. It has to be one calm person who does not flinch when the hard thing is finally said.

That is the whole of what I have spent this year building. A pathway that prepares a child before they meet the world online, a guide that helps a parent be that calm askable adult, and an agreement you write together so the trust is there before it is needed. Because the careful evidence keeps landing in the same place. A ban delays the apps and prepares no one. A gate leaks. But a child with one adult they trust is safer than a child with every parental control ever made. Connection is the protection. That is not soft. On this evidence, it is the strongest thing we have.
