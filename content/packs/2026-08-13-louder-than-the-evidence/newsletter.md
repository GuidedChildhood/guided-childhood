## The most important parenting book of the decade is louder than its evidence

I want to do something unfashionable and take Jonathan Haidt seriously, which means neither cheering him nor dismissing him. Most of my feed does one or the other. Both are lazy, and our children are the ones who lose when we get this wrong.

So let me start with the credit, because it is real and it is large.

Haidt named a worry that millions of parents were already feeling and could not act on, and he gave them permission. He made delaying the smartphone a normal, sensible, low cost thing to do, which it is. He is right that phones in school are a distraction, and phone free classrooms are a reasonable idea. He is right that we have flipped childhood upside down, over protecting our children in the physical world while leaving them wide open online. And he argues in the open, posting his data and naming the people who disagree with him. He has moved the whole conversation. Whatever comes next, he deserves that.

Here is the turn, and it is the reason for this letter.

The story Haidt tells is louder than the evidence he has to tell it with. Not wrong at the edges. Louder in the middle. A small, mostly correlational signal gets narrated as a proven, brain rewiring epidemic sweeping a generation. And when the volume runs ahead of the proof, something quietly expensive happens. Frightened parents aim at the wrong target, and the thing actually hurting their children carries on in the dark, unnamed and unfunded.

Let me show you the gap, because it is not my opinion, it is sitting in the journals.

### The number under the panic

The headline number in this whole field, the one everything rests on, is smaller than most people would ever guess. Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski at Oxford, working with data on about 355,000 adolescents, found that digital technology use explains at most 0.4 percent of the variance in a young person's wellbeing. To make the size vivid, they compared it in the paper itself to wearing glasses, and noted it is smaller than the effect of being bullied. They even put eating potatoes in the comparison, on purpose, to stop anyone pretending the effect was large.

That is the number underneath a decade of headlines about phones rewiring your child's brain.

Now, I will not do to you what the panic does, so here is the honest other side. An average hides things. Orben's own later work found windows where the effect is bigger, roughly ages 11 to 13 for girls and 14 to 15 for boys. And a small average absolutely does not mean no child is harmed. Some are, seriously. Wrong villain does not mean no villain.

But hold two children in your head. Same three hours a day. One is a settled 12 year old making daft videos with her friends. The other is an anxious 12 year old awake alone at one in the morning. The average says the phone barely touched either of them, and the average is not lying, it is telling you the phone is doing almost nothing to the first child, and sitting on top of something much heavier in the second. The number is small because for most children the phone is not the story. For a few, it is one chapter of a story that was already being written.

### The villain most of us have got wrong

The person who has looked hardest at this is not a tech optimist. Candice Odgers has followed thousands of children aged 10 to 14 since 2008. Reviewing Haidt's book in Nature, she wrote that the claim that digital technology is rewiring children's brains and driving an epidemic "is not supported by science," and then she wrote the sentence almost nobody quoted. Blaming social media, she warned, "might distract us from effectively responding to the real causes of the current mental health crisis in young people."

Two things sit inside that.

First, the signal often runs backwards. A child who is already struggling reaches for the phone more, the way a struggling adult does. So a lot of what looks like screens causing distress is distress driving the scrolling. Last year a UK study with proper clinical assessment of 3,340 teenagers found exactly that, and its authors were careful, it shows no proof of cause, and the relationship probably runs both ways.

Second, and this is the part that keeps me up, the things genuinely driving the crisis are the ones that never trend. Poverty. What happens to a child inside their own home. The 2008 crash landing hardest and longest on the poorest families. A parent's own mental health, carried with too little support, and Odgers points out that parental overdose deaths more than doubled in a decade. None of that fits on a poster. A phone does. A phone is visible and it is rich, which makes it a deeply satisfying villain. The real drivers are old, expensive and awkward to film, so they sit in the dark while we shout at a screen.

I am not letting the platforms off. Their design deserves hard scrutiny and real regulation. But if we spend ten years fighting the phone and the empty CAMHS waiting rooms and the hungry homes are still exactly where they were, we will have lost a generation while facing the wrong way.

### The ban that just failed its first real test

Australia did the thing everyone is asking for and banned social media for under 16s. Three months in, more than eight in ten of those teenagers were still using one. But the government's own evaluation tells a quieter story than the headline. Most did not beat a clever gate. The gate was mostly never switched on. Half of the children who still had an account said the platform simply never asked them to prove their age, and using a VPN, the trick everyone pictures, was under one in ten. That is the regulator's own data, not a critic's.

I will not reopen the ban argument. It is settled, and it is coming here. I care about the bit that comes after, because nobody prepares you for it. Account ownership did fall, from 52% to 42%, and one in three children who had an account had it shut down, so the ban is not doing nothing. But look at what moved most. The share of parents who did not know their child was on social media rose, from 23% to 33%, worst of all among girls and the youngest. The clearest early effect was not less use. It was less visibility. You have not removed the risk. You have removed your view of it.

Odgers says it harder than I would dare. We have already pushed teenagers out of physical public spaces, she argues, and now we are taking the online ones too, because adults broke them, and it is the children we punish for it. For an isolated child, a disabled child, a queer kid in a small town who found their only community online, a ban does not confiscate a toy. It cuts a lifeline. And the single thing that most protects a child, an open conversation with a parent, is the first casualty, because the day it goes underground is the day they stop telling you.

### The thing that actually works

There is a new study everyone in my world is about to misuse. An Italian team followed 5,227 children through their real national test scores and found that opening a social media account at 11 rather than 14 tracked with lower maths and reading years later, published in Nature Human Behaviour this month. The restriction camp is already holding it up as proof.

Read it properly and it says something better than a wall. The authors do not conclude lock it away. They conclude three things together, delay, better platform design, and digital education, and they are explicit that delay on its own is not enough. And the strongest thing they measured was not screen hours at all. It was the phone bleeding into everything, study, meals, sleep. The pattern, not the clock.

That is the whole of what I have spent this year building. Not a wall, a pathway. You cannot ban your way to a capable 16 year old, you grow one, slowly, from about the age of four, one stage at a time, so that when the apps arrive, and they will, they meet a child who has already learned to see the machinery and steer it.

And underneath all of it is one belief I will say straight, just once. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are poverty, the hard things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with too little help. Social media takes the blame because it is visible and rich. The real drivers sit in the dark. I cannot fix them from an app. But I can refuse to build one more thing that points at a frightened parent and calls it a solution. What I can build is preparation, and the trust that comes from doing this alongside your child instead of to them. A child who trusts you is safer than a child with every parental control ever made. Connection is the protection.

Haidt got us to care. That was the hard part, and he did it. The next part is getting the proportion right, so we spend our fear where it will actually help. Not panic, and not a wall. Preparation, and trust, built early, at the age your child is now.
