# Ban outcomes: the evidence pack

Recent, sourced data on social media and phone bans, for the Louder Than The Evidence series and the
comment replies. Companion to 00-plan.md and philosophy-education-is-key.md. Every claim carries a
source and is calibrated to the Odgers test, defensible to a hostile expert. Represent the bans
evidence whole, including what cuts against us, or a critic lands the cherry-picking hit. No dashes.
Researched August 2026.

## 1. Australia's under 16 ban, the early outcomes

The strongest single fact set we have, because it is the government's own regulator and a leading child
safety charity, not tech critics.

- **eSafety (the Australian regulator).** Three months after the ban came into force on 10 December
  2025, more than eight in ten under 16s were still using social media, most as often as before. In
  most cases they did not even need a workaround, because platforms failed to detect or remove their
  accounts. About half said a platform had not yet asked them to verify their age. 37 percent had an
  account age listed over 16, 18.2 percent said the platform got their age wrong, about 11 percent
  said a parent helped them get around it, and 7.4 percent used a VPN. Use of messaging services rose
  from 40.5 to 52.3 percent and gaming from 26.4 to 29 percent in the first three months, with some
  migration to less regulated apps like Lemon8. (Al Jazeera and Engadget reporting eSafety data,
  August 2026.)
- **Molly Rose Foundation (April 2026).** The charity founded after Molly Russell's death, so the
  opposite of a tech apologist, ran the first large scale poll of young Australians, 1,050 aged 12 to
  15 via YouthInsight. 61 percent who previously held accounts on restricted platforms still had
  access to at least one. More than half still used TikTok, YouTube or Instagram. 70 percent of those
  still on restricted sites said circumventing the ban was easy. 51 percent said the ban had made no
  difference to their online safety. The Foundation warned that for the UK to follow would be a "high
  stakes gamble." (Molly Rose Foundation briefing, April 2026; ITV News.)

The honest line: the ban has so far mostly not removed access, it has removed the platforms' and
parents' view of it, and it moved use toward messaging and gaming.

## 2. The experiment problem, no hard data on the target group

The single strongest backing for "an experiment run without the data."

- **Scientists' consensus, Frontiers in Developmental Psychology (May 2026), "We cannot ban our way
  out of a youth mental health crisis."** Not a single social media restriction experiment has
  included people under 16, so the effect on the exact group being targeted has never been tested. The
  adult experiments that do exist show weak, null and mixed effects, with about 40 percent showing
  harmful effects such as decreased life satisfaction and increased loneliness, or no effect at all.
- **Electronic Frontier Foundation (May 2026), "The Science is Not Settled."** Documents how weak
  evidence is driving the national push to ban, a useful survey of the evidence gap.

The honest line: a nation is running a real time experiment on its children, and the one group it is
aimed at is the group never studied.

## 3. School phone bans, mixed and mostly weak

Represent this whole, because it is where we can get caught cherry picking.

- **SMART Schools, Goodyear et al., Lancet Regional Health Europe (2025).** 1,227 adolescents aged 12
  to 15 across 30 UK schools, restrictive versus permissive phone policies. No association between the
  school's phone policy and mental wellbeing, sleep, or educational outcomes. What tracked with
  outcomes was total daily phone and social media use across the whole day, not the school rule.
- **Ferguson (2025), "Smartphone bans in schools remain unproven," World Journal of Pediatrics.**
  Concludes the case for school bans is not established.
- **The counterweight, state it out loud.** Abrahamsson's Norway study did find benefits from school
  phone bans, cut bullying for both sexes, and better mental health and grades for girls, largest for
  girls from low income homes. So the picture is mixed, with the benefits, where found, concentrated
  in already vulnerable groups rather than spread across the board. That is uneven effects, not a case
  for blanket policy, and it is the honest way to hold Norway and SMART Schools together.

## 4. Circumvention and displacement

- Circumvention is trivial, and the Australia data shows most children did not even need a trick,
  enforcement simply failed (section 1).
- On VPNs, hold it honestly. After the UK's July 2025 age checks, VPN downloads and bypass discussion
  surged (UK VPN downloads around 2 million, Proton sign ups up roughly 1,400 percent). But Internet
  Matters found no rise in children's first time VPN use after the checks. So say circumvention is
  easy and real, do not claim age checks are single handedly driving a child VPN epidemic.
- Displacement is real toward messaging, gaming and less regulated apps (Australia data). Keep the
  framing to "unseen, less protected spaces," not the literal dark web, which we cannot evidence at
  that strength.

## 5. Who it hits hardest, the vulnerable

- **Marginalised and isolated youth.** Advocates and researchers (Monash Lens and the Castan Centre
  for Human Rights Law, December 2025) warn the under 16 ban severs online lifelines for already
  marginalised young people, deepening isolation for those for whom an online community was the only
  safe one.
- **The polling.** An Australian study of queer youth found 82 percent worried the ban would leave
  them disconnected and 91 percent said social media had helped them find friends. (Monash, MJA
  InsightPlus, 2025.)
- The pattern to name: a blunt, universal ban lands hardest on the isolated, disabled and marginalised
  child for whom the platform was support, not a toy, which is the exact child the policy claims to
  protect.

## 6. What actually protects, guidance over prohibition

The positive evidence for our answer, education and the open conversation.

- **JMIR (2024), England, 16,655 adolescents aged 11 to 18.** Protection from anxiety and depression
  was found among adolescents with active parental guidance of their online life, and this was more
  protective than stricter regulatory or restrictive approaches, or no guidance at all. Active guidance
  beat restriction. (Note, this is a larger and stronger citation than the roughly 4,000 teen figure
  used in the current post, worth updating the post to this.)
- **Professional guidance.** The American Psychiatric Association and NIMH both advise ongoing adult
  discussion and coaching around social media content for young adolescents, not silence and not a
  wall.

The honest line: the thing that protects a child, an adult in the room and an open conversation, is
exactly what a ban that drives use underground shuts down.

## 7. The caveats, so we stay unkillable

- The Australia data is early, three to four months, and cross sectional. Say early, not final.
- School ban evidence is genuinely mixed, Norway found benefits, so never say "bans never work," say
  the population case is weak and unproven and the benefits where found are concentrated.
- Do not claim social media is harmless, and never minimise the worst harms. Hold both, the average is
  small and uneven, and the design can make a vulnerable child far worse, which is Molly Russell and is
  why we go after the design.
- Do not overclaim VPN or dark web migration. Use the government's own "still using, mostly undetected"
  framing, which is stronger and safer.
- Attribute cleanly, the 0.4 number is Oxford's, this ban data is eSafety and the Molly Rose
  Foundation, the experiment gap is the Frontiers 2026 piece.
