## The issue

The Wall Street Journal ran a piece this month headlined "Yes, Children Really
Are Getting Dumber."

If you are a British parent, I want to tell you something that did not make the
coverage. Whatever happened, it did not happen to your child.

The OECD published PISA 2025 a few weeks ago. 760,000 fifteen year olds across 91
countries, the largest thing of its kind we have. Across the OECD the results are
genuinely bad and there is no softening them. Between 2022 and 2025 the average
fell in all three subjects. Maths 472 to 463. Reading 476 to 461. Science 485 to
482. Reading is now the lowest PISA has ever recorded.

England did not do that.

Reading went from 496 to 497. Maths held at 492. Science went from 503 to 516, a
rise of 13 points in the same three years the OECD lost 3. England now sits
around eighth in the world for science, ninth for reading and tenth for maths.

Before that goes on a poster, two things.

England's reading is still below where it was in 2018, when it was 505. Holding
steady after a fall is not the same as being fine, and I am not going to dress it
up as a triumph.

And the UK's exclusions exceeded PISA's own tolerance this cycle. The OECD still
judged the data fit to report, but hold all of these numbers slightly more
loosely than you normally would.

With both of those said, the thing underneath is still worth sitting with.

### The phones did not go anywhere

English fifteen year olds have the same phones as the fifteen year olds whose
countries fell off a cliff. The same feeds. The same chatbots. The same bedrooms
at midnight, the same group chats, the same everything.

If smartphones were the explanation for the global decline, England should have
gone down with everyone else.

It did not.

I want to be careful, because this is exactly the point where people overclaim in
the opposite direction. This does not mean phones are harmless. It does not mean
the worry is silly. And PISA is cross sectional, so it cannot tell you what caused
anything, in either direction.

What it does mean is that the simplest version of the story cannot be the whole
story, because a country holding the same devices did not get the same result.

And that has a consequence most of this month's commentary has skipped.

If removing the phone were the answer, the countries that kept them would all
have fallen. They did not. So whatever is doing the work, it is not simply the
presence of a device in a pocket, and we have spent three years arguing about
lock boxes on the assumption that it was.

### The number I cannot stop thinking about

Well below the headline there is a category the OECD calls hasty readers.

These are not students who cannot read. They are students who move through a
complex text quickly and answer without going back to it. They get the question
wrong, and they get it wrong fast.

In 2018 they were 6.6 percent of students. In 2025 they were 11.4 percent. Nearly
double, in seven years.

Two limits on that, and I would rather say them than have them pointed out.

PISA cannot tell us why. Again, snapshot, not a study that followed anyone.

And I do not know whether English children are in that number. I have looked. It
is reported as an OECD average and I could not find it broken out for England,
and given England held its reading while the average fell, it is entirely
possible the picture here is different. Anyone telling you this describes British
teenagers is telling you something they cannot know.

But sit with what is being described, because it is oddly specific.

Two children with the same reading age. One meets a difficult paragraph and slows
down, goes back, reads it again. The other meets the same paragraph and answers.
On the test they produce a gap that looks like literacy. It is not literacy. It
is the willingness to stay.

We have spent two decades measuring whether children can read. This is the first
number I have seen that says something about whether they will stop, go back, and
sit with something that resists them. Those are different skills, and only one of
them is taught anywhere.

### The thing that looks like help

The third finding is the one I have thought hardest about and am most worried
about repeating.

Fifteen year olds who said they never or almost never use AI for schoolwork
outscored the ones who do. In science. On nearly every kind of task. The widest
gap was on summarising, where daily users came in around 30 points below non
users.

For scale: a majority of students in most participating countries now use AI
chatbots for schoolwork, and 46 percent across the OECD use one at least weekly.
The most common reason they give is to help me learn.

Before this gets forwarded anywhere, the obvious explanation runs the other way.
A student who is finding the work hard reaches for help more often. That alone
would produce exactly this pattern and tell you nothing about the tool. PISA
cannot separate the two and neither can I.

If you remember one sentence from this letter, remember that one.

So why write about it. Because of which task had the widest gap.

Summarising is not a chore attached to learning. In summarising, the effort is
the learning. The only way to find out whether you understood something is to try
to say it shorter and discover which bits you cannot. Hand that step to a machine
and you have not saved time on the work. You have skipped the part where you find
out.

Two children, same homework, same chatbot, same ten minutes. One asks it to
explain the bit they did not follow, then writes the summary themselves. One asks
it for the summary. Only one of them learned whether they understood, and from
the outside the two are indistinguishable.

The question was never whether children use AI. Most of them already do and that
argument is over. The question is which part of the thinking they hand across,
and I have not yet met a child who has been taught to ask it.

### What the three have in common

Not one of them is a restriction.

Staying with a hard paragraph. Knowing which part of a problem is yours to solve.
Noticing what a feed is doing to you while it is doing it.

None of those can be installed, legislated or handed over on a birthday. They are
taught, practised badly, practised better, and eventually done with nobody
watching.

That is the argument, and England's result is the best evidence for it I have
seen. A country kept the phones and did not fall. Whatever protected those
children, it was not the absence of a device.

### What I would actually do this week

Nothing dramatic and nothing that needs buying.

**Read something difficult in front of them.** Not a performance, just the thing
itself, visibly, with your phone in another room. Children calibrate what is
normal from what they see, and most have never watched an adult stay with
something hard.

**Ask which part they did themselves.** On any homework that involved a chatbot,
ask what they worked out and what it gave them. Not to catch anyone. The question
has probably never been asked, and it is exactly the question they will need in
their own head at eighteen.

**Ask their school what changed.** England held while the world fell, and the
people who know why are in the building. Most schools will not have a tidy
answer. Asking is still worth more than another article.

## Deliberately not here

**Any causal claim.** PISA is cross sectional. Said in the body three times rather
than once in a footnote.

**England as a success story.** The data shows England held while others fell. It
does not show why, and this does not hand anyone the credit.

**The United States.** The OECD's own country note warns US response rates,
exclusions and school participation all fell below target, that bias cannot be
ruled out, and that over 40 percent of responding US students never received the
questionnaire. No reportable US figures on AI, distraction or phone rules exist.
The market is UK.

**The 79 percent AI figure** from the original brief. Could not be confirmed. The
verified numbers are a majority in most participating countries and 46 percent
weekly across the OECD.
