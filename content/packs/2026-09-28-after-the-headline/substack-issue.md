## The issue

The Wall Street Journal ran a piece this month headlined "Yes, Children Really
Are Getting Dumber."

I want to take the opposite position, using the same data.

The OECD published PISA 2025 a few weeks ago. 760,000 fifteen year olds across 91
countries, which makes it the largest thing of its kind we have. The headline is
genuinely bad. Reading across the OECD has fallen from 489 in 2015 to 461 in
2025. That is 28 points, which the OECD puts at roughly a year and a half of
learning, and it is the lowest reading average PISA has ever recorded.

So the headline is not wrong about the direction. It is wrong about what is
happening, and being wrong about that matters, because it determines what any of
us do on Monday.

Three things in the report deserve more attention than they are getting. Each one
is being misread, and the misreadings all point the same unhelpful way.

### One. They are not reading worse. They are stopping sooner

Buried well below the headline number is a category the OECD calls hasty readers.

These are not students who cannot read. They are students who move through a
complex text quickly and answer without going back to it. They get the question
wrong, and they get it wrong fast.

In 2018 they were 6.6 percent of students. In 2025 they were 11.4 percent.

Nearly double, in seven years.

Now, PISA cannot tell us why, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. It is a
snapshot of fifteen year olds sitting a test on one day. It is not a study that
followed anyone. Anybody using this to prove that phones did it is describing
something PISA did not measure.

But sit with what is being described, because it is oddly specific.

Two children with the same reading age. One meets a difficult paragraph and slows
down, goes back, reads it again. The other meets the same paragraph and answers.
On the test they produce a gap that looks like literacy. It is not literacy. It
is the willingness to stay.

We have spent two decades measuring whether children can read. This is the first
time I have seen a number that says something about whether they will stop, go
back, and sit with something that resists them.

Those are different skills. Only one of them is taught anywhere.

### Two. The wall works. It is just not the same as the skill

Here is the second finding, and it is the one most likely to be weaponised in
both directions.

Nearly half of fifteen year olds now attend a school where phones are banned on
the premises. In 2022 it was 34 percent. That is a fast change by the standards
of school policy.

Reading and science did not improve. The OECD looked at the systems that brought
restrictions in and stopped short of endorsing bans, because at system level the
link to performance is not there.

One camp will read that as proof bans fail. They should not, and I am not going
to let this letter be used that way.

The same report associates digital distraction with lower science scores in 59 of
82 education systems, and more than one in four students say devices distract
their classmates. The distraction is real and it is measured. A phone ban reduces
it. Any head teacher who has cleared phones off a corridor already knows this and
does not need the OECD to tell them.

The other camp will read it as proof bans are the answer. They should not either.

Both are wrong in the same way, because they are treating one job as though it
were two.

Protecting a child's environment and building a child's capability are separate
pieces of work. A ban does the first one, and does it well. It was never designed
to do the second, and we keep behaving as though it were.

A school can take every phone at the gate for five years. On the last day it
hands them back to a seventeen year old who has had no practice whatsoever. The
wall comes down and there is nothing standing behind it.

This is the thing I keep saying and will keep saying. **A delay is not
preparation.**

The years behind the wall are the opportunity. They are quiet, they are
supervised, and they are precisely when a child could be taught what a feed is
doing to their attention, and given a chance to practise noticing it. Mostly we
are not using them for that. We are enjoying the quiet, which is understandable,
and calling it a solution, which is not.

### Three. The thing that looks like help

The third finding is the one I have thought hardest about, and the one I am most
worried about repeating.

Fifteen year olds who said they never or almost never use AI for schoolwork
outscored the ones who do. In science. On nearly every kind of task.

The widest gap was on summarising, where daily users came in around 30 points
below non users.

For scale: a majority of students in most participating countries now use AI
chatbots for schoolwork, and 46 percent across the OECD use one at least weekly.
The most common reason they give is to help me learn.

Before this gets forwarded anywhere, the obvious explanation runs the other way.
A student who is finding the work hard reaches for help more often. That alone
would produce exactly this pattern, and it would tell you nothing at all about
the tool. PISA cannot separate the two. Neither can I.

If you remember one sentence from this letter, remember that one.

So why write about it.

Because of which task had the widest gap.

Summarising is not a chore attached to learning. In summarising, the effort is
the learning. The only way to find out whether you understood something is to try
to say it shorter, and discover which bits you cannot. Hand that step to a
machine and you have not saved time on the work. You have skipped the part where
you find out.

Two children, same homework, same chatbot, same ten minutes. One asks it to
explain the bit they did not follow and then writes the summary themselves. One
asks it for the summary. Only one of them learned whether they understood the
material, and from the outside the two are indistinguishable.

The question was never whether children use AI. Most of them already do and that
argument is over. The question is which part of the thinking they hand across,
and I have not yet met a child who has been taught to ask it.

### What these three have in common

Every one of them is a capability, and not one of them is a restriction.

Staying with a hard paragraph. Noticing what a feed is doing to you. Knowing
which part of a problem is yours to solve.

None of those can be installed. None can be legislated. None arrives on a
birthday. They are taught, practised badly, practised better, and eventually done
without anyone watching.

That is the whole argument. Protection buys the time. It does not do the work.

### What I would actually do this week

Nothing dramatic, and nothing that needs buying.

**Read something difficult in front of them.** Not a performance. Just the thing
itself, visibly, with the phone in another room. Children calibrate what is
normal from what they see, and most of them have never watched an adult stay with
something hard.

**Ask which part they did themselves.** Not as an accusation. On any piece of
homework that involved a chatbot, ask what they worked out and what it gave them.
The point is not to catch anyone. It is that the question has probably never been
asked, and it is the exact question they will need in their own head at
university.

**If your child's school has a phone ban, ask what they are doing with it.** Not
to challenge the ban. To find out whether anything is being built in the quiet.
It is a fair question and most schools will not yet have an answer, which is
itself worth knowing.

## What is deliberately not here

**Any causal claim.** PISA is cross sectional and cannot establish that phones,
social media or AI caused any of this. Said in the body rather than in a footnote.

**The United States.** The OECD's own country note warns that US response rates,
exclusions and school participation fell below PISA's targets, that bias cannot be
ruled out, and that over 40 percent of responding US students never received the
student questionnaire. There are therefore no reportable US figures on AI use,
distraction or phone rules, and none are implied here.

**The 79 percent AI usage figure** that was in the original brief. Could not be
confirmed. The verified numbers are a majority in most participating countries and
46 percent weekly across the OECD, and those are what appear.
