# After the Headline · a three part LinkedIn series on PISA 2025

Drafted 28 September 2026. PISA 2025 Volume I was published by the OECD in
September 2026: 760,000 fifteen year olds across 91 countries.

The series name answers the Wall Street Journal headline "Yes, Children Really
Are Getting Dumber" and Jeff Raikes' reply to it, which was to read the data and
reject the headline. That is the position, and it is the one Justin already
holds.

Post one on the day, two and three at two or three day gaps. Never two in a day.

Every figure below is verified against the OECD release. The ledger is at the
foot of this file.

## Part one · The hasty reader

1,676 characters.

Between 2018 and 2025 the share of fifteen year olds who move through a hard text fast and never go back to it nearly doubled.

6.6 percent to 11.4 percent.

The OECD published PISA 2025 this month. 760,000 students, 91 countries. The headline number is that reading across the OECD fell from 489 to 461 since 2015, a drop of 28 points, which the OECD puts at about a year and a half of learning.

That is not the number I cannot stop thinking about.

The OECD has a name for the other group. Hasty readers. Not students who cannot read. Students who go through a complex text quickly and answer without returning to it. They get it wrong, and they get it wrong fast.

PISA cannot tell us why, and I want to be plain about that. It is a snapshot of fifteen year olds sitting a test. It is not a study of what happened to them over ten years. Anyone telling you this proves phones did it is telling you something PISA did not measure.

But look at what is actually being described.

Two children with the same reading age. One meets a difficult paragraph and slows down. One meets it and answers. On a test they look like a gap in literacy. They are not. They are a gap in willingness to stay.

We have spent twenty years measuring whether children can read. This is the first time I have seen data that says something about whether they will stop, go back, and sit with something hard. Those are different skills and only one of them is taught.

This is After the Headline, part one of three.

When did you last read something difficult all the way through without reaching for your phone? I am asking genuinely, because I am not sure I am better at this than my children are.

### Prepared first comment, part one

Sources, because the two numbers are doing the work. PISA 2025 Results Volume I, OECD, September 2026, 760,000 students across 91 countries. Hasty readers went from 6.6 percent in 2018 to 11.4 percent in 2025 on average across OECD countries with comparable data. OECD mean reading fell 489 in 2015 to 461 in 2025, which the OECD describes as roughly one and a half years of learning.

One thing I am not claiming. PISA is cross sectional. It cannot tell you what caused the change, and it does not try to. The reason I find the hasty reader finding more useful than the headline is not that it proves anything. It is that it describes a behaviour a parent can actually recognise at their own kitchen table.

## Part two · The wall and the skill

1,831 characters.

Nearly half of fifteen year olds now go to a school where phones are banned on the premises. In 2022 it was 34 percent.

Reading and science did not improve.

That is from PISA 2025, published this month. The OECD looked at the systems that brought in restrictions and stopped short of endorsing bans, because the link between them and performance at system level is not there.

Now, before that gets forwarded as proof that bans do not work, it is not, and I am not going to let it be used that way.

The same report links digital distraction to lower science scores in 59 of 82 education systems. More than one in four students say devices distract their classmates. The distraction is real, it is measured, and a phone ban reduces it. Any head who has taken phones off the corridor already knows this and does not need a report to confirm it.

So hold both. The wall works. It just does not do the other job.

Protecting a child's environment and building a child's capability are two different pieces of work. A ban does the first one well. It was never designed to do the second, and we keep acting as though it were.

A school can take every phone at the gate for five years. On the last day it hands them back to someone who has had no practice. The wall comes down and there is nothing behind it.

That is the whole problem in one sentence. A delay is not preparation.

The years behind the wall are the opportunity. They are quiet, they are supervised, and they are exactly when a child could be taught what a feed is doing to them. Mostly we are not using them for that. We are just enjoying the quiet.

This is After the Headline, part two of three.

If your school has a phone ban, what are you doing with the quiet it bought you? Genuine question, and I would like to hear from schools who are doing something with it.

### Prepared first comment, part two

Sources. PISA 2025 Results Volume I, OECD, September 2026. Schools banning phones on the premises rose from 34 percent of students in 2022 to nearly half in 2025. Digital distraction is associated with lower science performance in 59 of 82 education systems, and over one in four students report devices distracting classmates.

The thing I want to be careful about. "Not associated with system level improvement" is a much weaker statement than "does not work", and the two get swapped constantly. A national average moves for a hundred reasons. What PISA can say is that the improvement did not show up in the aggregate over three years. What it cannot say is what happened inside any particular school, and a head who tells me their corridors are calmer is not contradicted by this.

## Part three · The thing that looks like help

1,967 characters.

Fifteen year olds who said they never use AI for schoolwork outscored the ones who do. In science. On nearly every kind of task.

The widest gap was on summarising.

PISA 2025, published this month, 760,000 students. A majority of fifteen year olds in most participating countries now use AI chatbots for schoolwork, and 46 percent across the OECD use one at least weekly. The most common reason they give for it is to help me learn.

Daily users scored roughly 30 points below non users on summarising what they had read.

Before anyone forwards this as proof that AI is making children worse, stop. The obvious explanation runs the other way. A student who is finding the work hard reaches for help more often. That would produce exactly this pattern and it would tell you nothing about the tool.

PISA cannot separate those two. Neither can I. If you take one thing from this post, take that one.

But look at which task had the widest gap, because that is the bit worth sitting with.

Summarising is not a chore attached to learning. In summarising, the effort is the learning. You find out whether you actually understood something by trying to say it shorter. Hand that step to a machine and you have not saved time on the work. You have skipped the part where you find out.

Two children, same homework, same chatbot. One asks it to explain the bit they did not follow, then writes the summary themselves. One asks it for the summary. Same tool, same ten minutes, and only one of them found out whether they understood.

The question was never whether children use AI. Most of them already do, and that argument is finished.

The question is which part of the thinking they hand over, and I have not yet met a child who has been taught to ask it.

This is After the Headline, part three of three.

What is the one piece of thinking you would not want a machine doing for your child? I have an answer for mine and I am genuinely not certain it is the right one.

### Prepared first comment, part three

Sources. PISA 2025 Results Volume I, OECD, September 2026, 760,000 students across 91 countries. A majority of students in most participating countries use AI chatbots for schoolwork, 46 percent across the OECD at least weekly, with "to help me learn" the most common stated purpose. Students reporting never or almost never using AI for schoolwork outscored users in science on nearly every task type, with the widest gap on summarising, where daily users were around 30 points below non users.

Saying again what the post says, because it is the part that will get dropped when this is quoted. This is an association. Reverse causation is not just possible here, it is the likeliest single explanation, and the OECD does not claim otherwise. The reason I still think the summarising gap is worth a post is not that it proves AI harms learning. It is that summarising happens to be the task where the effort and the learning are the same thing, which makes it the worst possible thing to delegate, whichever way the arrow points.

## Why the series is shaped this way

**Three posts, not one.** Each is a thing the internet is about to misread, and they
run in the order the misreadings will arrive: what happened, what we tried, what
we are about to get wrong next. One post carrying all three would lose all of
them.

**The honest pivot moves earlier each time.** In part one it is halfway down. In
part three it is near the top and it is the longest paragraph in the post,
because part three is the one that could do damage if it travels without its
caveat. The rule is that the concession sits wherever the reader's objection
would form, and by part three that is immediately.

**Nothing claims an outcome for a child.** No post says a child will read better,
think better or be safer. The claims are about what the data describes and what
is not being taught.

**Each close is a real question Justin does not know the answer to.** Two of the
three admit that in the post. That is the difference between a question that
earns a comment and one that reads as engagement bait.

## What is deliberately not in the series

**Any causal claim.** PISA is cross sectional. It cannot establish that phones,
social media or AI caused any of this, and all three posts say so in their own
words rather than in a disclaimer.

**The United States.** The OECD's own country note warns that US response rates,
exclusions and school participation all fell below PISA's targets, that bias
cannot be ruled out, and that over 40 percent of responding US students were not
given the student questionnaire at all. So there are no reportable US figures on
AI use, distraction or phone rules. The series is OECD and UK. If a US version is
ever wanted it needs different evidence, not the same evidence relabelled.

**The 79 percent AI figure** from the original brief. The verified numbers are a
majority in most countries and 46 percent weekly across the OECD. 79 percent
could not be confirmed, so it is not used.
