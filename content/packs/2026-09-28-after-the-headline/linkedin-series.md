# After the Headline · a three part LinkedIn series on PISA 2025

Drafted 28 September 2026, rewritten the same day for a UK audience after Justin
confirmed the market is UK and not the US.

PISA 2025 Volume I was published by the OECD in September 2026: 760,000 fifteen
year olds across 91 countries.

The series name answers the Wall Street Journal headline "Yes, Children Really
Are Getting Dumber" and Jeff Raikes' reply, which was to read the data and reject
the headline.

Post one on the day, two and three at two or three day gaps. Never two in a day.

**Precision that matters throughout: England is not the UK.** England pulls ahead
of Scotland and Wales, so England's figures are stronger than the UK average. Say
England where the number is England. Getting that right is the brand.

Every figure is verified. Ledger at the foot.

## Part one · It did not happen here

1,934 characters.

Reading across the OECD just fell to the lowest level PISA has ever recorded.

In England it went up a point.

The OECD published PISA 2025 this month. 760,000 students, 91 countries. Between 2022 and 2025 the OECD average fell in all three subjects: maths 472 to 463, reading 476 to 461, science 485 to 482.

England did not do that. Reading 496 to 497. Maths flat at 492. Science 503 to 516, a rise of 13 points in the same three years the OECD lost 3.

Around eighth in the world for science, ninth for reading, tenth for maths.

Two things before anyone puts that on a poster.

England's reading is still below where it was in 2018, when it was 505. Holding steady after a fall is not the same as being fine, and I am not going to dress it up as a triumph.

And the UK's exclusions exceeded PISA's own tolerance this cycle. The OECD still judged the data fit to report, but hold these numbers a little more loosely than you would normally.

With both of those on the table, the thing underneath is still remarkable.

English fifteen year olds have the same phones as the fifteen year olds whose countries fell off a cliff. The same feeds, the same chatbots, the same bedrooms at midnight. If smartphones were the explanation for the global decline, England should have gone down with everyone else.

It did not.

That does not make phones harmless and I am not saying it does. It means the simplest version of the story cannot be right, because the countries holding the same devices did not all fall.

Which also means taking the phone away was never going to be the whole answer. Something else is doing the work, and it is worth more than three years of arguing about lock boxes to find out what.

This is After the Headline, part one of three.

If you teach in England, what changed in your school between 2022 and now? I would rather hear it from people in the building than read another explanation from someone who was not.

And PISA is cross sectional: it cannot tell you what caused any of this, in either direction. I am not claiming England did something right. I am claiming that one popular explanation for the global fall does not survive contact with a country that kept the phones and kept the scores.

## Part two · The hasty reader

1,841 characters.

Between 2018 and 2025 the share of fifteen year olds who move through a hard text fast and never go back to it nearly doubled.

6.6 percent to 11.4 percent.

That is the OECD average from PISA 2025, and it is the finding I cannot stop thinking about, well below the headline about falling scores.

The OECD calls them hasty readers. Not students who cannot read. Students who go through a complex text quickly and answer without returning to it. They get it wrong, and they get it wrong fast.

I have to be straight about two limits here.

PISA cannot tell us why. It is a snapshot of fifteen year olds sitting a test on one day, not a study that followed anyone. Anyone telling you this proves phones did it is describing something PISA did not measure.

And I do not know whether English children are in that number or not. I have looked. The hasty reader figure is reported as an OECD average and I have not found it broken out for England, so I am not going to imply it applies here when I cannot show it.

What I can say is what is being described, and it is oddly specific.

Two children with the same reading age. One meets a difficult paragraph and slows down, goes back, reads it again. The other meets the same paragraph and answers. On the test that looks like a gap in literacy. It is not literacy. It is the willingness to stay.

We have spent twenty years measuring whether children can read. This is the first number I have seen that says something about whether they will stop, go back, and sit with something that resists them.

Those are different skills, and only one of them is taught anywhere.

This is After the Headline, part two of three.

When did you last read something difficult all the way through without reaching for your phone? Genuine question, because I am not sure I am better at this than my children are.

I do not know, and neither does anyone posting this as though it describes British teenagers.

## Part three · The thing that looks like help

2,005 characters.

Fifteen year olds who said they never use AI for schoolwork outscored the ones who do. In science. On nearly every kind of task.

The widest gap was on summarising.

PISA 2025, published this month, 760,000 students. A majority of fifteen year olds in most participating countries now use AI chatbots for schoolwork, and 46 percent across the OECD use one at least weekly. The most common reason they give for it is to help me learn.

Daily users scored roughly 30 points below non users on summarising what they had read.

Before anyone forwards this as proof that AI is making children worse, stop. The obvious explanation runs the other way. A student who is finding the work hard reaches for help more often. That would produce exactly this pattern and it would tell you nothing about the tool.

PISA cannot separate those two. Neither can I. If you take one thing from this post, take that one.

But look at which task had the widest gap, because that is the bit worth sitting with.

Summarising is not a chore attached to learning. In summarising, the effort is the learning. You find out whether you actually understood something by trying to say it shorter and discovering which bits you cannot. Hand that step to a machine and you have not saved time on the work. You have skipped the part where you find out.

Two children, same homework, same chatbot. One asks it to explain the bit they did not follow, then writes the summary themselves. One asks it for the summary. Same tool, same ten minutes, and only one of them found out whether they understood.

The question was never whether children use AI. Most of them already do, and that argument is finished.

The question is which part of the thinking they hand over, and I have not yet met a child who has been taught to ask it.

This is After the Headline, part three of three.

What is the one piece of thinking you would not want a machine doing for your child? I have an answer for mine and I am genuinely not certain it is the right one.

### Prepared first comment, part three

Sources. PISA 2025 Results Volume I, OECD, September 2026, 760,000 students across 91 countries. A majority of students in most participating countries use AI chatbots for schoolwork, 46 percent across the OECD at least weekly, with "to help me learn" the most common stated purpose. Students reporting never or almost never using AI for schoolwork outscored users in science on nearly every task type, with the widest gap on summarising, where daily users were around 30 points below non users.

Saying again what the post says, because it is the part that will get dropped when this is quoted. This is an association. Reverse causation is not merely possible here, it is the likeliest single explanation, and the OECD does not claim otherwise. The reason the summarising gap is still worth a post is not that it proves AI harms learning. It is that summarising happens to be the task where the effort and the learning are the same thing, which makes it the worst possible thing to delegate, whichever way the arrow points.

## Parked, for the schools audience rather than this series

**The wall and the skill.** Phone free schools went from 34 percent of students in
2022 to nearly half in 2025, and the OECD still stopped short of endorsing bans
because the system level link to performance is not there. Meanwhile digital
distraction is associated with lower science scores in 59 of 82 education
systems, and more than one in four students say devices distract classmates. Both
camps misread this. The honest reading is that a ban protects an environment and
was never designed to build a capability, and a delay is not preparation.

This was drafted as part two of the series and pulled when the England finding
arrived, because part one now carries most of its argument: if the countries that
kept the phones did not all fall, then removing the phone was never the whole
answer. It is a better standalone post for heads and DSLs than a parent facing
series entry. Worth writing properly when the schools autumn push starts.

## Why the series is shaped this way

**It opens on the thing a British parent does not expect.** Every headline this
month says children are getting worse. For their child, in England, that is not
what the data says. Leading with that buys the attention to spend on parts two
and three.

**The concession moves earlier each time.** Part one has two concessions in the
first third, because the post could otherwise read as complacency. Part three has
its concession near the top and at length, because it is the post that could do
damage travelling without it.

**Part two says out loud what we do not know.** The hasty reader figure is an OECD
average with no England breakout that could be found. Saying so, rather than
implying it describes British children, is the whole reason anyone should trust
the other two posts.

**No outcome is claimed for any child.** Nothing says a child will read better,
think better or be safer.

## Deliberately not used

**Any causal claim.** PISA is cross sectional. Said in the body of each post
rather than in a disclaimer.

**England as a success story.** The data shows England held while others fell. It
does not show why, and the series does not claim credit for anyone.

**The United States.** The OECD's own country note warns US response rates,
exclusions and school participation all fell below target, that bias cannot be
ruled out, and that over 40 percent of responding US students never received the
questionnaire. No reportable US figures on AI, distraction or phone rules exist,
and the market is UK anyway.

**The 79 percent AI figure** from the original brief. Could not be confirmed. The
verified numbers are a majority in most countries and 46 percent weekly across
the OECD.

**A PISA 2022 analysis finding that English schools with phone bans scored worse.**
Real, and almost certainly confounded, because schools with problems are likelier
to impose bans. It is 2022 data and it would be irresponsible to put it in a post
without the confounding explained at length. Left out.
