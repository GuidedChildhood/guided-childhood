# Australia's ban, three months in, a LinkedIn post from the primary source

Read from the actual eSafety report (Early days, early insights, the three month SMMA evaluation,
July 2026), not the headline. Passes the Haidt test: no fear, no villain, the numbers honest, the
ask is preparation, and it corrects our own earlier framing, which is the most on brand thing we can
do. Justin's voice, viral formula, no dashes. Soft CTA.

Verified figures from the report (sample: baseline 4,121 children aged 10 to 16, three month
follow up n = 803, aged 10 to 15, self report):
- Used a restricted platform in the past four weeks: 85.9% at baseline to 81.5% at three months.
- Held at least one account: 52.4% to 42.1% (statistically significant fall).
- 1 in 3 (34.7%) who held an account had one shut down or deactivated.
- Why they still held accounts: platform had not yet asked them to confirm their age 50.2%; listed
  age as 16 or over 37.1%; platform got their age wrong 18.2%; make up tricks or someone's ID 12%;
  parent helped 10.9%; new account after deactivation 8.5%; VPN 7.4%.
- Parents unaware their child used social media: 23.3% to 33.3% (worse for girls and ages 10 to 12).
- Wellbeing, self esteem, distress, problematic use: unchanged at three months. 65.4% of children
  said the restrictions had no impact on them.
- The report's own framing: too early to judge outcomes, this is whether early change is beginning.

## POST, I told you Australian kids beat the ban with VPNs. The government's own report says I was wrong.

**Post text:**

I have said more than once that Australian children beat the under 16 social media ban with VPNs and fake ages. The government's own three month report just landed, from the actual data, and it says I was wrong. The truth is quieter and far more useful.

Start with the number everyone ran with. Three months in, 81.5% of under 16s were still using a restricted platform, down from 85.9%. So use barely moved. That part of the headline holds.

But here is what the headline missed. Account ownership did fall, from 52.4% to 42.1%, and one in three children who had an account had it shut down. So something happened. The ban is not doing nothing.

Now the part that changed my mind. Why did most children keep their accounts? Not cunning. The single most common reason, in the regulator's own survey, is that the platform never got round to asking them to prove their age. Half of them. Fifty point two percent. The next most common is that they simply typed in that they were sixteen. Using a VPN, the thing I kept citing, was 7.4%. The children barely had to try, because the gate was mostly not switched on. The regulator's own word for it is ineffective age assurance. It is not a story about clever kids. It is a story about a gate nobody built properly.

And two findings should stop any parent who thinks a ban is the plan.

Wellbeing did not move. At three months, sleep, self esteem, distress, problematic use, all unchanged. Sixty five percent of the children said the law had no impact on them at all.

And this one is the tell. The share of parents who did not know their child was using social media rose, from 23% to 33%. Worst of all among girls and the youngest, the 10 to 12s. So the clearest early effect on families was not less use. It was less visibility. A gate that quietly moves a child's life out of a parent's sight is the opposite of what you wanted.

To be fair to the ban, and I will be, the report is careful and says it is far too early to judge, and it did find real early shifts in what feels normal, fewer children feeling everyone is on it. That matters, and I am not going to overclaim in either direction.

But three months of the government's own data says the ban has not cut use much, has not moved wellbeing, and has made some children harder for their parents to see. That is not a reason to despair. It is a reason to do the thing a gate was never going to do. Prepare the child, and keep the parent in the room.

A ban you cannot see behind is not safety. A child who is prepared, with a parent who can still see them, is. That is the whole of what we build, and it is a free place to start, at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack.

When a rule quietly makes your child harder to see, has it protected them, or just moved them out of view?
