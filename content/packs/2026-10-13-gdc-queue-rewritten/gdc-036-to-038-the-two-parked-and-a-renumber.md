## GDC-041 · How would you test readiness

*Was the parked GDC-001. The original was built on a Californian bill whose fate
I cannot verify from here, which is most of why I parked it. The idea underneath
it was always the good part, and it does not need a news peg at all. This is the
only evergreen post in the queue, which is worth having when everything else is
pegged to a date.*

I have built a product around the word readiness and I still cannot tell you how to test it.

That is the uncomfortable centre of this, and picking a better age does not make it go away.

Everyone argues about the number. Thirteen for an account. Sixteen for social media. Eighteen for an AI companion. I understand why. An age is enforceable, auditable, the same for every child, and a regulator can write it down.

What an age cannot do is describe the child in front of you.

A child can turn thirteen without knowing that an AI which sounds caring does not care. Without understanding that a chatbot can be confidently wrong about something it has no business being confident about. Without noticing when a conversation is being kept going rather than finished. With no idea when to stop talking to a machine and go and find a person.

So what would a readiness test actually look like.

Not a quiz. A child can pass a quiz on Monday and hand over their address on Friday. Not a certificate either, because the whole point is that it moves as they grow and as the technology does.

The closest I have got is three things a parent can see for themselves. Can the child explain, in their own words, why this thing is showing them this. Can they tell you about a time they stopped. And do they come to you when something turns, which depends entirely on whether coming to you went well enough last time that they would do it again.

That last one is the real test, and it is not about the child at all.

Our stage check is an attempt at this and I will not dress it up as more. Three questions about your own child, and a stage with the things worth practising next. A starting point rather than a measurement. We have no outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently afterwards, and almost nobody in this field does.

An age tells you when the door opens. It tells you nothing about who walks through it.

If you had to judge whether a child was ready for something online, what would you actually look at?

### First comment, post immediately

Nothing in this one is a research finding and I want to be straight about that. There is no validated readiness instrument for children and digital environments that I am aware of, which is the problem rather than an oversight on my part. The three signals above are my own working answer from building this, not a measure anybody has tested. If you know of serious work on assessing digital readiness rather than digital knowledge, I would genuinely like to read it, because I have looked and come up fairly empty. The stage check is at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack if you want to see what our attempt looks like.

## GDC-042 · Same app, different rules

*Was the parked GDC-025. I had parked it as making the same point as "Two
children, same game", and on a second read that was wrong. GDC-036 is about
capability inside one product. This is about a boundary running through the
middle of a single app, which is a different problem and the harder one to
teach.*

A pupil can move from a school noticeboard into an algorithmic feed without anything telling them they have moved, and I do not know how you teach that.

It is the part of digital literacy I find hardest to put into a lesson, because the thing a child has to notice is the absence of a signal.

Meta says nearly 17,000 US schools are now in its School Partnership Program, and that it is expanding from a reporting route into school Channels, Stories, clubs, teams, directories and verified school communities.

The case for it is real and I am not going to skip past it. Schools already struggle to reach pupils across half a dozen systems nobody checks. If teenagers are in the app anyway, a verified school space beats another portal, and a direct line to the platform helps a school deal with things that cross the gate.

This is a company announcement about a US programme. It is not evidence that putting school communities inside a social app makes pupils safer, or less safe, and I would not use it as either.

But hold the phone the way a fifteen year old holds it.

The school Story sits in the same app as the group chat, the creator they follow, the recommendation feed, and whatever the algorithm decides comes next. Same device, same icon, four completely different sets of rules about who can see them and who decides what they see.

Our instinct has always been to teach children about apps. Instagram is risky. The school platform is fine. That holds up until the boundary runs through the middle of one app, and then it teaches a child nothing at all.

So the thing our lessons ask is not which app you are in. It is which kind of space. Who can see this. Who controls it. Why am I being shown this particular thing. What happens if I reply. When does this need an adult.

A phone free lesson is still a sensible boundary. It just cannot teach the decision at four fifteen, when the boundary has gone.

How does your school teach the difference between a school space and an open feed, when they are one swipe apart?

### First comment, post immediately

Meta's announcement was made on 29 September 2026 and the figure of nearly 17,000 schools is the company's own, in a US programme. Worth being clear what it is not: there is no independent evidence yet on whether school communities inside the app improve communication or safeguarding outcomes, and I am not claiming either direction. The reason I think it matters is narrower and it would matter without Meta: platform categories are collapsing, so "which app" is becoming a worse question than "which kind of space". Our schools scheme teaches that as context rather than as an approved app list, mapped to Education for a Connected World and the 2025 RSHE guidance, at guidedchildhood.com/schools.
