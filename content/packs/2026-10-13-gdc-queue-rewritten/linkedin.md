## 1. From GDC-002 · The relationship with no friction

I do not know what a child learns from a relationship where the other side never gets tired of them.

That is the question I cannot answer, and I have stopped treating it as the same question as whether a child might mistake an AI for a friend. It runs the other way. Not what the machine says to the child. What the child practises when they talk back to it.

Researchers at the University of Washington and Rutgers watched eight children aged 6 to 11 play with generative AI toys. The children started socially, asking names and birthdays. When the toys kept failing to understand them, some began testing them, provoking them, speaking to them in ways they probably would not use on another child in the room.

Eight children, two sessions. That tells us nothing about whether AI toys make children aggressive or wear down empathy, and I would not use it that way.

It did change what I think AI literacy has to cover.

A human relationship has friction in it. Someone gets bored. Someone disagrees. Someone says that is enough. Someone can be hurt. A child learns slowly and badly that another person has needs that exist whether or not they are convenient.

An AI has no feelings to hurt. It can now perform the entire language of a relationship with none of the stakes underneath it.

So our AI module does not only ask whether the answer is true. It asks why the machine is answering like a person, what it remembers, whether you would speak to a person this way, and when a conversation should stop being with a machine and start being with a human. DiGi runs to the same rule: it never claims to need the child, and never sets itself up as the relationship that understands them best.

I would rather teach the friction than assume children will not go looking for the thing that has none.

What would you want a child to notice about a machine that never gets tired of them?

**First comment, post immediately**

The study is Interaction Design and Children, from the University of Washington and Rutgers, circulated in September 2026. Worth being precise about what it is: eight children, two sessions, one product context. It generates questions about how children build a model of these things. It cannot estimate how common any of it is and it does not establish developmental harm. If you want the stage by stage version of what we teach about AI, it starts with three questions about your own child at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack. No sign up to see the result.

## 2. From GDC-024 · Ready for what

When my eldest started asking for a phone, I wanted an age. I assumed somebody had worked out the safe one.

Five years of reading this research later, I am fairly sure nobody has, and I am no longer sure the age is the useful question.

A study in JAMA Network Open followed 8,957 US adolescents. Owning a smartphone at 12 was associated with a 49 percent higher chance of reporting at least one eating disorder symptom two years later, after adjusting for symptoms already present at 12.

That deserves taking seriously, and the inconvenient part goes first. It does not show the phone caused anything. Ownership was not random. The researchers could not see what those children were actually doing on the phone. A symptom is not a diagnosis. And it is US data.

What it does expose is how much we compress into one word.

Getting a smartphone is not one change. It is private messaging. A camera in a bedroom. An algorithmic feed. Notifications after lights out. Appearance content. Friends. Homework. Independence. All of it arriving on the same afternoon under a single question: what age.

The question I find more useful is, ready for what.

So the stage check asks three things about your actual child instead of handing you a number. Their age, what you are dealing with right now, and how much time you can give it. Three questions, no sign up, and you get the stage with the things worth practising before independent access. Whether they can notice appearance pressure. Whether they understand why a feed keeps showing them more of something. Whether they know when to bring an adult in.

Delay buys you the space. It does not fill it.

What did you decide in the end, and would you decide it the same way again?

**First comment, post immediately**

Nagata et al., JAMA Network Open, 25 September 2026. Observational longitudinal cohort, 8,957 adolescents, relative risk 1.49 for any eating disorder symptom, 95 percent credible interval 1.19 to 1.87. Symptoms are self reported and are not a diagnosis. Mechanism and content of use are unmeasured, so this cannot tell you which part of owning a phone matters. The three questions are at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack if you want the stage rather than the age.

## 3. From GDC-029 · Two children, same game

Two children can both say "I'm playing Minecraft" and have almost nothing in common.

I cannot tell you which one is fine. That is the hardest thing to answer for a parent, and the age rating on the box does not answer it either.

Australia's eSafety Commissioner used legally enforceable notices to ask Roblox, Minecraft, Fortnite and Steam what they actually do to protect children. The answers describe four quite different safety systems sitting behind four things that all look, from a kitchen table, like "they're on a game".

During the period covered, Epic and Mojang still relied on a user declaring their own age at sign up. Somebody declaring 18 could reach higher risk communication features without a further check, which in Minecraft could include third party unmoderated servers. Valve relied on self declared age for Steam and did not collect age information at all. Roblox had gone further, bringing in stronger age assurance for some higher risk features in Australia from December 2025.

This is a transparency report, not an outcomes study. It does not show that one platform causes more harm than another and I will not use it that way. It is also Australian, and UK configurations and duties differ.

But it does retire the question parents keep being asked to answer. Not "is this game safe", which nobody can tell you, but what can my child actually do in here.

Who can contact them. Whether a conversation can move somewhere private. Whether the space is moderated. Whether the service has any idea how old they are. Whether they know how to block, report and tell someone.

Which is why our schools scheme is built on capability rather than an approved app list, and why DiGi will not answer "is X safe for my child" with a yes or a no. It asks the mode, the chat settings, who can make contact and what the child can already handle, then gives you a pathway rather than a verdict.

The platform carries the first responsibility here. An age rating still cannot teach a child what to do when the room changes around them.

What do you ask before you say yes to a game?

**First comment, post immediately**

Source is the Australian eSafety Commissioner, "Responses to transparency notices: online game and gaming adjacent services", 1 October 2026. It is a compliance and transparency comparison built from what the providers told the regulator, not a measurement of harm, and I am not ranking the four. eSafety's earlier work found 40 percent of Australian children aged 8 to 17 played online with people they did not know offline, and that is Australian, so do not transpose it to the UK. The capability version for parents is at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack.

## 4. From GDC-032 · The child who never put them on

The most interesting child in the smart glasses argument is the one who never put them on.

I do not have a good answer for that child yet, and almost everything we teach is written for the other one. Do not share this. Do not film that. Check your settings. Ask first. All of it addressed to the child holding the device.

Norway has said it intends to propose temporary restrictions on AI glasses in privacy sensitive public places, with schools, nurseries, playgrounds and youth clubs among those being considered. A proposal, not a ban, and not here.

The other side deserves its due, because it is real. Meta has not ignored this. The glasses carry a recording light, and over the summer the system was strengthened so the camera is disabled if that light is covered or tampered with. There are genuine accessibility uses for wearable cameras, particularly for people who are blind or have low vision. A phone can record without consent too. None of this is new in kind.

What is different is who has to notice.

A phone camera changes the behaviour of the person holding it. They take it out and point it, and the act of recording is usually obvious. A camera built into something worn on a face changes the problem for everyone else in the room.

So the lesson has to run both ways. Does a child know what a light on a pair of glasses means. Would they feel able to say they do not want to be recorded. What happens in a changing room, a playground, a corridor. Who do they tell when a recording gets shared.

That is a privacy and consent capability, and in our scheme privacy and consent are taught years before independent access, because the bystander version of this does not wait for a child to own the device.

Our lessons currently teach a child to use a camera well. They do not yet teach a child how to stand in front of one. That is the gap I am working on this term.

How are schools handling the child who is not wearing them?

**First comment, post immediately**

Reported by Reuters and AP on 5 October 2026. Worth being exact: Norway announced an intention to propose temporary restrictions in selected privacy sensitive places, and child focused spaces are among those under consideration. It is not enacted, the scope and exceptions are not settled, and Oslo schools had already restricted them locally. Meta strengthened the capture light anti tamper safeguard in July 2026, which is a meaningful change and should be said out loud. The bystander framing is mine, not a research finding.

## 5. From GDC-033 · A photograph is information

I cannot tell a parent the chance that a photograph of their child will be misused. Nobody can, and the number I am about to give you does not answer it either.

The Internet Watch Foundation assessed 6,310 AI generated images in the first six months of this year that met the legal definition of child sexual abuse. Across the whole of 2025 the figure was 4,512. Where age and gender were recorded, 98 percent showed girls, and nearly four in five showed children aged 7 to 13.

Before anything else: that is a count of images, not a count of children, and it is not a risk estimate for a family photo. It does not show that posting a picture of your child causes this material to be made. I will not use it to frighten parents into deleting their albums, and the IWF does not use it that way either.

What it does show is that the environment around a child's image has changed. Some of this material is wholly synthetic. Some is made by altering real pictures of real children. Once an image exists, what can be done to it is not what it was five years ago.

The responsibility here sits overwhelmingly with platforms, model developers, regulators and adults. The IWF is arguing for the ability to detect material nobody has seen before, because a newly made image has no fingerprint until somebody finds and assesses it. That is the right fight and it is not a child's to win.

What a child can have is language for their own image. A nine year old does not need a lecture on generative models. They can understand that a photograph is information about them. That sharing it creates an audience. That people should ask first. That they are allowed to say no. And that if an image is altered or used to humiliate them, the first job is to tell an adult rather than carry it alone.

Which is why personal information and privacy sit in the early stages of the passport, years before social media independence. Image consent belongs in exactly the same place.

When did your school, or your family, first teach a child that their picture is part of their digital life?

**First comment, post immediately**

Internet Watch Foundation, published 5 October 2026. The precise figures: 6,310 AI generated images assessed as meeting the legal definition between January and June 2026, against 4,512 across all of 2025. Of 6,221 where age and gender were recorded, girls featured in 98 percent, and children aged 7 to 13 accounted for 79 percent. It counts images assessed, not unique children, and detection practice affects the count, so the rise is not a clean incidence measure. This material is already criminal in the UK. The early privacy stages are at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack.

## 6. From GDC-034 · The safety feature is not the outcome

Here is something I cannot prove about my own product, and I would rather say it than have somebody find it.

I do not yet know whether a child who finishes one of our stages behaves differently afterwards. We measure what a parent reports, and whether a lesson was genuinely passed rather than tapped through. We do not have outcome evidence. Almost nobody in this field does, and that is the whole problem.

Meta, TikTok and X have been in the Administrative Court in London challenging how much information Ofcom can demand under the Online Safety Act. The requests reportedly include how much content was removed or restricted and, the part I care about, how many users were actually exposed to harmful material. The companies argue the requests are too broad and too burdensome. Ofcom says it needs the data to judge whether the regime works, and says it narrowed the scope.

Their argument is not frivolous. A regulator should have to say why it needs data, requests should be proportionate, and producing granular numbers across enormous services is genuinely hard. This was a dispute about the reach of an information power, not a finding that anybody breached a child safety duty.

Underneath it is the question that catches everyone, us included.

An age check can exist. A parental control can exist. A reporting button can exist. A rule can exist.

Did fewer children encounter the thing. Did reporting get easier. How fast was it dealt with. Did it work the same way for a nine year old as for a fifteen year old.

"Safety feature introduced" can hide whether the feature changed anything, the same way "screen time" hides what a child was doing and "social media" hides four different products with four different designs.

So every stage we write has to finish a sentence: after this, the child should be able to recognise, explain or do what. Not "we covered privacy". We judge our own films on how many people started a stage check, never on views, for exactly the same reason.

If you work in a school or in safeguarding: when you bring in a digital safety measure, what do you measure afterwards to know whether it helped?

**First comment, post immediately**

Reported by Reuters on 5 October 2026, on information notices Ofcom issued in February. The hearing has since concluded. To be clear about what it was and was not: a challenge to the scope and proportionality of an information gathering power, not a ruling that any of the three has breached a child safety duty, and not evidence that the Act has improved or worsened anything for children. Ofcom's own May 2026 agreement with X, which set review time targets and quarterly reporting, is the nearest thing to what defined performance measurement looks like in practice.

## 7. From GDC-035 · AI literacy needs somebody to own it

A child cannot spot a confident wrong answer in a subject they know nothing about. That is the part of AI literacy I have no neat solution for, and it is why the phrase makes me uneasy.

Mearns Castle High School in East Renfrewshire has appointed Jamie Laycock as Principal Teacher of AI Literacy, believed to be the first dedicated post of its kind in the UK. S1 and S2 get dedicated lessons. Older pupils meet it through citizenship and personal education, with an elective for seniors. The teaching reported so far covers synthetic images, fabricated answers, bias, anthropomorphism, and the habit chatbots have of agreeing with you.

The cautious case goes first. One appointment proves nothing about outcomes. Another specialist post is not affordable in most schools. And AI should not become the fashionable subject that crowds out reading and subject knowledge, because critical thinking does not float free of knowing things. A pupil checking an AI claim needs something to check it against.

Which is the argument for progression rather than an assembly.

At one stage the question is whether you can tell this image might not be real. Later, whether you can check a claim against another source. Later again, whether you notice when a chatbot is flattering you, agreeing too readily, or starting to feel more like a person than it is.

Those capabilities travel. The tools will not stop changing.

Our schools scheme is built that way, 29 modules mapped to Education for a Connected World and the 2025 RSHE guidance, with the AI work staged across them rather than delivered in one talk, and a real pass rather than a tap through. I am not claiming it improves outcomes, because the pilot has not measured capability change yet. That is the honest position, and it is the same standard I would hold a school to.

I do not think every school now needs a Principal Teacher of AI Literacy. Mearns Castle has made the harder point: this needs curriculum time, progression, and somebody whose job it actually is.

Who owns AI literacy in your school, and what should a pupil be able to do by the end of each key stage?

**First comment, post immediately**

BBC reporting, 6 October 2026, plus Jamie Laycock's own public material. "Believed to be the first in the UK" is the careful wording and I would keep it, because exhaustively proving a first is close to impossible. No evaluation yet establishes effects on attainment, safeguarding or wellbeing, so this is a practice development rather than an intervention trial. A distributed model across subjects may well prove more scalable than a dedicated post. Our module map for schools is at guidedchildhood.com/schools.
