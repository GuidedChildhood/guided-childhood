## The post

Nobody has told me what a twelve year old should be able to do by the time they turn thirteen.

That is the gap I keep falling into, and Europe has just made it a much more concrete question.

The proposed EU KIDS Act would build a staircase rather than a wall. No social media account under 13. At 13 and 14, a parent sets up a limited account with parental tools always on, approved contacts, and no more than an hour a day. From 15, an independent account, with the service still carrying its safety by design duties.

It also goes after the mechanics rather than just the age. For minors it would restrict infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks and attention pulling notifications, require recommenders to prioritise safety and quality over engagement, and prohibit AI systems from using designs that simulate a human relationship in ways likely to create emotional dependency.

The objection goes first, because it is a fair one. Thirteen, fifteen and one hour are blunt numbers. Children develop differently. A law like this can leave everyone feeling that an age has settled a readiness question it has not touched. The Commission does not claim otherwise, and I am not going to pretend Europe has proved our approach right.

But the shape matters, and it is the shape we are heading for too. The UK confirmed its own under 16 restrictions in June, expected in spring 2027.

So here is the question a staircase creates, and I have not seen anybody answer it.

A protected twelve year old does not become a prepared fifteen year old. The protection buys you years. It does not fill them.

What should a child be able to do before the next step? Recognise persuasive design. Explain why a feed chose that particular thing. Hold a group conversation when it turns. Protect their own information. Notice an AI starting to behave like a relationship. Know when something has to come back to an adult.

That is what our pathway is built to stage, which is why it is organised around what a child can do rather than the birthday that unlocks an app.

The Commission is taking feedback from children, parents, teachers and schools until 26 November. Six weeks. School leaders are exactly who it needs to hear from and exactly who tends not to reply to these.

If access becomes staged, what should children be taught at each stage?

## First comment, post immediately

The proposal was published on 17 September 2026 and the formal feedback window opened on 2 October, closing 26 November. Precise status: this is a Commission proposal, not law. Parliament and Council scrutiny can change any of it, and nothing in it establishes that 13, 15 or one hour are the right developmental thresholds, which is a policy design choice rather than a research finding. The specifics above on parental tools always on, the daily limit and parental approval of contacts come from the Commission's own FAQ. On the UK side, the under 16 restrictions were confirmed on 15 June 2026 and are expected in spring 2027, with default protections for 16 and 17 year olds alongside. The staircase framing, and the argument that the teaching should mirror the access, are mine rather than the Commission's.

## Notes

**The close is a real ask, not a flourish.** The consultation genuinely takes
responses from schools, it genuinely closes on 26 November, and a head reading
this can do something about it that afternoon. That is rarer than it sounds in a
policy post and it is why this one earns its place.

**Never.** No claim that the proposal will improve anything for children before
outcome evidence exists. No claim that it validates our stages. No suggestion
the UK restrictions are already in force.
