## GDC-037 · The film is dramatic. The design problem is real.

*The clock: it opened on 9 October, so "in cinemas today" is gone. The argument
survives without it.*

*The angle: the YouTube moment. He watched a recommendation chain lead somewhere
he had not expected, in his own house. He has first hand experience of the exact
thing the film dramatises, which is why he can say the dramatic version is
tempting without sneering at anyone who finds it so.*

I have watched a feed narrow in my own house and I still could not tell you the moment it turned.

That is the honest problem with this, and it is why a film about it is so tempting. A film gives you the moment. A villain, a decision, a turn.

The Social Reckoning is in UK cinemas now, built on Frances Haugen's disclosures and the Facebook Files reporting. That spine is real. The dialogue is not. Timelines compressed, characters composited, an institutional failure given a cast you can look at.

None of which makes the problem underneath it fictional.

Ofcom says personalised recommendations are a main route by which children meet harmful content. Its own evidence also refuses to let me round that up: recommender systems are not inherently harmful, they help people find useful things, and the risk comes from repetition, combination and intensity rather than from the existence of a feed.

The same care belongs with Molly Russell. The coroner did not find that one app or one algorithm killed her. The conclusion was that she died from an act of self harm while suffering from depression and the negative effects of online content.

Accuracy is not softness here. It is the only thing that puts the responsibility where it belongs, with the people who choose the design and the people who regulate it, rather than with a tired parent at nine at night.

Which still leaves the child who is in the feed today.

I keep coming back to the fact that I could not name the turn in my own house. So the thing worth teaching is not what an algorithm is. It is the noticing. This is getting narrower. This is getting darker. I did not choose this and it seems to know what is coming next.

Then pause, stop feeding it, reset the recommendations, leave rather than try to outscroll it, and tell somebody.

A situation a child can learn to interrupt is worth more than a diagram of how it works.

What would tell you that a feed had started choosing for you?

## GDC-038 · Four thousand teenagers are about to test what argument cannot

*The angle: he has spent years following this literature precisely because the
researchers disagree with each other, and he has been waiting for somebody to
run the experiment rather than another survey. The post is stronger when it
admits his own position rests on the same associations as everyone else's.*

I have been arguing about this for years on evidence that cannot settle it, and so has almost everybody else.

Which is why Bradford matters if you have a child between twelve and fifteen.

The IRL Trial has moved into schools. Year groups are being assigned either a daily social media limit with an overnight curfew, or their usual use, for six weeks, and researchers will compare anxiety, sleep, bullying, and time spent with friends and family. It is led from Bradford with Amy Orben at Cambridge, funded by Wellcome.

Nearly everything the rest of us quote, me included, shows an association. This is built to show what changes when use actually goes down.

That is a different thing, and I have quietly wanted somebody to do it for a long time.

The fair objection is that families and regulators cannot sit still waiting for perfect evidence about risks we already know are there, and that six weeks will not answer every question about every child. Both true. It can still trade some of our confidence for a measurement, which I will take.

The result worth reading will not be the headline either. It will be underneath it. Did they sleep more. Did anxiety move. Did they spend the time with people, or move to a different screen.

There are no results yet. Anyone telling you this proves limits work, or proves they do not, has not read it.

And here is the part nobody can hand to the researchers. A limit changes access. It does not teach judgement. If a child gets six quieter weeks, something has to happen in the quiet, or they come out of it with the same instincts and a worse temper.

That is the argument I would rather we were having.

What would you want them to measure before anybody calls it a success?

### First comment

The official Bradford page puts it at around 4,000 young people aged twelve to fifteen, randomly assigned, six weeks, measuring anxiety, sleep and bullying. Wellcome funding of £1.2 million, running to April 2028. Two things I have deliberately not stated, because public sources currently disagree on them: the exact daily cap, and how many schools are in. It is a daily limit plus an overnight curfew rather than a full ban, so it is not a referendum on the Government's policy. When results do come, adherence, other devices in the house, and what the time got spent on instead will all matter more than the top line.

## GDC-039 · The rule I could actually enforce

*The angle he approved: he set time rules because a clock is checkable. Green,
never used in a post, and it turns the draft's most bloodless paragraph into the
confession the post needs.*

I set a timer because it was the rule I could actually enforce.

That is the honest reason, and it took me a while to admit it. A time limit is checkable. I could look at a clock and know whether I had done my job. Who my child was talking to was a much harder question, so I asked it less often.

Ofcom published Growing Up Gaming yesterday, and the numbers have a version of that in them.

Among parents whose children play games, 64% said talking to strangers worried them. Among children who game online, 55% said they at least sometimes play with people they have never met offline.

Only 43% set any rule about who their child can play with. Time limits ran at 56%.

I am not going to turn that into a neat twelve point gap, because those figures come from different questions and different groups and the arithmetic would be dishonest. What it does show is which rule an adult reaches for first.

Two children, both an hour.

One is building something with a school friend whose mum you have met at the gate.

The other is talking to someone they met inside the game, who has started asking them to carry it on somewhere private.

Same hour. Nothing else the same.

The part that costs me goes here. Someone your child met online is not automatically a threat. Ofcom's own figures say 96% of children who game online play with friends or family at least sometimes, and the report describes gaming as a real way to socialise and make things. I am not arguing against online friends.

I am arguing that a timer cannot tell a child who is trustworthy, why somebody wants secrecy, or that a conversation has quietly changed under them.

And none of it is a parent's job alone. The platforms carry a legal duty here that no amount of good parenting replaces.

But tonight, four questions beat the clock.

Who are you playing with. How do you know them. Can they reach you outside the game. What would make you leave and come and tell me.

Which of those four has your family actually had?

### First comment

Ofcom, Growing Up Gaming, published 13 October 2026. The precise bases, because they are not the same and the difference is the reason I would not call it a gap: the 64% and 43% figures come from 2,522 parents whose child plays games, and the 55% from 1,719 children who game online. All self reported, none of it causal. The counterweight is in the same report: 96% of children who game online play with friends or family at least sometimes, and Ofcom describes gaming as a genuine way to socialise, learn and create. Parent rules do not replace what the Online Safety Act requires of the platforms.
