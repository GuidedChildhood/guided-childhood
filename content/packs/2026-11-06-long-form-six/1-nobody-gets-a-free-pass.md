# Issue 1 · Nobody gets a free pass

**Venue:** Substack. **Length:** 1,816 words.

## Subject lines

1. Nobody gets a free pass
2. Meta had a plan for its loudest critic
3. Are we judging the evidence, or whose side people are on?

**Preview line:** A court filing, a newspaper implication, and the week the argument stopped being about children.

---

Court filings unsealed at the end of October show Meta employees discussing a whisper strategy against Jonathan Haidt.

Nobody has shown it was ever carried out.

Both of those sentences are true, and the gap between them is where I want to spend the next ten minutes, because that gap is the whole problem with how we argue about children and screens now.

## What the filings show

The notes come from an internal meeting in March 2023, in a document headed Well-being Meeting Sound-check Notes. They talk about turning academics against Haidt, about getting the academic community to disavow him, and about finding someone to write a piece attacking his work. They were unsealed in the federal social media litigation in California, in front of Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers, and reported from 27 October.

They are also a mess. Full of typos and shorthand, the kind of thing somebody types fast in a meeting where six people are talking. Reporters who have read them describe a brainstorm rather than a plan, and Meta has not commented.

I want to be careful about one line in particular. Two outlets reporting the same note give it differently, one as "turn academics against Haidt" and one with an extra word and a longer tail. I have not read the filing myself, because the court's document service refuses the tools I use, so I have described that line rather than put it in quotation marks. The phrase I am willing to quote is whisper strategy, because every outlet quotes it identically.

That may look like excessive care about two words. It is the entire point of this issue, so bear with me.

## Then the story turned

Candice Odgers is one of the researchers who has challenged Haidt's conclusions. Her review of The Anxious Generation ran in Nature. She is Chancellor's Professor of Psychology and Informatics at the University of California, Irvine, and by her own count she has spent twenty five years on child and adolescent mental health.

On 29 October she wrote that The Times had suggested she and other critics were paid by Meta.

Her answer was flat. Meta does not fund her research. She has never been approached by Meta to shape what she says publicly.

And her name is not in those Meta documents. Not once.

The link between the filing and the people who disagree with Haidt was made by readers, not by the filing.

So in the space of about three days we got a genuine disclosure about a company's media strategy, and then an insinuation that the company's critics were bought, and the second thing travelled on the back of the first.

## Why this bothers me more than it should

The reasoning runs backwards, and once you see the shape of it you see it everywhere.

It goes like this. Her findings are inconvenient. Therefore her motives must be suspect. Therefore the findings can be set aside without ever being read.

That is not an argument. It is a way of avoiding one. And it works in every direction: you can run exactly the same move on Haidt, and plenty of people do, pointing at his book sales and his speaking fees as though a man who earns money from a position cannot also hold it sincerely.

I have had a very small taste of this myself. Question the size of an effect in public and somebody will decide you are defending Big Tech.

I am not.

I want harmful design challenged, and I want researchers free to challenge a popular claim. Both of those things, at the same time, which is apparently an eccentric position to hold.

## What the evidence actually says, which is the bit nobody is fighting about

What makes the motive fight so strange is that the underlying evidence isn't actually a mystery. It's just unsatisfying.

The US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine ran a consensus committee on this and published in December 2023. Its words, not mine:

> Available research that links social media to health shows small effects and weak associations, which may be influenced by a combination of good and bad experiences. Contrary to the current cultural narrative that social media is universally harmful to adolescents, the reality is more complicated.

And on the question everyone actually wants answered, whether social media causes the decline:

> The committee's review of published literature did not support the conclusion that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the population level.

I have read that document rather than a summary of it, because I have been caught out before by a number that turned out to be somebody's paraphrase of somebody's press release.

Now the part that cuts against me as much as anyone. The same report says the rigorous study designs that would settle the causal question are severely lacking. That is not a win for my side. It means my confidence should be lower too, and I am saying so in writing so that you can hold me to it.

Small average effects. Weak associations. Not enough good evidence to settle causation. That's the honest state of the field, and it's nobody's banner.

## What Haidt is right about, which I should say more often

I've spent three sections being careful about his critics, so let me be careful about him too.

The thing Jonathan Haidt is right about is that parents aren't imagining the problem. Something changed in the lives of adolescents over roughly the period he describes, parents noticed it before researchers did, and the instinct to dismiss parental observation as moral panic has been wrong before and is a bad habit dressed up as rigour.

He's also right that the burden of proof has been placed in a strange spot. We generally don't ask parents to wait for randomised evidence before worrying about something their child does for four hours a day. Nobody demanded effect sizes before we put seatbelts in cars.

Where I part company is the size of the effect and the confidence of the conclusion. A generation rewired is a claim about mechanism and magnitude, and the mechanism and magnitude are the two things the evidence is weakest on. The National Academies committee, which had no book to sell, looked at the same literature and could not get there.

Both of those can be true. He can be right that something is wrong and wrong about how much of it is the phones. That combination is unsatisfying and it's where I actually live.

## The one thing the Academies did recommend

Something gets lost when a report is reduced to "the effects are small", and it's the part nobody quotes.

The same committee that refused to conclude social media causes population level harm also proposed a set of practical steps, including design transparency at the platform level, broader education for young people and training for the adults around them, and action to reduce online harassment.

Read that list again. Not one of those is a ban, and not one of them is nothing.

That's the shape of the position that no camp is recruiting for: the evidence doesn't support the loudest claim, and the absence of a loud claim doesn't mean there's nothing to do.

## Which is exactly why we fight about motives instead

A careful answer does not move anybody. "The effects look small and the designs are weak" has never once been shouted at a select committee.

So both camps reach for something that does move people. One side reaches for alarm, because a generation rewired is a story and an epidemic has a start date. The other side has started reaching for conspiracy, because a company plotting against a critic is also a story.

And the second one is newer, which is why I am writing about it. The people whose job is correcting overreach have started overreaching. A Psychology Today piece last month cited a BMJ paper as proof that Australia's ban had failed. The paper does not say the ban failed. It says we cannot tell yet, and its own authors say the full picture may take a decade.

When the referees start playing, a parent has nobody left to read.

## What a parent is supposed to do with a field like this

I think there's an unspoken assumption in most writing about children and screens, which is that the reader will eventually be told what the answer is.

They won't be. Not this decade.

So the useful question isn't what the evidence says, it's how to act while the evidence is unsettled, and those are different skills. The second one nobody teaches.

What I've landed on is that the size of your response should match the confidence of the evidence, and the confidence here is low. Low confidence doesn't mean do nothing. It means prefer the actions that are cheap if you turn out to be wrong.

Talking to your child about why a feed shows them something is cheap if the effect of social media turns out to be tiny. Removing a phone for two years is expensive if it turns out to be tiny, because you've spent trust and taught nothing. That asymmetry is the whole of my position and it survives either outcome, which is the only reason I'm willing to hold it in public.

## What this leaves you with on a Tuesday night

Nothing in the last two thousand words helps you with a thirteen year old who will not come off a screen. I know that. It is the uncomfortable fact underneath every piece of commentary on this subject, mine included.

So here is the one thing I would take from it, and it is a reading habit rather than a parenting tip.

When you next see a study about children and screens in your feed, ask what the finding would look like if it were small. Not whether the study is right or wrong, and certainly not who funded it. Just: if the true effect here were modest, what would the headline look like? Almost always, the answer is that it would look exactly like the headline you are reading, because a modest finding and a dramatic one are written up the same way.

That one question does more for you than knowing whose side anybody is on.

## What to do on Sunday

Pick one claim about screens that you currently believe and that would change what you do if it turned out to be wrong. Write it on the back of an envelope. Then write what would have to be true for you to drop it.

That is it. Not a quiz for your child, a sentence for yourself. I have done it twice this year and dropped something both times, and the second one stung.

If you want somewhere to start with the child rather than the argument, our stage check asks three questions about your own child and gives you a stage with the things worth practising next. I will say plainly what it is: a starting point, not a measurement. We have no outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently afterwards, and almost nobody in this field does.

Meta's motives deserve scrutiny. So does Haidt's evidence. So does Candice Odgers' evidence.

Nobody gets a free pass, and that has to include me.
