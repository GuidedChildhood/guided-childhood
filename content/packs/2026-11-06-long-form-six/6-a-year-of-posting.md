# Issue 6 · A year of posting, and the thirteen clicks

**Venue: LinkedIn newsletter, NOT the parent Substack.** **Length:** 1,813 words.

**Why the venue matters.** This issue is about our own numbers. Putting our traffic
data in front of a parent who is worried about their eleven year old spends their
attention on our business rather than their child, which is the opposite of what
the Substack is for. It belongs in front of founders and marketers.

## Subject lines

1. A year of posting, and thirteen clicks
2. 275,018 impressions and the number that embarrassed me
3. The same story, two openings, fourteen times the reach

**Preview line:** A year of LinkedIn, honestly audited, including the bit where I had been doing one thing wrong the whole time.

---

In the last 365 days my LinkedIn posts were shown 275,018 times, up about 2,065 percent on the year before, and reached 114,314 people.

Thirteen of them clicked a link in a post.

Thirteen. For the year. Not thirteen percent, thirteen human beings.

## The number that changed what I do

Over the same period, 165 people clicked the button on my profile.

So the profile outperformed every link in every post by more than twelve to one, and I had spent a year putting links in posts.

I knew in theory that LinkedIn suppresses posts with external links. Everybody who writes about this platform says so, usually with a confident percentage attached. What I did not have was my own number, and my own number is not a 25 to 60 percent penalty. It is thirteen clicks against 165, which is not a penalty so much as a different mechanism entirely.

The link is not a weak call to action. The link is not the call to action at all. The post is, and the profile is what collects.

Two other things in the account summary are worth more than they look.

**Seventy percent of the reach was out of network.** Which means the posts were travelling to strangers rather than circulating among people who already know me. That is the only number on this page that actually matters for growth, and it is the one nobody reports.

**Saves: 801. Reposts: 121.** Six and a half saves for every repost. People were quietly filing things rather than publicly endorsing them, which tells you something about the subject I write about.

## The near controlled experiment I did by accident

This is the part I would not have believed if it had happened to somebody else.

I wrote about Australia's under sixteen ban twice, a couple of months apart. Same story, same evidence, same argument. Different opening.

The first version opened like this:

> Australia did the thing everyone is asking for. It banned social media for under sixteens. Eight in ten of those...

It did under 1,154 impressions. I cannot be more precise than that, because below a certain level the export stops giving exact figures, which is its own kind of verdict.

The second version opened like this:

> Australia banned social media for under 16s. Three months later, more than eight in ten of those teenagers were still on it.

It did **15,186**.

Same subject. Same facts. Roughly fourteen times the reach.

## What actually differs between those two openings

I have stared at this pair for a while, because if you can see the difference you can repeat it, and if you cannot you are just lucky.

The first one sets up. "Australia did the thing everyone is asking for" is a frame, and a frame asks the reader to wait. Then the fact arrives in the third sentence, by which point the reader has made a decision.

The second one is a complete sentence containing a number that contradicts what you expect. Banned, and then still on it. The contradiction is finished inside the first two lines, and the reader has been given something rather than promised something.

It is not that the second is shorter. It is that the second one closes a loop and the first one opens one.

Scanning the whole year against that test, the pattern holds up. Every one of my scene led openings, the ones that start with a moment in my own house, sits in the bottom third: 1,701, 1,167, 1,164. I like those posts. Some of them are the best writing I have done for the platform. They do not travel.

And the all time best, at 75,842, opens on a peer reviewed meta analysis of 46 studies and lands on a single strange number. Not a story. A number in a sentence that contradicts what the reader believes.

## The thing I got wrong for a year

Here is the uncomfortable conclusion, and the reason I am writing this publicly rather than quietly adjusting.

I had a theory that opening with a personal scene builds trust, and trust is what this subject needs. It is a reasonable theory. I believed it for a year. My own data says it costs me roughly 90 percent of my reach.

The scenes are not wasted. They work in paragraph nine. They do not work in line one, and the difference between those two positions is a factor of ten.

I only found that out because I exported a year of data and read it against a rule instead of against my preferences.

## What I still cannot tell you, which is the honest half

If this were a marketing newsletter it would stop at the lesson. Here is the part that makes me uneasy.

**I do not know which image type works.** The export records impressions, reactions, comments, engagement rate, post age and the opening line. It has no column for format. Every piece of format information I hold on a year of posting is four posts: one research card, which happens to be the 75,842, two posts carrying an article link, at 6,940 and 3,561 with engagement rates under half the text posts around them, and one native video at 1,164.

One card, one video and two link posts. That is not a finding, it is an anecdote with a spreadsheet. My own rule is that anything built on fewer than ten posts is a hypothesis, so the honest answer to "which image works" is that I have no idea and neither does anybody else looking at this account.

**I do not know which newsletter issue ever worked.** Twenty seven have gone out. I hold no open rate and no subscriber trend for any of them. Which means every editorial decision I have made about this newsletter for a year has been taste.

Both of those are one column in a spreadsheet away from being answerable, and I had not added the column, which is the same failure as the thirteen clicks: I was running on folklore where I could have been running on my own data.

## The comments told a different story from the impressions

If I'd only sorted by reach I'd have drawn the wrong conclusion, so here's the correction my own data forced on me.

Across the year: 4,750 reactions and 1,712 comments. But the posts with the most comments weren't the posts with the most impressions, and they weren't close.

The 15,186 post, my second best for reach, ran at 0.73 percent engagement. Several posts that reached a tenth of that ran above 3 percent, and one hit 4.87 percent on 3,288 impressions.

So the surprising number recruits strangers. It does not start a conversation.

What starts a conversation is a named argument or a real question, and those posts reach fewer people and get answered properly. Two completely different jobs, and I'd been judging every post against one scoreboard.

That's changed how I plan a week. Not five posts all trying to go wide, but a reach post, an argument post, and an authority post, each judged against what it was for. The reach post goes first, because it raises the baseline the argument post lands into.

I'd have missed that entirely if I'd stopped at impressions, which is the real argument for exporting rather than eyeballing.

## Seventy percent out of network, and why it's the only number that matters

Thirty percent of the reach came from people connected to me. Seventy percent came from strangers.

I'd been watching the wrong figure for a year. Total impressions goes up when your network grows, which means it partly measures how long you've been doing this rather than whether the writing works. The out of network share measures whether the platform is choosing to show your post to people who've never heard of you, and that's the only mechanism by which a small account becomes a bigger one.

Seventy percent is high. I didn't earn it by posting more.

I earned it, as far as I can tell, by staying in one subject. Children, screens, evidence. Nothing else, for a year, even when something else was tempting and topical. The 2026 shift in how LinkedIn ranks rewards consistency within one defined area over raw volume, and an account that wanders reads to the system as an account with no subject.

The practical version: the discipline that felt like a constraint was the thing doing the work.

## The 801 saves

One more figure I nearly skipped. 801 saves against 121 reposts, so six and a half to one.

People were filing these posts privately rather than endorsing them publicly, and once I noticed that I understood the subject better than I had from any of the engagement data.

If you write about children and screens, your reader is often a parent who agrees with you and does not want their colleagues, their family or their child's school to see them agreeing. The subject carries judgement in both directions: you're either the parent who's too relaxed or the parent who's too strict, and neither is a thing people want attached to their name in a professional feed.

A high save to repost ratio is a signal that your audience is being careful, not that they're uninterested. I'd been reading it as the second thing.

## The three rules I would give anyone writing about a contested subject

**Open on a number inside a complete sentence that contradicts what the reader expects.** Not a bare number, not a number delayed to sentence three. The exception that also works is declared uncertainty, saying out loud what you cannot do: two of my best performers for the year were admissions rather than assertions. Neither beats the other, and both beat a scene.

**Put nothing in the body that asks the reader to leave.** The post earns the reader, the profile collects them. My ratio is thirteen to 165 and I suspect yours is similar.

**Export your own numbers before you believe anyone's percentages, including mine.** Every figure in this issue is from my own account. The agency blog numbers I had been carrying around turned out to be directionally right and quantitatively useless.

## The one thing to do this week

Export your last year. Sort by impressions. Then read the first two lines of your top five and your bottom five next to each other, and say out loud what differs.

It took me twenty minutes and it changed how I write. You may find the opposite of what I found, in which case I'd genuinely like to hear it, because a single account is a sample of one and I've just spent two thousand words being careful about exactly that kind of claim.

One last thing, since this is a newsletter about being honest with data.

The reason I can show you any of this is that I exported it once and wrote it down in a file. Before that I'd been working from memory and from other people's blog posts, and I'd have told you with real confidence that scene led openings were my strongest work. My memory of my own performance was not slightly wrong. It was inverted.

Whatever you measure, the thing you'll learn most from is the number that contradicts what you already believe about yourself. Mine was thirteen.

## Sources

Every number here is from the 365 day export of my own LinkedIn account, taken on 20 October 2026, and held in our repository as `content/analytics/linkedin-365-days-2026-10-20.md`. Account totals: 275,018 impressions, up 2,065 percent year on year; 114,314 members reached; 30 percent in network and 70 percent out of network; 7,505 social engagements; 4,750 reactions; 1,712 comments; 121 reposts; 801 saves; 121 sends; 165 premium profile button clicks; 13 visits to links in posts across the whole year.

The paired Australia posts are both in that file with their opening lines recorded. The lower of the two is given as "under 1,154" because the export does not report exact impressions below a threshold.

The 75,842 post is the Wrong Villain meta analysis post, at 2.09 percent engagement. The scene led openings cited at 1,701, 1,167 and 1,164 are all in the same file.

**The limits of all of this.** It is one account, in one subject area, over one year, with no control for topic, timing, or the fact that I got better at writing over the period. The paired Australia posts are the closest thing to a controlled comparison and they are still two posts, two months apart, with a different audience size behind them. I am reporting what my data says, not what is true in general.
