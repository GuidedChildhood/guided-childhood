I have built a product around the word readiness and I still cannot tell you how to test it.

That is the uncomfortable centre of this, and picking a better age does not make it go away.

## Three answers about the same child, in the same year

Consider one child, turning thirteen this year in Britain.

She can open an account on most platforms, because thirteen is the minimum in most terms of service. She cannot use social media once the new restriction starts, because that line is drawn at sixteen. And she cannot have an open ended conversation with an AI companion on Character.AI, because that company closed those to under eighteens from 25 November 2025.

Thirteen, sixteen, eighteen.

Same child, same year, three different answers about what she's ready for.

Everyone is arguing about which number is correct. Almost nobody is asking why there are three.

## Where each number actually comes from

**Thirteen** is the oldest and the weakest. It is the platform minimum written into terms of service across the industry, and it traces back to American children's privacy law rather than to any finding about what thirteen year olds can do. It is a compliance threshold that became a developmental claim by repetition. Nobody studied thirteen year olds and concluded thirteen.

**Sixteen** is the UK restriction, announced in June 2026. The Culture Secretary has named March 2027 as the start, though the regulations that would fix that date had not been laid when I wrote this, and official statements have also said spring 2027. Sixteen is a political number, arrived at through a political process, and I do not mean that as an insult. Parliament has to pick something.

**Eighteen** is the newest and the most interesting, because a company picked it rather than a government. Character.AI ended open ended chats for under eighteens from 25 November 2025, after lawsuits and an inquiry from the US Federal Trade Commission, replacing them with something narrower and more creative. That was a legal risk calculation, not a developmental one.

So we have a compliance threshold, a political compromise and a liability decision. Three entirely different kinds of reasoning, and the only thing they have in common is that none of them started from the question of what a child at that age can actually do.

## Why an age is so attractive anyway

I want to be fair to the people drawing these lines, because the alternative is harder than the critics admit.

An age is enforceable. It is auditable. It is the same for every child, so it cannot be applied unfairly to one and not another. A regulator can write it down, a company can implement it, and a court can rule on it. Those are real virtues and I would not want to design a system without something like them.

An age also protects a child whose parents are not paying attention, which is the argument I find hardest to answer. A readiness test that depends on an engaged adult does nothing for a child who does not have one.

So I am not against the number. I am against the pretence that the number is the hard part.

## What an age cannot do

What an age cannot do is describe the child in front of you. I know this, having three very different children.

One child can turn thirteen without knowing that an AI which sounds caring does not care. Without understanding that a chatbot can be confidently wrong about something it has no business being confident about. Without noticing when a conversation is being kept going rather than finished. With no idea when to stop talking to a machine and go and find a person.

Another child can be eleven and spot all four.

The age tells you when the door opens.

It tells you nothing about who walks through it.

## So what would a readiness test look like

Here is where I have to be honest about how little anyone has.

It is not a quiz. A child can pass a quiz on Monday and hand over their address on Friday, and I would rather have no test than one that gives a parent false confidence.

It is not a certificate either, because the whole point is that it moves. A child's readiness changes as they grow, and it changes again when the technology changes under them. Something that was true about a child in January can be false in September because an app added a feature.

And it cannot be a measurement of the child alone, which took me a long time to accept.

The closest I have got is three things a parent can see for themselves.

**One. Can the child explain, in their own words, why this thing is showing them this?** Not a definition of an algorithm. Their own account of why this video and not another. A nine year old can often do this better than an adult, and a thirteen year old who cannot is telling you something.

**Two. Can they tell you about a time they stopped?** Any time. Put the phone down, closed the app, left a group chat, decided not to send something. One real example from their own life. Not a promise about the future, a memory from the past.

**Three. Do they come to you when something turns?** And this one is not about the child at all.

Whether a child brings you a problem depends almost entirely on whether bringing you the last one went well enough that they would do it again. If the last time ended in a confiscated phone and a row, you have taught them to handle the next one alone. That is the real test, and the person being tested is the parent.

I find the third one uncomfortable to write because I've failed it. Years ago my son wouldn't come off a dance video and I threatened to take away Saturday football. I knew inside a minute I'd got it wrong, and what stayed with me wasn't the row. It was that I had nothing to give him instead.

## What that looks like at different ages

The three signals don't change with age. What a good answer sounds like does, and I think this is where most readiness advice falls down, because it gives parents one standard for a nine year old and a fifteen year old.

**At nine**, on the first signal, you're listening for anything causal at all. "Because I watched one like it" is a complete pass at nine. The child has noticed that their own behaviour and the screen's behaviour are connected, which is the whole foundation. On stopping, you want one example of leaving a game when something felt off, even if the reason was boring. On telling you, this is the age where it's almost entirely about you: a nine year old who has been shouted at once will go quiet for a year.

**At twelve**, the answers should get more specific and usually get worse, because this is the age where a child starts managing you. On the first signal you want more than cause and effect. You want them to notice that the thing showing them this wants something. On stopping, "I came off because I was bored" is not the same as "I came off because I noticed I'd been on it for an hour and I didn't like it". On telling you, twelve is where most families lose the thread, and it's almost always because the previous report was met with a consequence rather than a conversation.

**At fifteen**, the honest version is that you're no longer testing them, you're asking them. A fifteen year old can usually explain a recommender system better than their parents. What they often can't do is apply it to themselves in the moment, which is a developmental fact rather than a character flaw. On telling you, at fifteen the useful question isn't whether they'd come to you, it's who they would go to, and the right answer doesn't have to be you.

None of that is validated. It's what I've got from asking, and I'd rather give you something specific enough to be wrong than something vague enough to be safe.

## The objection I find hardest

There's one argument against all of this that I haven't been able to answer, and it would be dishonest to leave it out.

A readiness approach depends on an adult who's paying attention. An age doesn't.

If the question is what protects the child whose parents are absent, exhausted, unwell or simply not interested, then a blunt age limit does something that a thoughtful conversation at the kitchen table cannot, because the conversation never happens. The children most likely to be harmed are the least likely to have the adult that my entire approach assumes.

I don't have a good response to that. The weak response, which is still true, is that a line at sixteen doesn't give that child an adult either; it gives them a slightly later start and the same lack of help. The better response is that this is exactly why the drivers nobody legislates about matter more than the platform does, and that's a different newsletter.

But I'm not going to pretend the objection doesn't land. It does.

## Where that leaves our own product, stated plainly

Our stage check is an attempt at this, and I will not dress it up as more.

Three questions about your own child, and a stage with the things worth practising next. A starting point rather than a measurement.

We have no outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently afterwards, and almost nobody in this field does. If anyone knows of a readiness instrument for children online that has been tested against what children actually do later, I would genuinely like to read it, and I will say so in this newsletter if it changes my mind.

That admission costs me something to publish. It is also the reason I think this is worth reading rather than the reason it is not.

## The part the ban cannot reach

In March, or in spring, a line will be drawn at sixteen. Whatever you think of it, something will change on that day and something will not.

What changes is access. What does not change is whether a fifteen year old understands why the rule exists, what they do with a friend's login, and whether they come and tell you when something lands in their feed that they did not choose.

A deadline is not a plan. It is not nothing either, and I have stopped pretending otherwise. It buys time. The question nobody is answering is what the time is for.

## What to do on Sunday

Ask your child the second question, because it is the easiest one and the one most likely to get a real answer.

"Tell me about a time you stopped."

Not tonight, if tonight is already difficult. On a Sunday, in the car or doing something else, when nobody is being examined. Then say nothing and let them think.

If they have an example, you have learned something worth more than any age. If they do not, you have also learned something, and now you know what to practise.

An age tells you when the door opens. This tells you who is walking through it.
