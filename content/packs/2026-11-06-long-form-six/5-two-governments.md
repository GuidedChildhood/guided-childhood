# Issue 5 · Two governments, same mistake, opposite directions

**Venue:** Substack. **Length:** 1,821 words.

**Preview line:** Sweden threw out a policy for not being evidence based and replaced it with one that is not evidence based. Guess which got better press.

---

Five years ago I would have shared the Sweden story without checking it.

Screens out of classrooms, printed textbooks back in. I thought screens were mostly bad for children and that school should stay close to paper and pen, so a country as digital as Sweden turning round would have gone straight onto my feed with something approving underneath it.

I changed my mind since, and not into the opposite camp, which is the part that takes longer.

## The policy is real

Let me start with what is true, because the viral version is not invented, it is incomplete.

In 2023 Sweden threw out its own national digitisation plan. In 2024 it funded printed textbooks. From July 2025, under twos in preschool use analogue materials only.

All of that is accurate, and the reason given for scrapping the digitisation plan is the interesting bit: it was not sufficiently evidence based.

Hold that phrase.

## What the viral version leaves out

Sweden's own education agency never called it a crisis.

Three Swedish researchers went back through how the 2021 international reading results were presented and found two different stories told about the same numbers. The National Agency for Education emphasised the positives. The government called it a reading crisis. Sweden had in fact moved up the league table and declined less than most countries did.

And the two of them named different culprits. The agency said the main problem in Swedish schooling was segregation. The government said digitalisation, migration, and not enough time spent reading.

Only one of those is a villain you can fix with a book order.

I want to sit on that for a moment, because it is the quiet centre of this whole newsletter. When a government picks a cause for a problem in children's outcomes, it is also picking what it will be asked to do about it. Segregation is expensive, slow and politically miserable. Screens are cheap, fast and nobody defends them.

## Nobody has shown the books worked

There is no evaluation of the textbook policy. None.

International reading results published in September had Swedish reading down 21 points. I am not using that against the policy, and I want to be explicit about why: those students were born in 2009 and were schooled before the reform. It judges nothing. If I used it I would be doing exactly what I am criticising.

So we have a policy introduced to correct an insufficiently evidence based policy, with no evidence base of its own and no plan to build one.

## The research is thinner than the story too

Paper does beat screen for reading. That part is real. Across 54 studies the advantage is about a fifth of a standard deviation.

Look only at children and it halves, with a confidence interval that still includes zero.

The handwriting brain study everyone quotes tested 36 university students. Not children. Thirty six undergraduates, and a 2025 commentary disputes the learning claim drawn from it.

I keep meeting the screens out of schools argument in its confident form, and the confident form is not what the literature supports. Nor, and this cuts the other way, does the literature support confident enthusiasm for classroom devices. Ferguson's 2025 paper on school smartphone bans is titled "remain unproven", and an English study of 1,227 students across 30 schools found no difference in wellbeing or attainment either way. I am not arguing for devices in classrooms. I am arguing that nobody has earned certainty.

## Same move, opposite directions

So here is the shape of it. A government threw out one plan for not being evidence based, and replaced it with another that is not evidence based. Same move, opposite direction, and far better press for the second one.

That is not a Swedish failing. It is what happens when a problem is urgent, real, and politically legible, and the honest answer is "we do not know yet". Nobody gets re elected on "we do not know yet".

Which brings me home.

## Britain is doing its own version right now

Britain has a social media restriction for under sixteens coming. The Culture Secretary has named March 2027, though no exact date is officially confirmed and official statements have also said spring 2027, and the regulations that would fix it had not been laid when I wrote this.

Meanwhile, in Bradford, something is happening that almost nobody has heard of.

The IRL Trial launched in secondary schools in autumn 2026. It is co led by Professor Amy Orben at Cambridge and Dr Dan Lewer at Born in Bradford, funded by Wellcome with a £1.2 million award from April 2026, and supported by the NIHR Yorkshire and Humber ARC. Around 4,000 students across years 8, 9 and 10, aged twelve to fifteen, in ten Bradford secondary schools. Half of each year group is randomly assigned a daily cap and a night time block on social media. The other half carries on as normal. The main outcome is self reported anxiety, with sleep, bullying, body image, school life and time with friends and family alongside it.

This is, as far as I can tell, the first serious British attempt to answer by experiment the question the ban is about to answer by legislation.

And after going looking twice, there's one thing I couldn't establish: **nobody has published when it reports.**

Not the trial's own pages. Not the university announcements. Not the funder's project listing. I can tell you when it started and I cannot tell you when it will tell us anything, and I spent real time trying.

## Why that is the finding rather than a gap

I had intended to write that Britain legislates before the trial reports. I cannot write that, because I do not know when the trial reports, and writing it would be exactly the sort of confident claim this issue is complaining about.

What I can say is narrower and I think worse.

The country is about to restrict social media for under sixteens. The only large British randomised test of restricting social media for under sixteens is running right now, in ten schools in Bradford, and no public source will tell a parent when its answer arrives.

Nearly everything the rest of us quote, me included, shows an association. The IRL Trial is built to show what changes when use actually goes down, which is a different and much more useful thing. It will not answer every question about every child in six weeks, and the fair objection is that families and regulators cannot sit still waiting for perfect evidence about risks we already know are there. Both true.

It can still trade some of our confidence for a measurement, which I'll take.

## Why the primary outcome is the thing to watch

One detail about the trial deserves more attention than it's had, because it determines what we'll be allowed to conclude.

The main outcome is self reported anxiety. Not sleep, not grades, not screen time. Anxiety, as reported by the young person.

That's a defensible choice and it's also the choice most likely to produce a result nobody is happy with. Self report is noisy. Six weeks is short for a mood measure. And teenagers who know they've been put in the limited group may answer differently for reasons that have nothing to do with their social media use, which is a problem every behavioural trial has and none of them solves.

So the honest expectation is a small effect with wide uncertainty, arriving into a public argument that has no vocabulary for small effects with wide uncertainty.

I'd rather say that in advance than after. If the trial finds nothing, that won't mean limits do nothing. If it finds something, it won't mean the ban is vindicated. It'll mean that in ten Bradford schools, over six weeks, a cap and a curfew moved a self reported number by some amount, and the interesting work will be in the secondary outcomes underneath.

Did they sleep more. Did bullying move. Did they spend the time with people, or move to a different screen.

That last one is the question I'd most like answered and the one least likely to make a headline.

## The political economy of not knowing

I keep coming back to why "we don't know yet" is so hard to say from a podium, because once you see the incentive you see the same pattern in both countries.

A minister who says the evidence is thin owns the problem and gets no credit. A minister who names a cause and acts gets a headline and a policy, and the evaluation lands long after they've moved department. Sweden's digitisation plan was scrapped by people who will not be in post when the textbook policy is assessed, if it ever is.

None of that requires bad faith. It's just what the job rewards.

The only thing that breaks the cycle is somebody measuring afterwards, which is unglamorous, slow, and the first thing cut. Which is why a trial in ten Bradford schools with no publication date is a more important piece of public policy than most of what will be said about this in Parliament.

## The part no researcher can hand you

A limit changes access. It does not teach judgement.

If a child gets six quieter weeks, something has to happen in the quiet, or they come out of it with the same instincts and a worse temper. That is as true of a six week trial as it is of a national ban, and it is the part that sits with a parent either way.

The fight in my house was never about which book my son was reading. It was about the thing in his hand that kept asking for him back. No book order reaches that, and no policy reaches it either.

A school can change its tools. A parent cannot.

So my bet, and I'll call it a bet rather than a finding, is on teaching a child to notice it.

That bet could lose. If the IRL Trial comes back showing that simply reducing access moves anxiety on its own, with no teaching attached, then I've been building the harder version of something a timer does for free, and I'll say so in this newsletter.

## What to do on Sunday

When the next policy story about children and screens crosses your feed, from any direction, ask one question before you share it: has anyone evaluated this?

Not whether it sounds right. Not whether the country doing it seems sensible. Just whether a single soul has gone back afterwards and measured what happened.

For Sweden's textbooks, the answer is no. For the UK ban, the answer is not yet, and the one British study that might tell us is running in Bradford without a published reporting date.

That question would have stopped me sharing the Sweden post five years ago, and it is the only reading habit I have picked up since that I would recommend to anybody.
