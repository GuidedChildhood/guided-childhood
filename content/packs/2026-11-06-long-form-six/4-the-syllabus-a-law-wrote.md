# Issue 4 · The syllabus a law wrote

**Venue:** Substack. **Length:** 1,847 words. **The Readiness Papers, three of four.**

## Subject lines

1. The syllabus a law wrote
2. A ban is already teaching. The question is what.
3. Half of them said nobody ever checked

**Preview line:** Australia banned social media for under sixteens. Three months in, the clearest result is a lesson nobody intended to teach.

---

Nobody can tell you yet whether banning under sixteens from social media works.

Australia can already tell you what it teaches.

## The numbers, three months in

Australia's online safety regulator went back three months after its ban began and surveyed 803 children aged ten to fifteen. Before the ban, nearly 86 percent were using at least one age restricted platform. After, still above 81 percent.

A separate study from the University of Newcastle, published in the BMJ, recruited 436 adolescents and followed up with 408 of them. It found insufficient evidence that use had meaningfully fallen.

One thing did move, and almost nobody quotes it. The share of children who actually held an account fell from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent. The regulator calls that modest and statistically significant. It is the one measurable thing the ban has done so far and it is missing from both sides of the argument, which tells you something about how the argument is conducted.

I am not going to tell you the ban failed. The regulator says plainly that its report is not an assessment of whether platforms complied, the BMJ paper's own authors say the full picture may take a decade, and it is one country with self reported answers. Anyone claiming three months settles this has not read it.

## Then look at how

This is the part that stopped me.

The BMJ study found the routes you would expect. Fake accounts, someone else's account, private browsing. VPN use was the smallest at two to three percent, which is worth saying because VPNs are what everyone assumes.

The regulator found something blunter. Of the children who still had accounts, about half said the platform had never checked their age. And 37 percent had simply put themselves down as sixteen or over.

Read that again.

Not circumvented. Not hacked. Typed a different number into a box.

That's the single most consequential finding in either study, and it arrived as a footnote in both. We spent two years arguing about whether a ban could be enforced, and the answer turns out to be that in roughly half of cases nobody attempted to enforce it at all.

## The lesson, and who delivered it

That is a child's first real lesson in digital life, and a law delivered it.

The lesson is that identity online is something you assert, and nobody checks.

I do not think anybody intended to teach that. A law was passed to protect children and its most measurable early effect has been to demonstrate, at national scale, that the gate is a formality. Hundreds of thousands of children now have direct personal experience of saying they are older than they are and nothing happening.

And once a child knows that, they know it for everything. Not just social media. Every age gate, every terms of service, every box that asks for a date of birth.

## We taught it first

Before I get too comfortable, the uncomfortable half.

Parents were always the teacher here. A survey of a thousand American parents, published in First Monday in 2011, found that around a third had a child who joined Facebook before turning thirteen, and that two thirds of those parents helped set the account up. The authors' point was not that those parents were careless. It was that an age rule parents do not accept gets opened by the parents.

I am in no position to be smug about it. During Covid we had two small girls at home and a business to keep alive, and we used YouTube as a babysitter to get through the day.

A ban only changes the syllabus. It does not change who is standing at the front.

## None of which makes the ban wrong

I want to resist the pull of my own argument here, because it would be easy to turn all of the above into "so the ban is pointless" and that is not what I think.

A protected period is worth having. If a thirteen year old spends two more years not being recommended content by a system optimised for engagement, that is two years, and two years at that age is a long time.

A deadline is not a plan. It is not nothing either.

What it buys is time. What nobody is answering is what the time is for.

## France looked at its own version and struck it down

There is a useful counterweight from a country that got further than most.

In August, France's constitutional council struck down the under fifteen ban at the heart of its own new law, two weeks before it was due to start. The rest of the law stands.

The main ground was proportionality: a blanket ban on every under fifteen, whatever the child and whatever the service, was out of proportion. The second ground was separate and in some ways more interesting. Parliament had written no legal safeguards for a scheme that, to work at all, requires every user to prove their age, adults included.

That is the age check problem arriving by the back door. You cannot check children without building a system that checks everybody, and France's highest constitutional authority decided that parliament had not thought about what that meant.

Britain has its own version coming, and the argument will be the same one.

## What both sides are counting

Something I notice about every piece of commentary on the Australian experiment, including some of mine.

Both sides are counting the same thing. Whether children got in. Whether children were kept out.

Nobody is counting what a sixteen year old can actually do.

## The eighteen percent nobody mentions

There's a third figure in the regulator's report that I think is the most interesting of the lot, and I've not seen anyone quote it.

Eighteen percent of the children who still had accounts said they'd been **wrongly estimated as over sixteen**.

Not that they lied. That the system looked at them and guessed wrong in their favour.

That's a different problem from the 37 percent who typed a false age, and it's a worse one, because it can't be fixed by better enforcement of honesty. It's the age estimation technology being asked to do something at the exact point where it's least reliable, which is around the threshold itself.

And it cuts both ways, which is the part that should worry a parent more than the circumvention. If the system wrongly waves through eighteen percent of under sixteens, it's also wrongly stopping some number of sixteen and seventeen year olds who are entitled to be there. Nobody surveys those children, because a child who's been refused doesn't show up in a study of children who got in.

## So what should the protected period contain

If a ban buys two years, the obvious question is what goes in them, and I've not seen a government anywhere answer it.

Here's the test I'd apply to any proposal. Does it change what the child can reach, or does it change what the child can do? Almost everything on offer is the first kind. Age checks, device limits, school phone policies, app timers: all access, none judgement.

The second kind is harder to legislate because it can't be audited. You cannot inspect a school and confirm that a fourteen year old would notice a feed narrowing. There's no checkbox for it, which is exactly why it keeps losing to things that have checkboxes.

What I'd want in those two years is narrow and unglamorous. That a child can say why something is being shown to them. That they've stopped something once, on purpose, and can tell you about it. That when something turns, coming to an adult is a live option rather than a theoretical one.

Three things. None of them inspectable, all of them observable at your own kitchen table.

There is no measure, anywhere in any of this, of whether a young person leaving a protected period understands why a feed shows them what it shows them, or can notice when a conversation has quietly changed under them, or would tell an adult when something lands that they did not choose. We are measuring the door, in detail, repeatedly, and ignoring the room.

## What Australia will actually be able to tell us, and when

Worth being clear about what this experiment can and can't deliver, because Britain is about to lean on it heavily in both directions.

Three months is nothing. The BMJ authors say the full picture may take a decade, and they're right for a boring reason: the children affected are still children. You cannot measure what a protected adolescence does to an adult until the adolescent is an adult.

What three months can show is implementation. Did the checks run. Did accounts close. Did children find routes round. All of that is answerable now, and all of it is about the machinery rather than the child.

What it cannot show is whether any of it helped.

So when somebody tells you Australia proves the ban works, or proves it failed, they're describing the machinery and calling it the outcome. The regulator itself says its report is not an assessment of whether platforms complied, which is a remarkably careful sentence for a regulator to publish about its own data.

My own prediction, recorded so you can hold me to it: the implementation numbers will keep improving, the circumvention numbers will keep falling, and in three years we still won't know whether Australian sixteen year olds are better off. Those are different questions and only one of them gets measured.

## The thing I had nothing to say

Years ago my son was watching a dance video and would not come off it, and I threatened to take away Saturday football.

I knew inside a minute I had got it wrong. What stayed with me was not the row. It was that I had nothing to give him instead. I had a punishment and no alternative, which is the position a ban puts a whole country in.

We spent a lot of nine o'clocks after days like that wishing something existed that would help us manage the devices rather than just switch them off.

A ban switches them off. It does not sit down with your child.

## What to do on Sunday

If a protected period is coming, the question is what happens during it, and that is not a government's job.

So: pick the one thing you would want your child to understand by the day the checks start, and name it out loud to them. Just one. Not a lecture and not a list.

Mine, for what it is worth, is that an account is a claim and not a fact. That everything online rests on somebody having said who they are, and that this applies to the person they are talking to as much as to them.

That single idea does more work than any number of hours saved, and it is the one thing the Australian data accidentally proved.

If you want the structured version, our stage check asks three questions about your own child and gives you a stage with the things worth practising next. A starting point rather than a measurement. We hold no outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently afterwards, and I would rather say that than imply otherwise.

So if the door opens on your child's sixteenth birthday, what do you want them to come and tell you first?

## Sources, and where I have been careful

**Australia, the regulator.** The eSafety Commissioner's three month follow up, published July 2026, surveyed 803 children aged 10 to 15. It found 85.9 percent using at least one age restricted platform before the ban and 81.5 percent after, measured as using a platform with or without an account, so it includes logged out viewing that the Act does not restrict. Account ownership fell from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent, which eSafety calls modest but statistically significant. eSafety states plainly that the report is not an assessment of whether platforms complied. The reasons children gave come from the same report: about half said the platform never checked their age, 37 percent had listed their age as 16 or above, and 18 percent said they were wrongly estimated as over 16.

**Australia, the study.** The University of Newcastle study in the BMJ recruited 436 adolescents aged 12 to under 17 and followed up with 408 three months later. Its reported circumvention categories are fake accounts at 15 to 19 percent, someone else's account at 9 to 29 percent, private browsing at 6 to 11 percent and VPN use at 2 to 3 percent. It found insufficient evidence of a substantive early reduction, and its authors say the full impacts may not be clear for a decade. One Australian state, self reported, with confidence intervals wide enough that modest effects cannot be ruled out, so I am not using it to say the ban failed.

**France.** Conseil constitutionnel decision 2026-911 DC, 14 August 2026, which struck down Article 1, the under fifteen ban, two weeks before it was due to start. The rest of the law stands. The main ground was proportionality. The second, separate ground was that parliament had provided no legal guarantees for a scheme that by its own operation requires every user, adults included, to prove their age.

**The 2011 finding** is boyd, Hargittai, Schultz and Palfrey in First Monday: a survey of 1,007 American parents, 36 percent of whom said their child joined Facebook before 13, with 68 percent of those parents helping set the account up. US sample, parent reported, and about a platform's own terms rather than a law.

**On school based restriction more broadly**, because somebody always asks: Christopher Ferguson's 2025 paper is titled Smartphone bans in schools remain unproven, and an English study of 1,227 students across 30 schools found no difference in wellbeing or attainment, while Norwegian work across roughly 400 middle schools did find lower bullying and better grades for girls. The field is split and I am not pretending otherwise.

**The scenes** with my son and with Covid are mine and both happened.
