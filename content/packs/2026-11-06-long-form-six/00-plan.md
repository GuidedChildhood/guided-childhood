# Six long form issues, 6 November 2026

Justin, 6 November 2026: "can we do all of them", answering the six ideas in
`content/newsletter/long-form-ideas.md`.

All six are written. The five Substack issues are 1,800 to 2,500 words, the
standard the ideas file proposed, which is what the best recent issues already
were rather than the 800 to 1,200 the content engine skill used to specify.

## The length note, after Justin caught an error

Issue 6 was first written at 1,813 words, because the Substack standard was
applied to it without asking whether a LinkedIn newsletter behaves the same way.
Justin asked "it's not LinkedIn newsletter length?" and he was right: nobody had
checked.

What checking found:

- **We hold no data of our own on newsletter length.** Not for Substack, not for
  LinkedIn. Twenty seven Substack issues have gone out with no open rate held
  anywhere in this repo.
- **External guidance is weak and contradictory.** Newsletter advice clusters at
  800 to 1,500 words, article advice at 1,500 to 2,500, all of it from vendor
  blogs with undisclosed methods.
- **What we do know, from `linkedin-engagement.md`,** is that three LinkedIn
  article posts came fourth, fifth and eighth of about fourteen, above every stat
  card but one, and that a LinkedIn newsletter link is not an off platform link
  because the penalty is for sending people away. The reason to use the
  newsletter is that subscribers get notified, which is distribution that does
  not depend on the feed.

So the standard is now split: **Substack 1,800 to 2,500, LinkedIn 1,200 to
1,600**, with the LinkedIn figure openly a judgement sitting in the overlap of
the external ranges rather than a finding. Issue 6 is trimmed to 1,585.

**And a LinkedIn newsletter is two deliverables, not one.** Our single previous
LinkedIn newsletter, for the Social Reckoning issue on 18 October, paired the
article with a 141 word note to the network, which is what shows in the feed when
the edition publishes. Issue 6 had no note until this was spotted. It now carries
one at 142 words, at the foot of its file. Both points are written into the skill
so the next session inherits them.

## What changed from the ideas file, and why

**Issue 5 was rebuilt.** The idea said the argument was that Britain legislates
before the trial that tests the ban reports. That claim cannot be supported: the
IRL Trial publishes no expected reporting date anywhere, and two passes could not
find one. The honest version is stronger and is what the issue now runs on. The
trial started in autumn 2026, the Culture Secretary has named March 2027, and
nobody has published when the trial reports. We are legislating while the only UK
randomised test of the thing being legislated is still running, with no public
date for its answer.

**A correction to our own working notes.** A first check reported that the trial
had no overnight curfew and that Amy Orben's involvement could not be found, which
would have made GDC-038 wrong. Both were wrong. Cambridge's own announcement and
St John's College confirm a daily cap and a night time block, co led by Professor
Amy Orben at Cambridge with Dr Dan Lewer at Born in Bradford, about 4,000 students
in years 8 to 10 across 10 Bradford secondary schools, funded by Wellcome at
£1,200,000 from 1 April 2026 with NIHR support (NIHR205448). GDC-038 is right on all of that. What it should not
claim is "running to April 2028" or results in summer 2027, because neither is
findable.

**The ban date is now pinned as far as it can be.** LBC reports the Culture
Secretary Lisa Nandy naming March 2027 at Labour Conference. Smartphone Free
Childhood's own parent FAQ says she expects March 2027 but that no exact date is
officially confirmed, and Hansard and the Commons statement say spring 2027. The
regulations that would fix it are due to be laid before the end of 2026 and had
not been laid as of these searches. Every issue below uses that formulation and
none of them states a bare date. This also answers the open "March, not spring"
row in the register's idea list.

  the consultation and the title and date are all confirmed through reputable
  secondary reporting including verbatim quotes. The 94 percent, the 90 percent and
  the 150,000 rest on a single compliance vendor's summary, and issue 3 says so in
  the text rather than in a footnote.
- **The eSafety 54 percent** is not used in any of these six. It is the one number
  from the queue that nothing outside eSafety corroborates.
- **The IRL Trial's reporting date** does not exist publicly. No issue claims one.

## House rules held throughout

No dashes of any kind. Contractions and at least one very short paragraph in each
issue, which is the register fault the 5 November scrutiny pass found across nine
LinkedIn posts. One thing to do on Sunday in every issue. Sources at the foot of
each, with the limits stated rather than buried. No outcome claim for any child.
The stage check appears as a starting point, never as a measurement, because we
hold no outcome evidence that a child who finishes a stage behaves differently
afterwards.

Issue 6 is the one that does not go to parents. It is about our own numbers, and
putting those in front of a worried parent spends their attention on our business
rather than their child.

flagged them. They are not faults: they are the three readiness signals, the
"who holds the file" test, and the test to apply to any proposal. The one closing
question rule is a LinkedIn post mechanic and does not apply to a long form
issue. This is now written into the content engine skill.
