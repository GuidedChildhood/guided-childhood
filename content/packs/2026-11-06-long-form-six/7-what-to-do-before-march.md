The Culture Secretary has named March 2027.

No exact date is official, the regulations that would fix it hadn't been laid when I wrote this, and official statements elsewhere still say spring. So call it roughly eighteen weeks, and hold it loosely.

And one thing is worth being clear about, because almost nobody says it out loud.

On the day it lands, nothing arrives for your child except a closed door.

## What the ban does, and what it leaves exactly where it is

The three previous issues in this series went through this in detail, so I'll be brief.

A restriction changes access. Ofcom has named four ways of checking who is sixteen, and says plainly that no method is "completely robust against circumvention by children". Australia, three months into its own version, still had more than eight in ten of the relevant age group using at least one covered platform, and about half the children who still held accounts said the platform had never checked their age.

One thing did move there. Account ownership fell from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent. That's real and it's the only measurable effect so far.

So the honest summary is that the door will mostly work, will not entirely work, and will not say a single word to your child about why it's there.

That's the gap. Eighteen weeks is how long you have to do something about it, and you do not need my product to use the time.

## The mistake I'd most want you to avoid

The instinct, if you're the sort of parent who plans, is to treat March as a deadline to prepare for. Get ahead of it. Tighten up now so the transition is smooth.

I think that's backwards, and I've watched it go wrong.

If you take something away in November to get ahead of a rule that arrives in March, your child experiences a punishment with no cause. Nothing happened. They did nothing. The thing they enjoy went away because of a news story. And the lesson they take is not about judgement or safety. It's that access is arbitrary and the adult is the weather.

That matters because of the third readiness signal, the one I keep going on about: whether a child comes to you when something turns depends almost entirely on whether the last time went well enough that they'd do it again. A confiscation that your child experiences as unfair is a withdrawal from exactly the account you'll need in 2027.

So the four things below are all additions. None of them is a removal.

## One. Find out what they actually use, not how long

Most parents, me included for years, know the number of hours and almost nothing else.

The useful inventory is different. Which apps. Which three people they talk to most. Which one thing they'd be most upset to lose. Whether anyone they talk to regularly is someone they've never met in person.

That last one is worth real attention. Ofcom's gaming research found that among children who game online, 55 percent at least sometimes play with people they've never met offline, while only 43 percent of parents set any rule about who their child plays with. Time limits ran at 56 percent. Those figures come from different questions and different groups so I won't turn them into a neat gap, but the direction is clear enough: we reach for the rule we can check.

Do this as curiosity, not audit. If it feels like an inspection you'll get an inspection's answers.

**Time needed:** one conversation, this week.

## Two. Have the one conversation that isn't about rules

Ask why something is being shown to them.

Not "how long have you been on that". Not "who are you talking to". Just, pointing at whatever is on the screen, ask why they think it gave them that one.

I've come to think this is the single highest value thing a parent can do in this whole area, for a reason that took me a while to see. A child who can answer that question has a model of the thing they're using. A child who can't is inside something they experience as weather.

And it's a question you can ask fifty times without it becoming a row, because it isn't about them. It's about the machine.

If they say "because I watched one like it", that's a complete answer for a nine year old. If they're thirteen and the best they have is "it just does", you've learned where to start.

**Time needed:** thirty seconds, repeatedly, for the next eighteen weeks.

## Three. Decide your own answer on the age check trade, before you're asked

Ofcom will consult on draft guidance once the regulations are laid. Parents are rarely the loudest voice in those, and the method is not settled.

The four on the table are a passport or licence upload, a reusable digital ID, a bank based check, and a facial age scan. Each asks your family for something different, and I went through the trades in the previous issue.

The thing to do now is pick one. Actually choose, in your own head, which you'd accept for your own child.

That sounds like homework with no reward. It isn't. An answer given during a consultation is worth considerably more than an objection raised after the system is built, and the people who will respond to that consultation with a settled view are mostly not parents.

**Time needed:** ten minutes, once, before the end of the year.

## Four. Practise the handover, in the opposite direction from a ban

This is the one that takes the whole eighteen weeks, and it's the one a law can't touch.

A restriction removes a decision from a child. Every month between now and March, hand one back.

Not a big one. Something with a real consequence that's also survivable. Who they follow. When the device goes in the kitchen. Whether a group chat stays on overnight. Let them decide, let them get it wrong, and then do the hard part, which is not saying I told you so.

Because the ban guarantees one thing. At some point the door opens, whether that's at sixteen or on their eighteenth birthday or the first time they use someone else's login.

And on that day, the only thing standing between your child and the feed is the judgement they've practised.

A child who has never made a decision about this cannot start making good ones at sixteen. That's not a moral claim, it's just how practice works.

**Time needed:** one decision a month, handed over on purpose.

## If your child is fourteen now

Everything above assumes you're preparing a child for something they haven't got yet. For a good number of you that isn't the situation, and I think this group has been almost entirely ignored in the coverage.

A child who's fourteen now will be fifteen when this lands. They already have the accounts. They already have the group chats, and in a lot of cases the friendships that live inside them. For them the restriction isn't a door that stays shut a bit longer. It's a removal.

That's a completely different experience and it deserves saying plainly: your child is likely to experience the ban as something being taken, by the government, for reasons they'll consider unfair, at the age when fairness matters most to them.

Two things follow.

The first is that you are not the author of this and you should say so, early and more than once. Not as a way of ducking it. Because a child who thinks you chose this will route around you, and a child who knows you didn't has somebody to be annoyed with it alongside. That distinction is worth more than any rule.

The second is more practical. If the accounts are going to close, the thing worth saving is the contact, not the content. A group chat that lives inside a covered platform disappears with the account. Phone numbers don't. Somewhere in the next eighteen weeks is a conversation about making sure the four or five friendships that actually matter have a route that survives March, and that conversation lands much better in January than in the week it happens.

I would not make that a project. I'd make it one remark, once, and let them do it themselves.

## Where I should be careful, and am

I'd be a hypocrite if I spent three issues demanding proportionate claims and then sold you this as a programme.

So: none of the four is tested. I have no trial, no outcome data, and no evidence that a child who does these things behaves differently at sixteen from a child who doesn't. Nobody has that evidence, which is a problem with the field and not an excuse for me.

What I can tell you is that these are the actions I'd pick on the asymmetry argument. They're cheap if the effect of all this turns out to be small, which the evidence suggests it is. Taking a phone away for two years is expensive if the effect turns out to be small, because you've spent trust and taught nothing.

And there's one external support I'll lean on, lightly. The US National Academies committee that refused to conclude social media causes population level harm also recommended, in the same report, broader education for young people and training for the adults around them. That's not a vindication of my four things. It is the most cautious body in this field saying that teaching and adult capability belong in the response.

## The thing I should say even though it isn't about screens

If you can only do one thing in the next eighteen weeks, and some weeks that's the honest situation, do the one that's about the relationship rather than the device.

The reason isn't sentimental. It's that the things which most strongly predict how a child's mental health goes are mostly not on the front page of the ban coverage: what the family is carrying, whether there's money, whether the adults are alright, whether help exists when it's needed. Social media sits a long way down that list.

A parent who gets the relationship slightly better has done more than a parent who gets the settings perfect. I've been both.

## What to do on Sunday

Do the second one. It's free, it takes thirty seconds, and it's the only one of the four that works at every age.

Sit down next to them while they're on something. Point at the screen. Ask why they think it gave them that one.

Then say nothing for ten seconds.

That pause is the hard part, and it's where the answer actually comes from.

What would your child say if you asked them that tonight?

**UK timing.** The Culture Secretary named March 2027 in remarks reported from Labour Conference. No exact date is officially confirmed, a parent FAQ from Smartphone Free Childhood notes as much, and Hansard and the Commons statement refer to spring 2027. The first regulations were due to be laid before the end of 2026 and had not been laid when this was written. The powers sit in the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026, amending the Online Safety Act 2023. "About eighteen weeks" is arithmetic from an unconfirmed date and is stated as such.

**Ofcom.** Rapid assessment of highly effective age assurance at 16, 29 October 2026. The four methods, and the quoted wording that no age assurance method is "completely robust against circumvention by children". I have **not read the primary document**, because ofcom.org.uk refuses the tools I use; everything here is traced to reputable secondary reporting including verbatim quotation. The commitment to consult on draft guidance once the regulations are laid comes from the same reporting.

**Australia.** The eSafety Commissioner's three month follow up, July 2026, 803 children aged 10 to 15: 85.9 percent using at least one age restricted platform before and 81.5 percent after, measured with or without an account; account ownership down from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent, which eSafety calls modest but statistically significant; about half of those still holding accounts said the platform never checked their age. eSafety states its report is not an assessment of whether platforms complied.

**Ofcom on gaming.** Growing Up Gaming, 13 October 2026. The 64 percent and 43 percent figures come from 2,522 parents whose child plays games; the 55 percent from 1,719 children who game online. Different bases, which is why I have not presented them as a single gap. The same report finds 96 percent of children who game online play with friends or family at least sometimes, and describes gaming as a genuine way to socialise and create.

**The National Academies.** Social Media and Adolescent Health, consensus study highlights, December 2023, which I read directly. Its review did not support the conclusion that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the population level, and it recommends among other things broader education for young people and training for adults. The same report says the rigorous designs needed to settle causation are severely lacking.

**The four things are mine and are not tested.** No trial, no outcome data, no evidence that doing them changes what a child does at sixteen. I have said so in the body rather than only here.

**On our stage check**, the line in the piece is literal. We hold no outcome evidence that finishing a stage changes a child's behaviour.
