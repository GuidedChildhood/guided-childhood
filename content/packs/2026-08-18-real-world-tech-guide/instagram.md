## DAY 2, It was never the minutes
**Hook (image text):** 70% of parents give a toddler a screen for a break.

**Caption:**

Seventy percent of parents of under fours allow screen time because they need a break.

I have been that parent. No finger wagging here.

But here is what surprised me in the research.

The experts did not answer it with a time limit. They answered it with two words. Connection, and quality.

When you hand a small child a screen to calm them, they learn that the screen is what calms them. Not that they can calm themselves.

The cost is not the minutes. It is the skill that never gets built.

So the goal is not less screen. It is a child who can settle themselves.

Stage one of five.

What calmed you down as a kid, before a screen was an option?

## DAY 3, A tool, not a default
**Hook (image text):** 39% of parents track their kids. Here is what it teaches.

**Caption:**

Thirty nine percent of parents now track their child's location.

It feels like the safe middle ground. The research says be careful.

Constant surveillance can tell a child the world is unsafe. And it can erode the one thing that actually keeps them safe. Trust.

A tracker can quietly say I do not trust you, at the exact age they are working out if they are trustworthy.

Not never track. Track like a tool. For a first walk to school. For rebuilding trust. And always out loud, never in secret.

A child who trusts you tells you where they are.
A child who feels watched learns to disappear.

Stage two of five.

Were you trusted or watched as a kid, and which made you tell the truth?

.

## DAY 4, There is no one big talk
**Hook (image text):** Most kids have a phone by 11.

**Caption:**

More than half of children have their own phone by age eleven.

So the question is rarely whether. It is whether they are ready, and whether you are.

The best line in the whole guide was one sentence.

There is no such thing as one big talk.

The phone is not a conversation you have once. It is dozens of small ones, for years.

We brace for The Talk, hand over the device, and hope. The handover is the start of the work, not the end.

So the experts said write it down together. A family agreement. What the phone is for, where it charges at night, what happens when something goes wrong.

One thing for tonight. Charge it out of the bedroom. Over half of teens use their phones between midnight and 4am.

Stage three of five.

What small conversation do you wish you had had before the first phone?

.

## DAY 5, Not ban or surrender. Teach.
**Hook (image text):** Half of under 13s are already on social media.

**Caption:**

About half of kids twelve and under are already on social media. The wall leaks before it is built.

Plenty of parents plan to hold out to sixteen. I share the worry underneath that.

But the guide named the cost honestly.

Hold a teen back too hard and you can shut them out of the thing their school, clubs and friends run on. Looking in from outside.

The delay is not free. It buys time, and it costs belonging.

So the advice is not ban or surrender. It is teach.

Sit with them. Show them how the feed is built. Curate it together. Talk about how what they see is the highlight reel, not the truth.

A child who can see the algorithm can step out of it.

Stage four of five.

Kept off the feed, or the one who can actually see how it works?

.

## DAY 6, Let go before they go
**Hook (image text):** The strictest screen rules left kids coping worst.

**Caption:**

Here is the finding that quietly settles the ban debate.

Researchers followed kids into young adulthood. The ones with the tightest screen rules their whole lives were the ones who could not regulate themselves once they were on their own.

The wall worked right up until the day it came down.

The fix is four words. Let go before they go.

Loosen the rules while they are still home, where a mistake is a lesson you are there for, not a crisis down the phone.

A ban does not build judgement. It postpones the day it is needed, then hands it to an eighteen year old with no practice.

You are not aiming for a controlled child. You are aiming for a capable adult.

Stage five of five.

What did you have to learn the hard way after leaving home?
