**Hook stat option:** "70% of under 4s get screen time because a parent needs a break."

**Post text:**

Seventy percent of parents of children under four told the New York Times they allow screen time because they need a break. I am not going to wag a finger at that number. I have been that parent, and so has every honest one.

Here is what surprised me in their reporting. The experts did not answer it with a time limit. They answered it with two words. Connection, and quality.

The line that stuck with me is from Katie Davis at the University of Washington. When you hand a young child a screen to calm them down, she said, they learn to associate the screen with calming down, instead of learning to calm themselves. The cost is not the minutes. That reframes the whole thing. The goal at this age is not less screen. It is a child who can settle themselves, and media that invites them to join in rather than just absorb.

And be gentle with yourself, because their own researchers said it out loud. Being a parent today is hard, and sometimes you genuinely need the break. This is a habit built over time, not a verdict on one rough afternoon.

This is exactly why our pathway does not start with a screen time counter. The first stage, from age four, is the values underneath, earning time through real jobs so a screen is a choice and not a reflex, and modelling the habit yourself. We built the thing the researchers described, a child who can direct their own attention, because that is the skill that carries all the way to sixteen.

## POST 3 (Wed), 39% of parents track their child's location. The experts had a warning.

**Hook stat option:** "39% of parents track their kids. Here is what it teaches them."

**Post text:**

Thirty nine percent of parents now track their child's location, and the number climbs as kids get older. The New York Times looked hard at whether that is the safe middle ground it feels like. Their answer is worth sitting with.

Mimi Ito at UC Irvine named the risk plainly. Constant surveillance sends a child the message that the world is unsafe, and it can erode the very trust between parent and child that actually keeps a child safe. A tracker can quietly say, I do not trust you, at exactly the age a child is working out whether they are trustworthy.

The article does not say never track. It says track like a tool, not a default. Use it for a transition, the first walk to school, a teenager rebuilding trust after a mistake, and talk to the child about it rather than doing it in secret. Tracking as a stepping stone to more independence, not a substitute for it.

This is the whole argument in miniature. A control you can buy feels like safety, but the thing that actually protects a child is the relationship. A child who trusts you will tell you where they are. A child who feels watched learns to disappear.

It is why we build trust, not surveillance. Our pathway hands independence in small steps a child earns, and gives a parent the words to grant it out loud, so a child hears I trust you far more often than I am watching you. Connection is the protection. That is not a slogan, it is forty years of attachment research, and now a page in the New York Times.

## POST 4 (Thu), More than half of kids have a smartphone by age 11. The best advice was one sentence.

**Hook stat option:** "Most kids have a phone by 11. There is no such thing as one big talk."

**Post text:**

More than half of children have their own smartphone by the age of eleven, according to Pew. So the question is almost never whether. It is whether they are ready, and whether you are.

The New York Times gathered the expert advice for that moment, and the single most useful line came from Linda Charmaraman at Wellesley. There is no such thing as one big talk. The phone is not a conversation you have once. It is dozens of small ones, at every new dilemma, for years.

That is the part parents are not told. We brace for The Talk, hand over the device, and hope. The research says the handover is the start of the work, not the end of it.

Their most repeated suggestion, across expert after expert, was to write it down together. A contract. A plan the child helps make, that says what the phone is for, what the rules are, where it charges at night, and what happens when something goes wrong. Not a punishment sheet. A shared agreement, so the child owns it too.

We built exactly that, because we kept hearing parents brace for one big talk and get blindsided by the fortieth small one. Our pathway is that drip of small conversations, staged before each new thing arrives, and the family agreement is written together inside it. And when the hard moment lands at nine at night, there is a script with the actual words, so you are not improvising the most important talk of the week.

## POST 5 (Fri), Half of under 13s are already on social media. Banning it to 16 has a hidden cost.

**Hook stat option:** "50% of under 13s are already on social media. The wall leaks."

**Post text:**

About half of children aged twelve and under have already found their way onto social media, according to Common Sense Media, even though thirteen is the minimum age. So the wall is leaking before we have finished building it.

More than half of parents in the NYT survey said they plan to hold out until sixteen, many of them inspired by Jonathan Haidt. I understand the instinct completely, and I share the worry underneath it. But the article named the cost of the wall honestly, and parents deserve to hear it.

Prohibiting social media until sixteen can mean a teen is shut out of the thing their school, their clubs and their friends actually run on. One expert put it starkly. A child held back too hard never gets to join anything, and ends up looking in from the outside. The delay is not free. It buys time, and it costs belonging.

So the guide's advice is not ban or surrender. It is teach. Sit with your teen and show them how the feed is built, how it shifts with who they follow and what they click, so they can see the machine instead of being run by it. Curate it together. Talk about how what they see is the edited highlight reel, not the true story, and that AI has made that gap wider still.

That is the stage we built for shaper age. Not a gate we know leaks, but a child who understands the algorithm well enough to feel the pull and step back. Our guide never hands a child a plain yes or no, it hands them the how and the why. And our balance check does not count hours, it reads the pattern, because what hurts a teen is rarely the minutes, it is what they meet and whether they can tell you about it.

## POST 6 (Sat), The kids with the strictest screen rules struggled most when they left home.

**Hook stat option:** "The kids with the tightest screen rules coped worst on their own."

**Post text:**

Here is the finding that should quietly settle the ban debate, and it is quiet, not loud.

Researchers following children into young adulthood found that the kids who had the tightest screen time restrictions all their lives were the ones who could not regulate themselves once they were on their own. The wall worked right up until the day it came down, and then it did not.

Linda Charmaraman at Wellesley put the fix in four words. Let go before they go. Loosen the rules while the child is still under your roof, where a mistake is a lesson you are there for, not a crisis you hear about down the phone.

This is the case for preparation over prohibition, made by the calendar itself. A ban does not build judgement, it postpones the day judgement is needed, and then hands it to an eighteen year old with a dorm room and no practice. Every skill you did not let them build at home, they now build alone, at the worst possible time. A 2023 survey found thirty nine percent of college students already feel lonely. That is not the year to be learning self regulation from scratch.

This is why everything I build is a pathway, not a gate. Five stages from four to sixteen, each one handing a little more independence than the last, so that by the time they leave, self regulation is a habit they own and not a rule they escaped. You are not aiming for a controlled child. You are aiming for a capable adult. The controls come off either way. The only question is whether you were still in the room when they practised.

Stage five of five, from the New York Times guide to kids and technology.
