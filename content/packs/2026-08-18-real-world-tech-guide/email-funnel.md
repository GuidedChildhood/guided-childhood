# The email funnel, the Real World Tech Guide drip

A five email nurture sequence, one per stage, that follows the lead magnet download. Each email is
valuable standing alone, each bridges softly to the matching pathway stage, and the last carries
the /starter-pack CTA hardest. Justin's voice, no dashes. Verify figures at source before sending.

## How it slots in

- Trigger: parent downloads The Real World Tech Rulebook (lead-magnet.md) and is dropped into this
  drip via the existing lead programme in lib/email.
- Cadence: the 6 day floor inside sendEmail still applies, so this runs roughly one email a week
  across five weeks, no faster. One email a week from all systems is a platform property, not a
  choice we make here.
- Aliasing and suppression: identityKey stops a parent already in another programme being double
  chased, suppression survives deletion. Nothing here overrides that.
- Every email is plain text first, warm, short, one idea, one soft ask. From Justin, not a brand.

## EMAIL 2, Tracking, trust over surveillance

**Subject A:** The tracking question nobody answers well
**Subject B:** 39% of parents track their kids. Should you?
**Preview:** A tool, not a default. One reframe from the research.

Hi,

Stage two, tracking. Thirty nine percent of parents now track their child's location, and it
climbs as kids get older. It feels like the safe middle ground. The research says be careful.

The reframe that stuck with me is from Mimi Ito at UC Irvine. Constant surveillance can send a
child the message that the world is unsafe, and it can erode the very trust that actually keeps
them safe. A tracker can quietly say I do not trust you, at exactly the age a child is working out
whether they are trustworthy.

This is not never track. It is track like a tool, not a default. Use it for a transition, the
first walk to school, a teenager rebuilding trust after a mistake, and always talk about it, never
in secret.

Here is the bigger point, and it runs through everything I build. A control you can buy feels like
safety. The thing that actually protects a child is the relationship. A child who trusts you tells
you where they are. A child who feels watched learns to disappear.

Our pathway hands independence in small steps a child earns, and gives you the words to grant it
out loud, so they hear I trust you far more often than I am watching you.

## EMAIL 3, The first phone, the family agreement

**Subject A:** Stop bracing for the one big talk
**Subject B:** The best phone advice was one sentence
**Preview:** Most kids have a phone by 11. Here is what to do first.

Hi,

Stage three, the first phone. More than half of children have their own smartphone by age eleven,
so the question is rarely whether. It is whether they are ready, and whether you are.

The single most useful line in the whole guide came from Linda Charmaraman at Wellesley. There is
no such thing as one big talk. The phone is not a conversation you have once. It is dozens of small
ones, at every new dilemma, for years.

That is the part nobody tells you. We brace for The Talk, hand over the device, and hope. The
handover is the start of the work, not the end.

So the advice the experts kept repeating was to write it down together. A family agreement. Not a
punishment sheet, a plan the child helps make, what the phone is for, where it charges at night,
and what happens when something goes wrong.

This is the part I am proudest of building. Our pathway is that drip of small conversations, staged
before each new thing arrives, and the family agreement is written together inside it. And when the
hard moment lands at nine at night, there is a script with the actual words, so you are not
improvising the most important talk of the week.

One small thing you can do tonight, charge the phone out of the bedroom. More than half of teens
use their phones between midnight and four in the morning.

Write the agreement with us, free, here: guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack

## EMAIL 4, Social media, teach the machine

**Subject A:** Half of under 13s are already on it
**Subject B:** The hidden cost of waiting until 16
**Preview:** Not ban or surrender. Teach.

Hi,

Stage four, social media. About half of children aged twelve and under have already found their
way onto it, even though thirteen is the minimum age. The wall leaks before it is finished.

Plenty of parents plan to hold out until sixteen, and I share the worry underneath that instinct.
But the guide named the cost of the wall honestly. Holding a teen back too hard can shut them out
of the thing their school, clubs and friends run on, and leave them looking in from the outside.
The delay is not free. It buys time, and it costs belonging.

So the advice is not ban or surrender. It is teach. Sit with your teen and show them how the feed
is built, how it shifts with who they follow and what they click, so they can see the machine
instead of being run by it. And agree, out loud, that what they see is the edited highlight reel,
not the true story, made wider still by AI.

That is the stage we built for teens. Not a gate we know leaks, but a child who understands the
algorithm well enough to feel the pull and step back. Our balance check does not count hours, it
reads the pattern, because what hurts a teen is rarely the minutes, it is what they meet and
whether they can tell you about it.

One honest note. A study of 9 to 13 year olds linked an hour or more a day to slightly worse
reading, vocabulary and memory two years later. It is one correlational signal, not a verdict, and
it points the same way, teach the skill.

## EMAIL 5, Setting them free, the whole plan and the ask

**Subject A:** The kids with the strictest rules coped worst
**Subject B:** The one number this whole thing rests on
**Preview:** Let go before they go, and why we built a plan not a gate.

Hi,

Last one, stage five, setting them free. Here is the finding that quietly settles the whole ban
debate.

Researchers following children into young adulthood found that the kids with the tightest lifelong
screen restrictions were the ones who could not regulate themselves once they were on their own.
The wall worked right up until the day it came down.

Linda Charmaraman put the fix in four words. Let go before they go. Loosen the rules while the
child is still home, where a mistake is a lesson you are there for, not a crisis down the phone.

That is the case for a plan over a gate, made by the calendar. A ban does not build judgement, it
postpones the day it is needed, and hands it to an eighteen year old with no practice.

And here is the number this whole series rests on. In the same survey, 62% of parents use parental
controls, and 75% rate the ones they use at three out of five or worse. Most parents are leaning on
a tool they already know is failing. Not because they are doing it wrong, but because a gate was
never going to do the job of a plan.

So we built the plan. A pathway from age four that prepares a child for each stage before they
reach it, a guide that gives you the actual words for the hard conversations, and an agreement you
write together so the trust is there before it is tested. Ten minutes a day, you and your child,
instead of the nightly fight.
