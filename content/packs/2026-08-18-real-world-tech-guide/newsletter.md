## The rulebook the New York Times went looking for, and where it landed

The New York Times just spent a whole summer on the question I have built my company around. They
interviewed ten experts. They surveyed more than seven hundred and fifty parents. They read the
research. And after all of it, the rulebook they came back with was not the one most people brace
for.

Here is their exact finding, and I could not have written it better myself. It is not based on
screen time limits or device bans. It centers on an intentional approach to introducing
technology, and close communication between adult and child.

That is the whole of what I believe, printed in the New York Times. Not a number of minutes. Not a
wall. A plan, built stage by stage, with the conversation kept open. One of their experts, Mimi Ito
at UC Irvine, named the miss in a single line. We spend too much time talking about whether a kid
has access to a device, and not enough on what we actually want them to be doing.

I want to be fair about what this is and is not. It is not proof I am right. It is a careful
magazine drawing the same map from the same evidence. But when the New York Times, ten researchers,
and a bootstrapped founder in the UK all arrive at intentional introduction over prohibition, that
is worth a parent's attention. So let me walk their five stages the way I walk them with families,
and show you what the research says at each one.

### Stage one, the early years, connection not minutes

Seventy percent of parents of children under four told the survey they allow screen time because
they need a break. I am not going to wag a finger at that number. I have been that parent, and so
has every honest one. Their own experts said it plainly, being a parent today is hard, and
sometimes you need the break.

What surprised me is that the researchers did not answer the toddler screen question with a timer.
They answered it with connection and quality. The line that stuck with me is from Katie Davis at
the University of Washington. When you hand a young child a screen to calm them down, they learn to
associate the screen with calming down, instead of learning to calm themselves. The cost is not the
minutes. It is the self regulation skill that never gets built. So the goal at this age is not less
screen, it is a child who can settle themselves, and media that invites them to join in rather than
just absorb.

### Stage two, tracking, a tool not a default

Thirty nine percent of parents now track their child's location, and the number climbs as kids get
older. It feels like the safe middle ground. The reporting says be careful. Mimi Ito named the risk
plainly, constant surveillance can send a child the message that the world is unsafe, and it can
erode the very trust that keeps a child safe. A tracker can quietly say I do not trust you, at
exactly the age a child is working out whether they are trustworthy.

This is not never track. It is track like a tool, for a transition, the first walk to school, a
teenager rebuilding trust, and always out loud, never in secret. And it is the whole argument in
miniature. A control you can buy feels like safety. The thing that actually protects a child is the
relationship. A child who trusts you tells you where they are. A child who feels watched learns to
disappear.

### Stage three, the first phone, a contract you write together

More than half of children have their own smartphone by the age of eleven. So the question is
rarely whether, it is whether they are ready and whether you are. The single most useful line in
the whole guide came from Linda Charmaraman at Wellesley. There is no such thing as one big talk.
The phone is not a conversation you have once, it is dozens of small ones, at every new dilemma,
for years.

That is the part nobody warns you about. We brace for The Talk, hand over the device, and hope. The
handover is the start of the work, not the end. So the advice the experts kept repeating was to
write it down together. A family agreement. Not a punishment sheet, a plan the child helps make,
what the phone is for, where it charges at night, and what happens when something goes wrong. And
one small thing you can do the first night, charge it out of the bedroom. More than half of teens
use their phones between midnight and four in the morning, according to a study in JAMA Pediatrics
this year.

### Stage four, social media, teach the machine

About half of children aged twelve and under have already found their way onto social media, even
though thirteen is the minimum age. The wall leaks before it is finished. More than half of parents
in the survey said they plan to hold out until sixteen, many inspired by Jonathan Haidt. I
understand the instinct completely, and I share the worry underneath it. But the guide named the
cost of the wall honestly, and parents deserve to hear it. Holding a teen back too hard can shut
them out of the thing their school, their clubs and their friends actually run on, and leave them
looking in from the outside. The delay is not free. It buys time, and it costs belonging.

So the advice is not ban or surrender. It is teach. Sit with your teen and show them how the feed is
built, how it shifts with who they follow and what they click, so they can see the machine instead
of being run by it. And agree, out loud, that what they see is the edited highlight reel, not the
true story, made wider still by AI. I will hold one finding honestly here. A study of nine to
thirteen year olds linked an hour or more a day to slightly worse reading, vocabulary and memory
two years later. It is correlational, one signal among many, and it points the same way, teach the
skill.

### Stage five, setting them free, let go before they go

Here is the finding that quietly settles the whole ban debate, and it is not loud. Researchers
following children into young adulthood found that the kids with the tightest lifelong screen
restrictions were the ones who could not regulate themselves once they were on their own. The wall
worked right up until the day it came down. Charmaraman put the fix in four words. Let go before
they go. Loosen the rules while the child is still home, where a mistake is a lesson you are there
for, not a crisis down the phone.

That is the case for a plan over a gate, made by the calendar itself. A ban does not build
judgement, it postpones the day judgement is needed, and hands it to an eighteen year old with a
dorm room and no practice. A 2023 Gallup survey found thirty nine percent of college students
already feel lonely. That is not the year to be learning self regulation from scratch.

### The one number the whole thing rests on

Now the number I keep coming back to. In the same survey, sixty two percent of parents use parental
controls, and seventy five percent rate the ones they use at three out of five or worse. Read that
twice. Most parents are paying for, and leaning on, a tool they already know is not working. Not
because they are doing it wrong. Because a gate was never going to do the job of a plan.

The article is fair about the controls, and so am I. Use them, especially early, as a jumping off
point. But they are glitchy, kids route around them, and they are not the plan. They are the
training wheels. The plan is the child.

### What I built, and where to start

So I built the plan. A pathway from age four that prepares a child for each stage before they reach
it, early screen time about connection not minutes, tracking as a tool not a default, the first
phone as a contract you write together, social media as a machine you teach a child to see, and
setting them free as something you rehearse for years rather than switch on at eighteen. A guide
that gives you the actual words for the hard conversations, and an agreement you write together so
the trust is there before it is tested. Ten minutes a day, parent and child, instead of the nightly
fight.

The New York Times went looking for the rulebook and found the shape of it. I have spent a year
building the working version, and the first stage is free.
