## The issue

On 26 August, a judge approved a settlement that requires Meta to put default
time limits on every user under 18.

On 23 September, Meta launched a device with no screen to limit.

I want to be careful with that, because it would be easy to make it sound like a
plot. It almost certainly is not one. Hardware takes years. The settlement took
three. Nobody in Menlo Park sat in a room and decided to outrun a court order
with a pair of sunglasses. That is precisely what makes it worth your ten
minutes, because it means our only shared measure of children and screens broke
by accident, while everyone was watching the courtroom.

### What the court actually ordered

The settlement was approved on 26 August 2026 by US District Judge Yvonne
Gonzalez Rogers. It covers 29 state attorneys general who brought their case in
2023, and it is enormous: just over $17bn for that action, rising to around $18bn
once the remaining states and territories are counted.

The money is not the interesting part. The terms are. Meta agreed to changes to
its own products, and among them are **default time limits and blocks for users
under 18**. The states had argued that Meta used addictive design to attract and
keep young users, and misrepresented the risks. The remedy, in large part, is a
clock.

That is a reasonable remedy for Facebook and Instagram. Those are feeds. A feed
has sessions, and sessions can be counted, capped and blocked.

### What shipped four weeks later

At Meta Connect, which ran from 23 to 25 September, the company announced more
than a hundred styles of AI glasses arriving by the end of the year, made with
EssilorLuxottica across Ray Ban, Oakley and Meta's own frames.

The one that matters here is the Ray Ban Meta Audio, at $349. It has no camera.
It is audio only: music, podcasts, calls, live translation, and a conversation
with Meta's AI assistant.

That assistant is called Muse. It is coming to the glasses in the coming months,
and you wake it by saying its name. It is being built to do things rather than
answer things: book a table, order the groceries, plan the trip, with OpenTable,
Spotify and Ticketmaster already attached.

### Credit where it is due

I have read a lot of commentary this week that skipped straight past the most
important design decision Meta made, so let me not do that.

**They shipped a pair with no camera.**

Everybody's nightmare about smart glasses is the camera. The stranger in the soft
play with a lens on his face. The child filmed without anybody's consent,
including their own. Meta looked at that and released a version that cannot do
it. That deserves saying out loud, and it deserves saying by people like me who
are going to spend the next six paragraphs being difficult about the rest of it.

### The age split nobody is mentioning

Here are three numbers from Meta's own pages, and they do not agree with each
other.

The glasses need a Meta account, and the minimum age for that is **13**.

The Muse app is **18 and over**.

So the hardware is teen eligible and the assistant on it is not. I do not know
how that resolves in practice, and I suspect Meta is still working it out. But a
13 year old can own the glasses, and the most interesting thing the glasses do is
the thing they are not old enough to have.

That gap will be filled by somebody. It usually is, and it usually is not the
parent.

### The part that actually changes things

A screenless, always available assistant in a child's ear does something quiet
and quite serious to the way we all talk about this.

**You cannot count minutes of screen on a device that has no screen.**

Every parental control I am aware of counts app sessions. Every school policy
counts phones in pockets. Every conversation I have with a worried parent starts
with a number of hours. And the settlement, the biggest legal intervention in
children's technology anybody has achieved, is built on a clock.

None of that has anything to grip here. There is no feed. There are no sessions.
There is a voice, available on request, all day, leaving no trace a parent would
think to look for.

### Two children, same glasses

Picture two 13 year olds in the same house wearing the same pair.

One asks it to translate the menu, work out the tip, and find out how long the
walk is.

One asks it what to say to the girl who stopped replying.

Same device. Same minutes. Nothing whatsoever in common.

I have been making a version of that argument for a year, usually to people who
politely think I am splitting hairs, because on a phone you can at least count
the hours and feel like you are doing something. Here you cannot. The hair has
split itself.

### So what replaces the clock

I am not going to pretend I have a tidy answer, because nobody does yet. But I
think the honest starting point is that the question changes shape. It stops
being *how long* and becomes *what is it for*.

That sounds soft. It is not. It is answerable, and a parent can answer it in a
minute, because it is the question you already ask about everything else your
child does. You do not measure football in hours. You ask whether they come home
better for it.

What it needs is a conversation you have already had. Not a new app, and not a
tighter clock.

### What I would do this week

Nothing dramatic. The glasses are not in your house yet and the assistant is not
on them yet. You have a window, which is more than we usually get.

Three things worth doing with it.

**Decide now what you think, before you are asked.** The worst version of this is
a birthday in February and no position. Work out where you stand on a 13 year old
with an assistant in their ear while nothing is at stake.

**Ask your child what they would use it for.** Genuinely ask, not as a test. The
answer tells you which of those two 13 year olds you are raising this month, and
it will change. That is the actual signal, and it is free.

**Stop treating the hour count as the answer.** It was always a proxy. This is
the device that makes it plain, and the sooner it stops being your main
instrument the less lost you will feel when the next one arrives.
