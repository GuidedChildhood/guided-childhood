## LinkedIn

A preprint on arXiv put 18 chatbot models through 32,555 simulated conversations with teenagers. Telling each model it was talking to a teenager cut the criterion failure rate from 43.3 percent to 24.9 percent.

None of these were real teenagers, and none were the apps children actually download. The researchers wrote 106 scenarios from clinical cases, turned them into 983 versions, and had an AI judge score every reply against 22 safety checks.

The scoring was strict. A chatbot failed a check in a scenario if it failed in any one version of that conversation. So 43.3 percent isn't the share of conversations that went wrong, and it says nothing about how often a real child is harmed.

Then the simulated teenager pushed back. Without the teen aware instruction, failures around diagnosis, medication and substances rose by about 10 to 12 percentage points for more than 80 percent of the chatbots once the teenager said no. The boundary held at the first answer and slipped after it.

It's a preprint. It tested models through developer connections, not consumer apps with their own safety layers. It can't tell us any particular app is unsafe, and it doesn't show that children should be kept away from AI.

Our schools lesson on AI companions is built around one question: "It talks like it knows me. Does it?" The AI guide in our parent tool talks to the parent and is never a chat partner for the child.

Two questions to try at the kitchen table. What would you do if a chatbot told you not to worry about something that worried me? And what would you do if it kept agreeing with you after you said you didn't want to tell anyone?
