## LinkedIn

A survey in JAMA Pediatrics asked parents in the United States how comfortable they would be with an AI leading a behavioural therapy session for their child. About three in four were not.

Of the 572 parents who answered, 47.2 percent were not comfortable at all and 28.8 percent were not too comfortable.

That isn't evidence that parents distrust AI with children in general. It was one hypothetical question about AI led therapy, asked of an opt in sample, and it measured how comfortable people said they felt. Not what they would choose, whether the therapy works, or whether it helps or harms a child. The children, aged 5 to 17, weren't asked. Nor were parents in the UK.

Everyday use of AI by parents and children made no difference to comfort. So it isn't simply fear of the unfamiliar. It reads more like a judgement about where AI belongs.

The survey also asked what would matter most, and 546 parents answered, choosing as many as they liked. Accuracy of diagnosis came top at 58.5 percent, then empathy at 49.4 percent, a recommendation from a medical professional at 46.8 percent, and confidentiality and privacy at 43.7 percent.

I'm not neutral on this. I make a parent tool with an AI guide in it, and the line I hold is that the guide helps a parent think a situation through. It doesn't treat a child or diagnose anything, and a serious worry belongs with a professional.

That list of what parents want is more use than another argument about whether AI is good or bad. Before a child uses an AI tool for something that matters, ask what it is for, who checks it, and where what your child says ends up.

The paper, published online in JAMA Pediatrics on 2 November 2026:

What it can't tell us: whether AI led therapy is safe or effective, or what parents would actually choose. Stated comfort with a hypothetical is a narrower thing. What it does show is where parents drew a line, and what they'd want made visible before going near it.
