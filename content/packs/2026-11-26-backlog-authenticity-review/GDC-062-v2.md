## LinkedIn

Among EU teenagers who use social media on a school day, 32 percent of 13 and 14 year olds said talking to a parent, teacher or another adult was one way they protect their mental wellbeing there. At 17 and 18 it was 18 percent.

The European Commission asked 26,297 young people aged 13 to 18 across the 27 EU countries. The survey ran from 30 March to 16 April 2026. At 15 and 16 the figure was 28 percent.

That doesn't mean teenagers stop talking to their parents as they grow. These were three different age groups surveyed at one point, not the same children followed over time. The answer also lumped parents, teachers and other adults together, and the UK wasn't included.

The older group more often chose reporting harmful content or accounts, and taking breaks from social media. They may be finding other ways to manage what they meet online.

The gap still matters. Older teenagers in the same survey more often reported seeing AI content that was hard to recognise, and unwanted sexualised content, while fewer named an adult as one of the ways they protect themselves.

That points to timing. The open door is easier to build on an ordinary calm evening than after something has gone wrong.

One of the first lessons in our app gives parents a simple line: if a screen ever gives you that uh oh feeling, you can always come to me. You will never be in trouble for telling me.

The research doesn't show that saying it changes what a teenager does years later. It's a promise a parent can make now, while it costs nothing.

The survey analysis is in the comments.

Could you say that sentence tonight, while nothing is wrong?
