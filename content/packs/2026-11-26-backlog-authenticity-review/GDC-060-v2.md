## LinkedIn

Europe's draft law would let a 13 year old have a social media account, capped at one hour a day.

Britain's plan for the same child is no account until 16.

The European Commission put forward its proposal, the EU KIDS Act, on 17 September. It works like a staircase. Under 13, no account. At 13 and 14, a service can let a parent set up a limited one, and the parent stays in control, with parental tools always on, contacts they can approve first and a daily limit of no more than an hour. From 15, a young person can open their own.

It goes after design as well as age. For under 18s, services couldn't use infinite scroll or endless autoplay without real breaks, streaks that penalise a child for missing a day, or notifications built to pull them back. AI companions couldn't simulate a human relationship in ways likely to create emotional dependency.

The UK went the other way in June. Social media banned for under 16s, expected in spring 2027 once the rules have been through Parliament.

Nobody has tested whether 13, 15 or one hour are the right numbers. The proposal gives its reasons: 13 follows a developmental approach recommended by the Commission's expert panel, and 15 is one age to replace national limits that run from 13 to 16. Those are reasons, not results. Parliament and the Council can change every line of it, and no child has lived under it yet, so I can't tell you the staircase beats the wall.

Both leave the same gap, though.

A law decides when a child gets in. It has nothing to say about what they can do once they're there. A 14 year old with an hour a day still needs to know why the feed chose that video, what to do when a group chat turns, and when something has to come back to an adult. So does a 16 year old on the first morning the ban stops applying to them.

That's what our pathway is for. It's staged by what a child can show they can do, because a birthday teaches nobody anything.

The Commission is still taking feedback from parents, teachers and children until 3 December. Details in the first comment, and if you know a head or a parent who'd want a say, pass it on.

## First comment

The Commission's own explainer of the EU KIDS Act:

https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained

Feedback is open to children, parents, guardians, teachers and educators until 3 December 2026, midnight Brussels time. One Commission page still says 26 November, but the consultation itself shows 3 December.

A UK parent has no stake in an EU proposal, so treat this as a comparison. Two governments looked at the same worry and one drew a staircase while the other drew a wall. It's a proposal and may change, and under it a service may offer the supervised account at 13 and 14 but doesn't have to. The staircase and wall framing is mine.
