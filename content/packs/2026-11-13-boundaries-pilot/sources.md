# Verified source sheet: Dr Becky Kennedy on boundaries, and the independent evidence

Pilot: carousel and Reel on relationship based parenting and digital boundaries.
Built 13 November 2026. Research only. No post copy here except section 4, which is labelled as ours.

How to read this sheet

- Every Dr Becky source below was opened on 13 November 2026 and the quoted words were checked against the page text itself (raw page download, not a summary).
- One exact quote per source, under 15 words, in quotation marks. Everything else is paraphrase.
- CONFIRMED means I opened the paper's abstract (PubMed record, or the abstract as held by OpenAlex) or the paper. NOT OPENED means I only confirmed that the paper exists (title, authors, journal, DOI) and could not read the abstract.
- No Instagram, TikTok or Facebook page was opened. Newspaper write ups were treated as pointers and are not used as sources.

Headline for whoever writes the post

1. She HAS published on screens, in her own name, on goodinside.com. Nothing needs inventing. Use source 1.
2. Nothing found on Roblox by name. Her gaming material exists only as member gated video titles, which I could not open. Do not characterise her view on gaming.
3. Her warm plus firm stance matches the best supported finding in parenting research (authoritative parenting). Her specific method (Good Inside) has no trial that I could find. Several of her stronger lines (on repair, on consequences, on screens and the brain) go beyond the evidence, and one part (her framing of consequences and time out) runs against the trial evidence.
4. On the science of screens she platforms Jonathan Haidt (three episodes) and Jean Twenge (one). Our own digi/02-scientists.md says DiGi does not build on either. Cite her for boundaries and repair, not for screen harm.

## 1. What she has actually said (verified sources, most relevant first)

### Source 1. "4 Scripts for Changing the Screen Time Rules in Your Home"

- URL: https://goodinside.com/blog/4-scripts-changing-screen-time-rules
- Platform: goodinside.com blog, bylined Dr Becky Kennedy, Clinical Psychologist
- Date: published 7 April 2026, modified 9 June 2026 (from the page's own metadata)
- Status: OPENED, full text read
- Exact quote: "I'm not going to change my mind because you're upset."
- What she says (paraphrase):
  - Four scripts: announcing a new screen rule, putting devices out of sight between sessions, answering "I'm bored", and holding steady through a protest or meltdown.
  - The announcement is short, warm and not apologetic. The parent owns the decision, says it is not a punishment, names that the child may be upset and that the parent can cope with that.
  - In the meltdown script the parent names the wish for screen time, restates the no, and says she will neither shout at the child for being upset nor change the decision.
  - Afterwards, in a calm moment, the parent goes back to it: it was hard, and they got through it together.
  - She states that scripts are openers and never the full answer.
  - She describes the pattern as the child's feelings being valid while the limit stays put.
- Claims in this piece that are NOT evidence and should not be repeated as fact:
  - That she has been "learning more about how screens affect kids' brains" (no study named).
  - That a visible device pulls on a child even when switched off. She attributes this to Michaeleen Doucleff, author of Dopamine Kids. See Parry 2023 in section 2.
  - That a tantrum shows the old pattern is breaking. See Lerman and Iwata 1995 in section 2.
- Commercial note: the piece ends in a pitch for the Good Inside app.

### Source 2. "Gentle vs Firm Parenting: Have You Overcorrected?"

- URL: https://www.goodinside.com/blog/gentle-vs-firm-parenting/
- Platform: goodinside.com blog, bylined Dr Becky Kennedy, signed "Becky"
- Date: published 17 August 2026
- Status: OPENED, full text read
- Exact quote: "A boundary is a sentence, not a question."
- What she says (paraphrase):
  - Kindness and firmness are not a choice between two things. A child gets a voice, not a vote. The pilot image: the pilot hears that you are nervous and still flies the plane.
  - She thinks many parents have overcorrected towards empathy without limits. Her example is bringing the iPad to a sibling's match to keep a bored child happy. She says plainly this is not a verdict on the iPad.
  - Her list of firm sentences includes screen ones: screens finishing at a set time, no iPad at the game, and a child being allowed to be cross while the answer stays no.
  - Use fewer words. She advises against justifying, reasoning or trying to win the child's agreement in the moment, and against a questioning "okay?" on the end.
  - Kindness while holding: end the TV, acknowledge that it is rubbish for the child, and the TV stays off. Her test is whether the limit moved. If it did not, the parent did it right.
  - Repair after it goes badly, with sample sentences, and the point that repairing does not cost a parent authority.
- Tension to know about: "no reasoning" sits awkwardly with research on limit setting that favours giving a brief reason (Koestner 1984, section 2). Note she does give a reason in the calm announcement script in source 1, so the difference is about the heated moment, not about never explaining.

### Source 3. Her definition of a boundary (two places)

3a. The Tim Ferriss Show, episode 784, transcript

- URL: https://tim.blog/2024/12/31/dr-becky-kennedy-good-inside-transcript/
- Platform: named long form interview, publisher's own transcript
- Date: 31 December 2024
- Status: OPENED, transcript read and searched
- Exact quote: "they require the other person to do nothing"
- What she says (paraphrase):
  - Her own stated definition: a boundary is something you tell someone you will do, and its success needs nothing from the other person. Telling a child to get off the sofa is a request, because it only works if the child acts.
  - Her worked example is physical follow through with a small child, then naming the child's wish while the limit holds.
  - She says a child will be upset each time a limit is set until they are used to it, and that this approach is not permissive.
  - She uses "TV time is over" as one of her examples of a parent's side of the line, with the child's feelings on the other side.
  - No discussion of tablets, games or phones found in this transcript beyond that passing example and a general remark about social media pulling attention outward.
- LICENCE WARNING: the page states the transcript may not be copied for commercial use, including on a social account that promotes a product. Do not lift wording from this page into the carousel. Use 3b or paraphrase.

### Source 4. TED talk, "The single most important parenting strategy"

- URL: https://www.ted.com/talks/becky_kennedy_the_single_most_important_parenting_strategy/transcript
- Platform: TED (TED2023). Recorded 17 April 2023, published 13 September 2023 (page metadata)
- Status: OPENED, official transcript read in full
- Exact quote: "Repair is the act of going back to a moment of disconnection."
- What she says (paraphrase):
  - Repair means going back, taking responsibility for your own behaviour and acknowledging its effect. She separates it from an apology that closes the subject down.
  - Two steps: repair with yourself first (you are not your worst moment), then with the child. Three loose elements: name what happened, take responsibility, say what you would do differently.
  - "Not repair" is an apology that blames the child for the parent's reaction.
  - Without repair, she says, a child left alone with distress falls back on self blame. She cites the psychoanalyst Ronald Fairbairn for this, which is theory, not a study.
  - No mention of screens, phones or games anywhere in the talk.
- Lines in the talk that go beyond evidence (do not quote as fact): that a brief repair can have lifelong impact, and that repair in effect changes the past. No study is cited for either.

### Source 5. "Is It True: Is Not Punishing... Permissive?"

- URL: https://www.goodinside.com/blog/is-it-true-is-not-punishing-permissive/
- Platform: goodinside.com blog, bylined Dr Becky Kennedy
- Date: published 21 March 2026
- Status: OPENED, full text read
- Exact quote: "This isn't “no limits.” It's limits, held differently."
- What she says (paraphrase):
  - Three worked scenarios, each with a punishing, a permissive and a "sturdy, same team" response: a toddler hitting, a nine year old refusing homework, and a thirteen year old who shared something private about a classmate in a group chat.
  - Group chat scenario: open with being on the child's side, get curious about what was going on, be clear that it hurt someone, and make it right together. The apology still happens and phone limits may still change, framed as done for the child and not to her.
  - She calls behaviour information about a missing skill, and argues that fear based control stops working in the teenage years.
- Where this outruns the evidence: she lists time out, removing screens and consequences under the "punishing" response and says the child ends up scared and ashamed without learning. The trial evidence does not support that as a general statement (section 2, part E).

### Source 6. "Dr. Becky partners with Amazon to share 3 tips to manage your kids' screen time this holiday season"

- URL: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/devices/dr-becky-kids-screen-time-tips-holiday-season
- Platform: Amazon corporate news site, written by Amazon staff, quoting her and carrying tips in her voice
- Date: 12 December 2023
- Status: OPENED, full text read
- Exact quote: "Technology will continue to be a part of kids' lives"
- What she says (paraphrase):
  - Set the screen limit in advance and use device settings to hold it. Before screen time starts, tell the child how long they have and what happens at the end. She puts many screen meltdowns down to a gap between what the child hoped for and what happens.
  - Expect conflict at the end and prepare for it. Stay calm, use a confident tone, empathise.
  - Sit with the child for the last few minutes and take an interest in the game or programme, to bridge from the screen back to the room. She says this limits meltdowns because the child feels less alone. No evidence is given for that.
  - In her own house, at that date, screen time ended at 6pm, set on the tablet.
- CONFLICT OF INTEREST: this is a paid brand partnership promoting Amazon Kids tablets. Handle as her stated view, not as independent advice. I would not put the Amazon link on a carousel.

## 2. The independent evidence, principle by principle

### A. A boundary is something the parent does, not a request of the child

What the research supports

- No study tests her definition. It is a clinical rule of thumb. The nearest research is on consistent follow through and on arranging the environment so the limit does not depend on the child's self control.
- Kaminski, Valle, Filene and Boyle (2008). A meta-analytic review of components associated with parent training program effectiveness. Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology 36(4), 567 to 589. DOI 10.1007/s10802-007-9201-9. Design: meta analysis of 77 evaluations, children 0 to 7. Finding: larger effects where programmes taught positive interaction, emotional communication, time out and consistency, and made parents practise with their child. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 18205039). Limits: component coding is correlational across programmes, not a head to head test.
- Hiniker, Suh, Cao and Kientz (2016). Screen Time Tantrums: How Families Manage Screen Media Experiences for Toddlers and Preschoolers. CHI 2016 proceedings, 648 to 660. DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858278. Design: interviews with 27 parents plus a diary study with a separate 28 families, children aged 1 to 5. Finding in the abstract: screens tend to go off at natural stopping points, and transitions handled by the technology went significantly better than transitions handled by the parent. CONFIRMED (abstract, via OpenAlex). Limits: small, US, self selected, self report diary, a computing conference paper, ages 1 to 5 only. The "two minute warning makes it worse" finding is NOT in the abstract; it comes from the paper body and the university press release, which this repo already holds as secondary only (research/haidt-response/05-citation-check.md).
- Sanders, Parent, Forehand and Breslend (2016). The roles of general and technology-related parenting in managing youth screen time. Journal of Family Psychology 30(5), 641 to 646. DOI 10.1037/fam0000175. Design: cross sectional, 615 parents, three age bands. Finding: general good parenting did not relate to screen time directly; it related through specific technology rules, for young and to a degree middle childhood, and not for adolescents. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 26751759). Limits: cross sectional, parent report.

Honest reading: "do something yourself, do not just ask" is consistent with the consistency and structure findings, and the Hiniker result fits the idea of letting a timer or a set end point carry the limit. None of this proves her definition, and none of it is about children over 12.

### B. Hold the boundary while validating the feeling (warmth plus structure)

What the research supports

- Pinquart (2017). Associations of parenting dimensions and styles with externalizing problems of children and adolescents: an updated meta-analysis. Developmental Psychology 53(5), 873 to 932. DOI 10.1037/dev0000295. Design: meta analysis of 1,435 studies. Finding: warmth, behavioural control, autonomy granting and an authoritative style had very small to small links with fewer behaviour problems, at the same time point and over time. Harsh and psychological control had the strongest links, in the harmful direction. Permissive parenting was also linked to more problems. Several links ran both ways (child behaviour shapes parenting too). CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 28459276). Limits: correlational, small effects, bidirectional. It supports "warm and firm beats harsh or permissive". It does not show that any one script works.
- Koestner, Ryan, Bernieri and Holt (1984). Setting limits on children's behavior: the differential effects of controlling vs. informational styles on intrinsic motivation and creativity. Journal of Personality 52(3), 233 to 248. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-6494.1984.tb00879.x. Design: experiment, 44 children in the first and second years of school, a painting task, limits set in a controlling style, an informational style, or not at all. Finding: controlling limits lowered intrinsic motivation, enjoyment and creativity; informational limits did not differ from no limits. CONFIRMED (abstract, via OpenAlex). Limits: 44 children, one lab task, 1984, the outcome is motivation and creativity, not compliance or distress. The often repeated detail that the informational wording acknowledged the child's feelings is from the paper body, which I could NOT open, so treat that detail as unverified here.
- Joussemet, Mageau, Larose, Robichaud, Dufour, Vitaro and Koestner (2025). Evaluating the Impact of the How-to Parenting Program on Child Mental Health: A Randomized Controlled Trial in Grade Schools. Family Process 64(4), e70081. DOI 10.1111/famp.70081. Design: waitlist randomised trial, 293 parents of school age children, a 7 week course teaching autonomy support, structure and affiliation (the "How to talk so kids will listen" course). Finding: a greater drop in externalising problems than the waitlist, held for at least 6 months; no difference for internalising problems; benefits described by the authors as small. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 41177539; protocol PMID 30071843, trial NCT03030352). Limits: parent rated outcomes, waitlist control, the authors name intervention diffusion as a weakness. This is the closest trial I found to "empathy plus limits" taught to ordinary parents. It is not a trial of Good Inside.
- Havighurst, Wilson, Harley, Prior and Kehoe (2010). Tuning in to Kids: improving emotion socialization practices in parents of preschool children, findings from a community trial. Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 51(12), 1342 to 1350. DOI 10.1111/j.1469-7610.2010.02303.x. Design: randomised trial against waitlist, 216 parents of children aged 4 to 5. Finding: more emotion coaching, less emotion dismissing, better child emotion knowledge, and fewer behaviour problems by parent and teacher report. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 20735794). Limits: waitlist control, preschool only, the programme's own developers ran it.
- England-Mason, Andrews, Atkinson and Gonzalez (2023). Emotion socialization parenting interventions targeting emotional competence in young children: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Clinical Psychology Review 100, 102252. DOI 10.1016/j.cpr.2023.102252. Design: meta analysis, 26 reports from 15 trials, children 18 months to 6 years. Finding: moderate gains in parents' emotion coaching (g about 0.50), child emotional competence (0.44) and child behaviour (0.34). CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 36706555). Limits: only 15 trials, young children only, the authors call for more rigorous trials.
- Gottman, Katz and Hooven (1996). Parental meta-emotion philosophy and the emotional life of families: theoretical models and preliminary data. Journal of Family Psychology 10(3), 243 to 268. DOI 10.1037/0893-3200.10.3.243. Design: theory paper with path models from one longitudinal sample followed from age 5 to 8. CONFIRMED (abstract, via OpenAlex). Limits: the title itself says preliminary; one modest sample; correlational. This is the origin of "emotion coaching", not proof of it.
- Vasquez, Patall, Fong, Corrigan and Pine (2016). Parent Autonomy Support, Academic Achievement, and Psychosocial Functioning: a Meta-analysis of Research. Educational Psychology Review 28(3), 605 to 644. DOI 10.1007/s10648-015-9329-z. NOT OPENED (publisher blocked the abstract; title, authors and journal confirmed only). Do not cite a finding from it.

Counterevidence and open questions

- Leijten, Melendez-Torres, Gardner, van Aar, Schulz and Overbeek (2018). Are Relationship Enhancement and Behavior Management "The Golden Couple" for Disruptive Child Behavior? Two Meta-analyses. Child Development 89(6), 1970 to 1982. DOI 10.1111/cdev.13051. Design: two meta analyses, 156 and 41 randomised trials, 15,768 families, children 1 to 11. Finding: adding relationship building to behaviour management was not better overall. It was better in treatment settings and worse in prevention settings. CONFIRMED (abstract, via OpenAlex). Why it matters to us: "connection first" is not shown to add benefit for ordinary families beyond clear behaviour management. This is the result a hostile researcher would raise against "connection is the protection".
- Greville-Harris, Hempel, Karl, Dieppe and Lynch (2016). The Power of Invalidating Communication. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology 35(6), 471 to 493. DOI 10.1521/jscp.2016.35.6.471. Design: lab experiment, 90 adults randomised to validating, invalidating or no feedback during stress tasks. Finding: validated people did not differ from controls; invalidated people showed more arousal and withdrew. CONFIRMED (abstract, via OpenAlex). Why it matters: the measurable effect was the harm of dismissing a feeling, not a bonus from validating it. Adults, a lab, not parents and children. I found no study showing that validating a child's feeling makes a screen ending go more smoothly.
- "No reasoning in the moment" (source 2) against "give a brief reason" (Koestner 1984, and the autonomy supportive mediation work in part D). Unresolved. Nobody has tested the two against each other at the point a tablet goes off.

### C. Repair after a rupture

What the research supports

- Tronick (1989). Emotions and emotional communication in infants. American Psychologist 44(2), 112 to 119. DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.44.2.112. Design: review of infant observation research. Finding, in the author's careful words: good development may be associated with interactions that include frequent reparation of interactive errors, and poor development with long periods of interactive failure. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 2653124). Limits: infants and moment to moment mismatches, not a parent apologising after shouting; hedged ("may be associated"); a review.
- Kemp, Lunkenheimer, Albrecht and Chen (2016). Can We Fix This? Parent-Child Repair Processes and Preschoolers' Regulatory Skills. Family Relations 65(4), 576 to 590. DOI 10.1111/fare.12213. Design: observation of 96 mothers and their 3 year olds in play and a hard puzzle task, with a four month follow up. Finding: pairs repaired about half their difficult moments, and more repair in the puzzle task predicted better emotion regulation and fewer behaviour problems later. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 28190911). Limits: 96 pairs, mothers only, four months, and "repair" here means the pair getting back to working together, not a spoken apology.
- Robichaud, Schumann, Kil, Koestner and Mageau (2025). Should parents apologize to their adolescents to mend their parent-adolescent relationship? A multi-method, multi-phase investigation. Journal of Research on Adolescence 35(2), e70024. DOI 10.1111/jora.70024. Design: three phases with 288 adolescents: perceived apologies, coded apologies, and experimentally manipulated apologies. Finding: apologies centred on the young person predicted more forgiveness and higher relationship satisfaction than defensive apologies or none. Little link, either way, with how the adolescent saw the parent's authority. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 40235069). Limits: adolescents' reports, one research group, outcomes are forgiveness and satisfaction, not mental health. This is the best single match to her "repair" versus "not repair" distinction, and it backs her point that apologising does not cost authority. The authors themselves call the topic understudied.
- Stern, King and Diamond (2023). Repairing attachment in families with depressed adolescents: a task analysis. Journal of Clinical Psychology 79(1), 201 to 209. DOI 10.1002/jclp.23399. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 35751901) but it is a pilot of three therapy sessions. Too small to cite. Listed so nobody picks it up from a search and leans on it.
- Tronick and Gianino (1986), "Interactive mismatch and repair", Zero to Three. NOT OPENED. This is the usual source for the "in sync only about a third of the time" figure. Do not use the figure.

Counterevidence and open questions

- Wang and Kenny (2014). Longitudinal links between fathers' and mothers' harsh verbal discipline and adolescents' conduct problems and depressive symptoms. Child Development 85(3), 908 to 923. DOI 10.1111/cdev.12143. Design: cross lagged longitudinal, 976 two parent families, ages 13 to 14. Finding: harsh verbal discipline at 13 predicted rises in conduct problems and depressive symptoms, the effect ran both ways, and parental warmth did not soften it. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 24001259). Why it matters: it cuts against the line that the shouting does no harm so long as there is repair. It measured warmth in general, not repair after each incident, so it does not settle the question either.
- I found no study that tests whether a parent's repair after shouting removes the effect of the shouting, and none on lifelong effects of a brief repair. Her strongest repair claims rest on clinical theory (she cites Fairbairn), not data.

### D. Screens specifically: parental mediation research

- Collier, Coyne, Rasmussen, Hawkins, Padilla-Walker, Erickson and Memmott-Elison (2016). Does parental mediation of media influence child outcomes? A meta-analysis on media time, aggression, substance use, and sexual behavior. Developmental Psychology 52(5), 798 to 812. DOI 10.1037/dev0000108. Design: three meta analyses over 57 studies. Finding: small links for restrictive mediation (r about minus .06) and for coviewing (r about .09, in the unhelpful direction overall); active mediation not significant overall, though linked to less aggression, sexual behaviour and substance use. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 26914217). Limits: tiny effects, correlational, mixed measures, mostly television era.
- Chen and Shi (2019). Reducing Harm From Media: A Meta-Analysis of Parental Mediation. Journalism and Mass Communication Quarterly 96(1), 173 to 193. DOI 10.1177/1077699018754908. Design: meta analysis, 52 studies, 74,159 participants. Finding: restrictive mediation did more to reduce time; active mediation and co use did more to reduce risks; age, medium and culture moderated. CONFIRMED (abstract, via OpenAlex). Limits: correlational.
- Lukavská, Hrabec, Lukavský, Demetrovics and Király (2022). The associations of adolescent problematic internet use with parenting: a meta-analysis. Addictive Behaviors 135, 107423. DOI 10.1016/j.addbeh.2022.107423. Design: meta analysis. Finding: problematic use had weak links with warmth (r minus .17), control (minus .10) and authoritative parenting (minus .12). Active mediation (minus .02) and restrictive mediation (.01) were not significant. In older adolescents restrictive mediation was linked to MORE problematic use. The authors advise caution with restriction for older teenagers. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 35933287). Limits: correlational, so a parent may restrict because use is already a problem.
- Valkenburg, Piotrowski, Hermanns and de Leeuw (2013). Developing and Validating the Perceived Parental Media Mediation Scale: A Self-Determination Perspective. Human Communication Research 39(4), 445 to 469. DOI 10.1111/hcre.12010. Design: two survey studies, 761 and 499 young people aged 10 to 14. Finding: the style of mediation (autonomy supportive, controlling or inconsistent) can be measured apart from how much of it there is, and the styles related as expected to family conflict and to prosocial and antisocial behaviour. CONFIRMED (abstract, via OpenAlex). Limits: a scale validation, correlational, young people's own reports. It supports "how you restrict matters", not a size of effect.
- Beyens, Keijsers and Coyne (2022). Social media, parenting, and well-being. Current Opinion in Psychology 47, 101350. DOI 10.1016/j.copsyc.2022.101350. Design: narrative review. Finding: media specific parenting may reduce anxiety and depressive symptoms, but the evidence is not yet robust and the direction of effect is not established. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 35561563).
- Van Petegem, de Ferrerre, Soenens, van Rooij and Van Looy (2019). Parents' Degree and Style of Restrictive Mediation of Young Children's Digital Gaming. Journal of Child and Family Studies 28(5), 1379 to 1391. DOI 10.1007/s10826-019-01368-x. NOT OPENED (publisher blocked; title and authors confirmed only). Probably the most relevant single paper for a gaming boundary. Do not cite a finding until someone reads it.
- Parry (2023). Does the Mere Presence of a Smartphone Impact Cognitive Performance? A Meta-Analysis of the "Brain Drain Effect". Media Psychology 27(5), 737 to 762. DOI 10.1080/15213269.2023.2286647. Design: six meta analyses, 56 effects, 7,093 participants. Finding: one small effect on working memory, nulls elsewhere, weak methods and low power across the field. CONFIRMED (abstract, via OpenAlex). Why it matters: it is the nearest test of the "a device in sight keeps pulling at you" idea in source 1, and support is limited. It is about adult thinking tasks, not children's cravings. "Out of sight" is a sensible structural step; the brain claim attached to it is not established.
- Lerman and Iwata (1995). Prevalence of the extinction burst and its attenuation during treatment. Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis 28(1), 93 to 94. DOI 10.1901/jaba.1995.28-93. Design: analysis of 113 published extinction data sets. Finding: a burst (worse before better) appeared in 24 percent of cases, and less often (12 percent) when extinction was paired with other procedures. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 16795857). Why it matters: "expect a bigger meltdown at first, it means it is working" is not a reliable rule. Clinical cases, not ordinary families at the end of screen time.

Honest reading of D: the effects of screen specific parenting are very small and mostly correlational. General warmth and an authoritative style show somewhat larger links than any screen rule. Heavy restriction looks worse, not better, for older teenagers. That lines up with our pathway and with her warm plus firm stance, but it cannot be sold as "this script stops meltdowns".

### E. Trials of parenting programmes, and whether her own method has been tested

- Good Inside itself: a PubMed search for "Good Inside" with Kennedy and parenting returned nothing, and ClinicalTrials.gov returned no study for "Good Inside" (both run 13 November 2026). I found no peer reviewed evaluation of the membership, the app or the scripts. Two empty searches are not proof that none exists. The accurate statement is: no trial found. She holds a PhD in clinical psychology (Teachers College, Columbia, per Wikipedia, which is a pointer). Her framework draws on attachment theory, Internal Family Systems and clinical practice. It is clinician informed, not trial tested.
- Leijten, Gardner, Melendez-Torres, van Aar, Hutchings, Schulz, Knerr and Overbeek (2019). Meta-Analyses: Key Parenting Program Components for Disruptive Child Behavior. Journal of the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry 58(2), 180 to 190. DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2018.07.900. Design: 154 randomised trials, 398 effect sizes. Finding: programmes work (effects from d 0.21 in universal prevention to 0.69 in treatment). Three of 26 techniques went with stronger effects: positive reinforcement, praise in particular, and natural or logical consequences. Relationship building went with stronger effects in treatment and weaker effects in prevention. No technique predicted stronger long term effects. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 30738545).
- Dadds and Tully (2019). What is it to discipline a child: what should it be? A reanalysis of time-out from the perspective of child mental health, attachment, and trauma. American Psychologist 74(7), 794 to 808. DOI 10.1037/amp0000449. Design: theoretical and evidence review. Finding: the authors argue the evidence for time out is strong and that, done properly, it is compatible with attachment. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 30802080). Limits: a position paper by researchers who develop behavioural programmes.
- Knight, Albright, Deling, Dore-Stites and Drayton (2020). Longitudinal Relationship Between Time-Out and Child Emotional and Behavioral Functioning. Journal of Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics 41(1), 31 to 37. DOI 10.1097/DBP.0000000000000725. Design: archival longitudinal data from the Early Head Start evaluation, time out use reported at 36 months, outcomes to the fifth year of school. Finding: no difference on any outcome between children whose parents used time out and those who did not. CONFIRMED (PubMed abstract, PMID 31513092). Limits: one yes or no question about time out, a low income US sample, a null result.
- Baumrind's original papers on authoritative parenting (1966, 1967, 1991). NOT OPENED. Use Pinquart 2017 as the citation.

Where her framing parts company with the trials: she groups time out, removing screens and consequences under a "punishing" response that leaves a child ashamed and unskilled (source 5), and a 2022 episode description says consequences do not build lifelong skills. The trial literature says calm, predictable consequences and praise are among the few components tied to better outcomes, and finds no harm from time out. She is right that harsh control is the most damaging pattern (Pinquart). The fair summary: her warm and firm core is well supported; her rejection of consequences is a position, not a finding. Our own digi/07-trust-framework.md already lists "natural consequences, proportionate, predictable, announced in advance" as something that works, which is closer to the trials than she is.

## 4. OUR wording, not hers

Five sentences a parent could say when holding a screen boundary. Written by us, consistent with the verified principles above (the parent acts, the feeling is named, the limit does not move, repair afterwards). Plain British English, no dashes. None of these is a quotation from Dr Becky Kennedy and none should be presented as hers.

1. "Screen time is finished for today, so I'm putting the tablet on the shelf now."
2. "You really wanted longer and you're gutted. That makes sense. The tablet is still staying off."
3. "You can be cross with me. I'm staying right here with you, and the answer is the same."
4. "It's a not yet on the phone. That one is my call, and I'll show you what needs to be in place before we look at it again."
5. "I shouted when the tablet went off earlier. That wasn't fair on you and it wasn't your fault. Next time I'll take a breath first. The screen rule is the same as before."

What each one rests on: 1 is the parent doing something instead of asking (part A, a rule of thumb with indirect support). 2 and 3 are warmth plus structure (part B, the best supported part). 4 is our calibrated pathway, with a brief reason and a next step (Koestner 1984; Valkenburg 2013). 5 is a repair that takes responsibility without blaming the child and without moving the limit (Robichaud 2025; Kemp 2016).

Claims the post can make safely: warm and firm together is the pattern the research favours; harsh and permissive both do worse; how you hold a screen limit matters as well as whether you hold one; an apology that owns your part is linked to a better relationship with a teenager. Claims it cannot make: that these words stop meltdowns, that repair undoes shouting, or that Good Inside has been proven.
