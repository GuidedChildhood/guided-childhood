## The story in one line

When the tablet goes off, it feels like a choice between being kind and being
firm. The research favours both at once: do it yourself, name the feeling,
keep the limit, and go back afterwards if it went badly.

## Do not quote

- Any sentence presented as hers. Every line a parent says in this pack is our own wording.
- "Two things are true". It is her registered phrase. Say the idea in our words.
- That these words stop or shorten a meltdown. No study has tested a script at the moment a screen goes off.
- That validating a feeling "reduces resistance dramatically". No study found. This line is in `digi/07-trust-framework.md` and should be softened there.
- That repair undoes shouting, has lifelong impact or changes the past. Wang and Kenny points the other way.
- That a bigger tantrum at first shows the rule is working. A burst appears in a minority of cases.
- That a device in sight keeps pulling at a child's brain. The nearest evidence is weak and is about adults.
- That consequences or time out harm children. The trials do not show that.
- "Parents only need to get it right a third of the time". Source not opened.
- That any programme, hers or ours, is proven. No trial of her method was found. Ours has none either.
- Any view on Roblox, gaming or the right age for a phone attributed to a clinician.

## 1. LinkedIn post

When the tablet goes off, most of us think we have two options.

Give in and keep the peace. Or hold the line and have the row.

I went looking for what the research says about that choice. The biggest review I could find pooled 1,435 studies of parenting and children's behaviour.

It did not pick a side.

Warm and firm together went with fewer behaviour problems. Harsh control had the strongest link with more. And permissive parenting was linked with more as well.

So it was never kind or firm. It is both, in the same sentence.

Two honest limits. The links are small, and they run both ways, because a hard evening changes the parent too. And nobody has tested a script at the exact moment a tablet goes off. Anyone who says their words stop a meltdown is ahead of the evidence.

What the research does support is a shape. Here is how it can sound.

You do it. You do not ask. "Screen time is finished for today, so I'm putting the tablet on the shelf now."

You name the feeling and the limit stays. "You really wanted longer and you're gutted. That makes sense. The tablet is still staying off."

And if you shouted, you go back. "I shouted earlier. That wasn't fair on you and it wasn't your fault. The screen rule is the same as before."

Parents often worry that an apology costs them authority. One study of 288 teenagers found that an apology which owned the mistake went with more forgiveness, and made little difference to how they saw the parent's authority.

The sources are in the comments.

Which is harder in your house: holding the limit, or going back afterwards?

## 4. Parent resource: four sentences for when the screen goes off

Our own wording. None of these is a quotation from anyone.

1. Do it yourself. "Screen time is finished for today, so I'm putting the tablet on the shelf now."
2. Name the feeling and keep the limit. "You really wanted longer and you're gutted. That makes sense. The tablet is still staying off."
3. Stay with them. "You can be cross with me. I'm staying right here with you, and the answer is the same."
4. Go back if it went badly. "I shouted when the tablet went off earlier. That wasn't fair on you and it wasn't your fault. Next time I'll take a breath first. The screen rule is the same as before."

For a not yet on a phone: "It's a not yet on the phone. That one is my call,
and I'll show you what needs to be in place before we look at it again."

What this does not claim: that the words prevent upset, or that an apology
cancels a shout. It gives you a way to be kind and firm in the same breath.
