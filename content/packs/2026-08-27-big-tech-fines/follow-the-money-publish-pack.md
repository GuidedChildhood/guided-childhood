## PART 1 · Monday · The zero dollar fines

### The post

113 years.

That is how long governments have been taking technology companies to court,
starting with AT&T in 1913.

Here is the strange part. The actions that actually changed the industry
carried no fine at all.

The Microsoft case, the biggest tech trial of the modern era, ended in 2001
with a penalty of exactly zero dollars. A conduct decree, a technical
committee, and a decade of caution that many credit with leaving the door open
for a young company called Google. I should be straight about that last part:
it is a widely held reading, not a provable one.

Now the other side of the ledger. In 2019 Facebook paid the largest privacy
penalty in history, 5 billion dollars. Within four years the FTC formally
alleged the company had violated the very order it paid for, on a product
called Messenger Kids, where coding errors let children join group chats with
adults their parents had never approved.

The record fine did not change the behaviour. The zero dollar decree changed an
industry.

That is not an argument that enforcement is dead. Europe's data rules cut EU
firms' data storage by 26 percent compared with American firms, mostly through
the threat, before the big fines were even collected. Deterrence is real. It
just does not live where the headlines point.

It lives in binding rules, audits and structure. Not in cheques.

This is The Wrong Villain: Follow the Money, Part 1 of 6. All week I am
tracing every major case from 1913 to last week's Meta settlement, asking one
question: where did the money actually go?

What is the biggest fine you can remember actually changing how a company
behaved? I have been looking all week and the list is short.

reader. They will also say fines obviously deter; the GDPR 26 percent line is
the concession that keeps that reader too.

## PART 2 · Tuesday · Headline versus final

### The post

€515,547,908.15.

That is not a fine the EU collected from a tech giant. It is the interest the
EU paid TO Intel in November 2024, after a 1.06 billion euro fine from 2009
was annulled by the court thirteen years later.

The final fine, after sixteen years of litigation, came to 237 million. A
fifth of the headline.

It is not a one off. Google's 1.49 billion euro AdSense fine from 2019 was
annulled in full in September 2024. Legally, it never existed. The famous 13
billion from Apple was never a fine at all, it was recovered tax paid to
Ireland, and Ireland spent eight years in court trying NOT to receive it.

So when you read that big tech has now paid some enormous cumulative total,
check the arithmetic. Most running totals sum the press release numbers, not
the figures that survived appeal. Even last week's Meta settlement is 12.7
billion dollars guaranteed, not the 17 billion in the headlines. Roughly 5.3
billion of it depends on what TikTok and YouTube decide to do next.

The honest pivot: annulled fines are not proof that enforcement is a sham.
They are proof that courts check regulators, which is exactly what courts are
for. Google Shopping's 2.4 billion was upheld by Europe's highest court after
seven years. The system corrects in both directions.

But it leaves one discipline for anyone who writes about this, me included:
only final after appeal figures count. Everything else is theatre, on both
sides.

This is The Wrong Villain: Follow the Money, Part 2 of 6.

Have you ever seen a corrected fine get a tenth of the coverage the original
announcement got? Genuine question. I could not find one.

## PART 3 · Wednesday · Tobacco (flagship carousel)

### The post

3.5 percent.

In 1998, US states won 206 billion dollars from the tobacco industry. The
promise was health, and especially children's health.

This year, states are collecting 22.1 billion dollars from tobacco settlement
money and tobacco taxes, and spending 764.8 million of it on prevention.

That is 3.5 percent. It is the 27th consecutive year the promise has been
broken.

It gets stranger. States turned roughly 36 billion dollars of future payments
into upfront bonds to plug budget holes. Washington State took 450 million
upfront; its tobacco money went on to pay investors 848 million. North
Carolina spent settlement money on tobacco curing barns and a processing
plant.

And because the payments scale with cigarette sales, the states became
business partners of the company they had sued. In 2003, dozens of attorneys
general, 37 by the New England Journal of Medicine's count, filed in court in
SUPPORT of Philip Morris, to protect a payment stream their budgets had come
to depend on.

Why dig this up now? Because last week's 12.7 billion dollar Meta settlement
runs on the tobacco playbook, down to some of the same law firms leading the
parallel family lawsuits.

The honest pivot: the promise can be written into the contract. The opioid
settlements proved it, with a binding 85 percent earmark for treatment written
into the terms. Tobacco's failure was a drafting choice, not a law of nature.

Which makes the next question the only one that matters: which kind of
contract did 47 states just sign with Meta?

That is Part 4, tomorrow.

This is The Wrong Villain: Follow the Money, Part 3 of 6.

If your council or state were handed its share tomorrow, what would you want
it spent on first? I am collecting answers, seriously.

## PART 4 · Thursday · The general fund machine

### The post

Zero dollars.

That is how much of every child privacy fine in history, before this month,
was legally required to reach a child facing service.

The 170 million dollar YouTube fine for tracking children in 2019: the US
Treasury's general fund and New York's coffers. Epic's 275 million, the
biggest children's privacy penalty ever: the Treasury. TikTok's 400 million
dollar settlement with the DOJ this month: the US government. Every EU fine:
the EU budget, where it quietly reduces what member states pay in. Germany's
contribution reportedly fell by about a billion euros when Google's Android
fine finally landed this summer.

Not one dollar, anywhere, was legally required to be spent on the harm it
punished.

The opioid settlements were meant to be the fix, and on paper they are: 85
percent earmarked for treatment, written into the contract. So what happened?
The failure changed shape instead of dying. Kentucky's local councils had
spent less than a tenth of their money by the end of the last fiscal year,
109.8 million dollars sitting idle. Louisiana carved 20 percent to sheriffs.
And only about 15 percent of the spending has to be publicly reported at all.

For a family in a queue, money sitting in a committee account is
indistinguishable from no money.

The honest pivot: the Meta settlement genuinely does carry earmark language
for youth mental health, a first for big tech. Utah's governor even said the
quiet part out loud: I don't want this to just go into the general fund. But
in most states the legislature holds the pen, and that is the tobacco
pattern, not the opioid one.

So here is a prediction you can hold me to: watch which states pass a law
dedicating their Meta money by the end of the 2027 sessions. That list will
tell you everything about whether this time was different.

This is The Wrong Villain: Follow the Money, Part 4 of 6.

Does your state or country dedicate settlement money by law, or by press
release? If you know a real example either way, I want to hear it.

## PART 5 · Friday · Six weeks (the thesis post)

### The post

Six weeks.

The biggest child safety settlement in history buys about six weeks of the one
policy proven to halve child poverty.

That sentence took me a while to believe, so here is the arithmetic. It is
mine, on sourced inputs, and I will show my working.

Meta's guaranteed payment is 12.7 billion dollars over ten years. Call it 1.27
billion a year.

In 2021 the United States expanded its Child Tax Credit. Child poverty fell
from 9.7 percent to 5.2 percent in a single year, the lowest ever recorded.
When the credit expired, the rate rebounded to 12.4 percent and more than five
million children fell back below the line. The expansion's cost ran to roughly
100 billion dollars a year.

1.27 billion against 100 billion is about six weeks.

The same settlement pot would fund roughly 15,000 American school counsellors
a year. Or come close to doubling England's entire children's mental health
budget, around 1.1 billion pounds against more than a million active
referrals, for a decade.

The honest pivot, because this claim has edges: the credit's poverty effect is
proven, but the direct mental health evidence on cash transfers is mixed. One
study found no short run change in parents' wellbeing, another found reduced
anxiety in low income families. So this is a claim about scale. It is never a
claim about a cure.

But the scale is the story. We have spent five years arguing about the villain
that attracts the cameras, while the drivers with the strongest evidence,
poverty, family adversity, a million child queue for help, get the leftovers
of the leftovers.

A fine cannot fix that. Only a budget can. And budgets follow attention, which
is why what we all choose to talk about is not a small thing.

This is The Wrong Villain: Follow the Money, Part 5 of 6, and it is the whole
series in one ratio.

What would you fund first with 1.27 billion a year for children? Real answers
wanted, especially from teachers and clinicians.

## PART 6 · Monday 7 September · The close (flagship carousel)

### The post

1.8 percent.

That is how many teenagers used the safety feature Instagram offered
voluntarily. Confirmed under oath, the day before Meta settled.

Now the other number. The most effective child safety intervention of the
digital era issued no fine at all. The UK's Age Appropriate Design Code came
into force in 2020, no company has ever been fined under the Code itself, and
it flipped defaults on every major platform worldwide: TikTok accounts private
by default for 13 to 15 year olds, YouTube autoplay off for under 18s,
Instagram accounts private for under 16s. A British design code quietly
changed what children see in Texas and Tokyo.

Independent testing last year found 64 percent of Instagram's teen safety
tools ineffective or discontinued.

Across 113 years and six parts, the pattern is now visible: defaults reach
every child, options reach almost none.

And the circumvention argument cuts the opposite way to how it is usually
used. Yes, 22 percent of UK 8 to 17 year olds hold accounts with an adult age.
A child can lie past an age gate. But no child can VPN past autoplay being
off, hidden like counts, or a filter that does not exist. Evasion defeats
gates. It does not defeat design.

The honest pivot: the settlement's new limits are parent removable defaults,
and no court in the states' case ever ruled on what caused what, because Meta
settled. The only adjudicated outcome anywhere is New Mexico's, whose 567
million dollar treatment fund Meta is appealing.

So here is where six parts land. Fines feed treasuries. Design changes reach
children. And the strongest layer is the one no regulator can order and no
teenager can uninstall: a plan the family builds together, where the child
helped write the rules, so there is nothing to switch off and nothing to
climb over.

That is what I spend my days building. The whole series is on my profile, and
