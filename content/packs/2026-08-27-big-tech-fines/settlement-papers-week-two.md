## PART 1 · Tuesday · The excluded inbox

### The post

13 per cent.

That is how many 13 to 15 year olds on Instagram received an unwanted sexual advance
within a single week, in Meta's own internal survey of roughly 237,000 users. The
figure reached the Senate under oath, from the engineer who built the survey.

Last week's settlement gives teenagers a two hour daily limit, a midnight curfew and
muted notifications in school hours.

All three exclude direct messages.

Read that again. The one place Meta's own data says the sharpest harm concentrates is
the one place the new rules do not reach.

The pattern is wider than one app. Reports of financial sextortion to America's
national center passed 50,000 last year, 137 a day, up 37 per cent in a year, and
that organisation knows of roughly three dozen teenage boys who have died by suicide
after being targeted since 2021. In UK police grooming records, where the platform is
known, 40 per cent of offences happen on Snapchat and 9 per cent on WhatsApp. Neither
app is in the settlement. WhatsApp is owned by Meta.

The honest pivot: the deal does include protections against unwanted contact, alerts
to parents, restrictions on suspicious adults. Real things. But they are notification
features, not the structural limits applied to the feed. The feed got walls. The inbox
got signage.

A curfew on scrolling, silence on the place the predators work. That is not an
oversight. It is a negotiation, and next Wednesday I will show you the state that
refused to make it.

This is The Wrong Villain: The Settlement Papers, Part 1 of 4.

Parents, honest question: does your child's app time worry you more than your child's
inbox? A week ago I would have answered differently myself.

## PART 2 · Wednesday · The state that said no

### The post

$942,000,000.

Forty seven states settled with Meta last week. One state refused, took Meta to a
jury, and that number is what refusing turned out to be worth.

New Mexico. A jury found 75,000 wilful violations of its consumer protection law,
$375 million, the statutory maximum. The court then added $567 million for a public
nuisance abatement fund. Meta is appealing, and that word belongs in every honest
telling of this story.

Here is the part the headlines missed. Look at what New Mexico's court ordered that
the 47 state settlement does not contain: a ban on romantic and sexualised AI chatbot
interactions with minors, and hardened safeguards against adults reaching children in
private messages.

The two protections the national deal left out are the two the verdict state won.

New Mexico's attorney general said it plainly on the record: the national settlement
is weaker than what his state won at trial. Florida's attorney general refused too,
calling the payouts peanuts compared to the profound harms, and added: we will see
them at trial.

The honest pivot: a jury verdict under appeal is not settled law, and New Mexico's
case was about exploitation and consumer protection, not about proving social media
causes depression. Nobody should read it as the science question answered.

But as a negotiation lesson it is unanswerable. When one party settles for less than
a courtroom was already awarding, the missing terms were not impossible. They were
traded.

injunctive terms: the chatbot ban and the DM safeguards exist in New Mexico and
nowhere in the national deal. That is the trade worth talking about.

## PART 3 · Thursday · Nobody has to find out

### The post

120 days.

Buried in the Meta consent judgment is the moment the outside world stops watching.
The independent auditor reports annually for five years, and its role ends 120 days
after the fifth report. The agreement's main terms run ten years. The back half has
no outside monitor at all.

And here is what the auditor checks while it exists: whether Meta shipped the
features. Not whether they worked. No binding term in the deal requires anyone to
measure whether a single child slept better, felt better, or was safer.

The two strongest protections, the time limit and the midnight curfew, are five year
commitments that only extend to ten if TikTok and YouTube sign up to the same
framework. Roughly $5.3 billion of the money is also released only if they join. So
the toughest version of the deal, and the last third of the cheque, both depend on
decisions made in companies Meta competes with.

The honest pivot: audits are not decorative. The assessor attached to Meta's 2019
privacy order surfaced the gaps that produced the regulator's next enforcement move.
Implementation checking has caught things before.

But think about what is missing. This deal will generate five annual reports on
whether features exist, and zero reports on whether children improved. The research
foundation that could measure outcomes appears in Meta's announcement with no
published governance. The settlement is the largest child safety experiment ever run,
and nobody is contracted to record the result.

So I will put predictions on the record instead, and you can hold me to them. By
2029: overall teen mental health numbers will not visibly bend, because the real
effects concentrate in vulnerable groups rather than populations. Teen sleep may
improve modestly, because sleep is the best evidenced mechanism the deal touches.
And watch whether TikTok and YouTube sign before the five year sunset. That single
fact will decide what this deal turns out to have been.

This is The Wrong Villain: The Settlement Papers, Part 3 of 4.

## PART 4 · Friday · Nobody asked them

### The post

0.

That is how many teenagers were quoted in the settlement coverage I could find in
the days after the biggest child safety deal in history. Wall to wall analysis from
attorneys general, analysts, advocates and parents. Not one person the rules are
actually about.

So here is what young people say when someone does ask.

Pew asked 1,391 American teenagers last year: 48 per cent said social media is
mostly negative for people their age. Asked about themselves, 14 per cent. Almost
half see the problem. One in seven thinks it is their problem.

And they are already acting: 45 per cent say they spend too much time on it, and
44 per cent have cut back on their own, no court order required. In the UK,
Girlguiding found 72 per cent of 10 to 16 year olds want the companies to do more,
while 56 per cent think a ban would not work.

They want the space made safer. They do not believe walls work. On the evidence from
Australia, where more than 85 per cent of under 16s were still on restricted
platforms three months into the ban, they are right about the walls.

The honest pivot: two days of silence may just be lag, and teenagers are not a
committee with one view. The ambivalence is the finding: the same UK girls who say
social media leaves them low, 36 per cent, also say it keeps their friendships
alive, 62 per cent.

But a rule written about people who were never asked, which they do not believe
applies to them, enforced by a parent's tap they can negotiate at the kitchen table,
has one variable left doing all the work: whether the young person is ready for the
space, because the restriction will not survive contact with them.

Readiness is buildable. That is the work, and it is what I build.

This is The Wrong Villain: The Settlement Papers, Part 4 of 4. The whole arc, both
weeks, is on my profile.

If you could put one question about these new rules to 40,000 school pupils, what
would you ask them? I may be able to do exactly that soon, so choose well.

## RESERVE · the following Monday · The mirror

### The post

73 per cent.

That is how many parents feel guilty about their child's screen time. Now the finding
that stopped me: in the peer reviewed study that measured it, the guilt did not track
the child's actual screen time at all. It predicted the parent's stress. Not the
child's exposure.

Which helps explain something strange about the anger at Meta.

Ask parents which platform should never have been invented and they point at Meta.
Ask Gen Z and they point at X, 50 per cent, and TikTok, 47 per cent, with Facebook
back on 37. Each generation nominates the platform it is most entangled with. The
villain is a mirror.

Psychology has a name for the mechanism. When a harm makes us feel guilty and out of
control, we reach for a single vivid target, because punishing it restores the
feeling of control. The research is clear that both guilt and lost control
independently increase the reach for a villain.

The honest pivot, twice over. Meta's record is genuinely bad, documented in its own
research and its own inboxes, and none of this excuses it. And parental guilt is not
foolish, it is love with nowhere to go.

But here is what the mirror means practically. The settlement punished the villain,
and the guilt research predicts the relief will not last, because nothing at the
kitchen table changed. The thing that actually dissolves the guilt is not a verdict
somewhere in California. It is a plan at home, one the child helped write, where the
parent can finally answer the question what are we doing about it with something
other than a rule that gets fought about.

That is why I build what I build.

This is The Wrong Villain, continued.

Parents, when the settlement news broke, what did you feel first, relief, or the
quiet suspicion that nothing at home had changed? I felt both, and the second one is
why this account exists.
