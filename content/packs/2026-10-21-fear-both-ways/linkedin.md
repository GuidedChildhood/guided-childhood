# GDC-045 · Fear is the engine on both sides now

Justin, 21 October 2026: write a post from the Psychology Today piece, with an
enemy, the Jonathan Haidt fear factor.

## The angle, and why it is ours rather than a restatement

The obvious post is "Haidt overstates it", and we have written it. It did 2,901
impressions at 3.31 percent. A fifth one adds nothing.

The piece Justin sent argues against bans, and in making that case it cites the
BMJ paper as proof that 85 percent of Australian teenagers circumvented the ban.
**We checked that exact paper yesterday for GDC-044 and that is not what it
says.** Continued use on an account a child already held is not circumvention,
and the paper does not test whether the ban failed. Our own correction is
already in GDC-044's first comment: "that is continued use, not breaking in".

So the post writes itself, and only we can write it: **the people correcting
Haidt have started using his move.** The enemy is not a man, it is fear used as
a policy engine, and Haidt is the named example because he is the loudest.

That keeps the enemy Justin asked for, stays generous enough to survive a
hostile reader, and says something neither camp is saying.

## The one number nobody is quoting

Both sides skip it. eSafety found account ownership fell from 52.4 percent to
42.1 percent. That is the only measurable thing the ban has done so far, it is
real, and it is inconvenient for the ban's critics and too small for its
champions. Putting it in the post is what makes the post honest rather than
clever.

## The post

*Body 1,970 characters, inside the band. Hook 110 characters, inside the mobile
fold. Thirteen paragraphs. One closing question, nothing after it. Link in the
first comment, never the body. No founder scene: the 365 day numbers put every
scene led opening in the bottom third, and this is an evidence post.*

A study everyone is quoting this week says 85 percent of Australian teenagers got round the under sixteen ban.

It does not say that.

The paper is in the BMJ, from the University of Newcastle. It recruited 436 adolescents and followed up with 408 of them three months after the ban began. What it found is that more than 85 percent of under sixteens were still using the platforms, mostly on accounts they already had.

Still using is not the same as broke in.

And the regulator's own follow up, 803 children, found the thing nobody is quoting. The share who actually held an account fell from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent. eSafety calls that modest and statistically significant. It is the one measurable thing the ban has done so far and it is missing from both arguments.

None of this means the ban works. The same paper says there is not enough evidence of a real drop in use, its own authors say the full picture may take a decade, and it is one Australian state with self reported answers.

Jonathan Haidt made the loudest case of the decade out of alarm. A generation rewired. An epidemic with a start date. A conclusion that keeps arriving ahead of the effect sizes that would carry it.

The people who spend their days correcting him have started doing the same thing. A Psychology Today piece this month cited that BMJ paper as proof the ban failed. The paper does not say the ban failed. It says we cannot tell yet.

So fear is the engine on both sides now. One side is frightened of the phones. The other is frightened of the ban. Both reach past what the study will carry, because a careful answer does not move anybody.

Britain has this argument next and the law lands in 2027.

Which leaves a parent somewhere else entirely. The ban will do whatever it does. What happens in your house between now and the day it lands is the part nobody is legislating.

### First comment

Sources, and where I have been careful. The study is Barnes et al., BMJ 2026;393:e363695, University of Newcastle, published 24 June 2026. It recruited 436 adolescents aged 12 to under 17 and followed up with 408 of them three months in. It reports that more than 85 percent of under sixteens were still using platforms covered by the Act, mostly through accounts they already held, and finds insufficient evidence of a substantive early reduction. Its own authors say the full impacts may not be evident for a decade. It is one Australian state, the answers are self reported, and the confidence intervals are wide enough that modest effects cannot be ruled out, which is exactly why it cannot carry the weight either camp puts on it. The regulator's figures are the eSafety Commissioner's three month follow up, published July 2026, which surveyed 803 children aged 10 to 15: 85.9 percent using at least one age restricted platform before the ban and 81.5 percent after, measured with or without an account, and account ownership down from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent, which eSafety calls modest but statistically significant. eSafety also says plainly that the report is not an assessment of whether platforms complied. The Psychology Today piece is Emma Bruehlman-Senecal, Why Banning Social Media Won't Fix Teen Mental Health, 15 October 2026, and it is worth reading: it is right that age checks create their own privacy problem, and right that for some children these platforms are where they find people like them. My disagreement is with one line, where the BMJ paper is cited as teenagers circumventing the ban. Continued use on an existing account is not circumvention, and the paper does not test whether the ban failed. On Jonathan Haidt, the thing he is right about is that parents are not imagining the problem. Where I part company is the size of the effect and the confidence of the conclusion. UK timing: the government has said enforcement from 2027, and a firmer date has been reported but I have not verified it, so I have said 2027.

## What was deliberately not said

- **The advocacy figures in the piece were left out of the body.** The Instagram
  survey, the Hopelab numbers and the Children's Commissioner figure on weight
  loss ads are all interesting and none has been through `citation-verifier`.
  The body runs only on the two sources we checked against primary sources

- **The piece's argument about Black, Brown and LGBTQ+ young people is real and
  is not in this post.** It deserves its own post rather than a line borrowed to
  decorate this one, and it is a claim about children we would want verified
  first.
- **No claim that the ban is wrong.** That would contradict GDC-044, which says
  a protected period is worth having. The post's position is that neither camp
  can tell yet, which is what the evidence supports.
- **Nothing about the author's motives.** The disagreement is with one citation.

## What would make this post wrong

If the BMJ authors meant circumvention when they wrote continued use, the whole
pivot collapses. The paper's own wording, and the eSafety split between use and
account ownership, are what hold it up. If a reader produces a line from the
paper that frames continued use as circumvention, concede it fast and say so.
