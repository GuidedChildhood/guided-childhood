**Instagram caption**

Send this to the parent who is worried about AI companion apps.

Fair point first: companion apps deserve attention, and worrying about them is not a panic.

Here is the part the headlines miss. Australia's online safety regulator surveyed almost 2,000 Australian children aged 10 to 17 about AI in early 2026. 78 percent had used an AI assistant, the ordinary kind that many parents think of as homework help. 8 percent had used an AI companion.

Among the children who had used either, 54 percent said they had used it for personal or social reasons. That is a wide bucket. It could be a chat about a game as easily as a worry, and it is one survey.

These are Australian children, not British ones, so do not carry the percentages across. What does carry across is the question: when your child is stuck or worried, who do they ask first?

Tonight: ask it as plain curiosity. "When you are stuck, who do you ask? Do you ever ask an AI?" Listen first. Do not react.

**Instagram caption**

Send this to the parent who is awake at eleven worrying about something their child saw.

It is eleven at night and something has happened online. The options are a Facebook group, a panicked search, or waking your partner.

We built an AI for parents and then forbade it from giving an answer.

That is DiGi. It will not say yes or no, because a verdict ends the conversation. Instead:

1. You tell DiGi what happened, in your own words.
2. It knows your child's stage, so the answer for a nine year old is not the answer for a fourteen year old.
3. It will not say allow or ban.
4. It gives you the conditions: what structure, at what time, agreed with whom, reviewed together when.
5. It gives you the words to open the conversation.

A verdict ends the conversation. Conditions keep it going.

What do you do at eleven at night when you do not know what to say?
