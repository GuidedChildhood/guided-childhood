## What changed from his draft, and why

Three risks were fixed and one line was added.

**The hook quote was not safe.** His draft opened on `"Turn academics against
Haidt."` in quotation marks with a capital T. No source supports that
capitalisation, and two outlets give incompatible versions of the line itself:
the New York Post quotes the note as "turn academics against Haidt", while
Technology Brief, citing The Verge, gives "turn other academics against Haidt"
followed by more text. One of them is wrong and the filing would settle it, but
CourtListener returns 403 to this container and both The Verge and the NY Post
block it. So the line is now described rather than quoted, which is true under
either version, and quotation marks are kept only for "whisper strategy", which
three outlets quote identically.

**Two credentials could not be verified.** "Over 100 scientific publications"
and "International recognition for her research" are unsupported by anything
found, and a reader who counts and gets ninety has him. Both cut, replaced with
facts that check in one click: Chancellor's Professor of Psychology and
Informatics at UC Irvine, and her review of The Anxious Generation in Nature.
Her own figure is 25 years, not "more than 20", so the post uses hers and says
it is hers.

**The National Academies line was softened in a way that hurt him.** His draft
had "often shows small effects and weak associations". "Often" is not in the
report and turns a firm finding vague. The Highlights PDF was pulled and read
directly, and both sentences in the post are now verbatim from it, December 2023.

**The line that was added**, because it is the accusation he will get: her name
is not in the Meta documents. Odgers appears nowhere in those notes. Saying so
himself stops anyone claiming he conflated two stories.

`says The Times suggested` is his wording and was left exactly as written. The
Times appears not to have stated that she was paid; that is her characterisation,
and attributing it to her is what keeps him safe. It must not drift to "The
Times accused her".

## The post

Court filings unsealed last month show Meta employees discussing a whisper strategy against Jonathan Haidt.

Nobody has shown it was ever carried out.

The notes come from a meeting in March 2023. They talk about turning academics against him and finding someone to write a piece attacking his work. They're also full of typos and shorthand, and they read more like a brainstorm than a plan. Meta hasn't commented.

Ugly and unproven, both at once.

Then the story turned.

Candice Odgers is one of the researchers who has challenged Haidt's conclusions. She says The Times suggested that she and other critics were paid by Meta to do it.

Her answer was flat. Meta doesn't fund her research. She's never been approached by Meta to shape what she says publicly.

Her name isn't in those Meta documents. The link was made by the reader, not the filing.

And that's where I have a problem, because the reasoning runs backwards. Her findings are inconvenient, therefore her motives must be bought.

She's Chancellor's Professor of Psychology and Informatics at UC Irvine. Twenty five years on child and adolescent mental health, her own count. Her review of The Anxious Generation ran in Nature.

That doesn't make her right.

It makes her someone you answer rather than someone you discredit.

And she isn't out on a limb. The National Academies, December 2023: available research linking social media to health "shows small effects and weak associations". The same report says its review "did not support the conclusion that social media causes changes in adolescent health at the population level".

That committee also said the evidence isn't robust enough to settle it. Which cuts at my own confidence, and I'll take it.

I've had a small taste of what happens when you question the size of an effect. Someone decides you're defending Big Tech.

I'm not.

I want harmful design challenged, and I want researchers free to challenge a popular claim. Both.

Meta's motives deserve scrutiny. So does Haidt's evidence. So does Odgers' evidence. Nobody gets a free pass.

Next I'm going through Haidt's recent interviews and the headlines built on them, to separate what the research shows from what it doesn't. Not to wave away real harm, and not to carry water for the platforms.

With the UK moving towards under sixteen restrictions, parents need perspective as much as children need protection.

Are we still judging the evidence, or have we started judging whose side people are on?

### First comment

Sources, and where I've been careful. The Meta documents were unsealed in the federal social media multidistrict litigation in California, in front of Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers, and reported from 27 October. I've described the line about turning academics against Haidt rather than quoting it, because outlets reporting the same note give it slightly differently and I haven't read the filing myself. The phrase I have quoted, whisper strategy, is quoted the same way everywhere. Nothing reported shows the plan was carried out, Meta hasn't commented, and the notes are a messy internal brainstorm, so treat them as what Meta discussed rather than what Meta did. Candice Odgers set out her position on her own Substack on 29 October: Meta does not fund her research and she has never been approached by Meta to shape what she says publicly. She characterises the Times piece as suggesting she and others were paid, and that characterisation is hers, not mine, which matters because so far as I can tell the article did not state it outright. Her name does not appear in the Meta notes. The National Academies report is Social Media and Adolescent Health, consensus study highlights, December 2023, and both quoted sentences are from it word for word. The same report says the rigorous designs that would settle causation are lacking, which is a limit on my argument as much as on anyone else's. On the UK, enforcement from 2027 is what the government has said; a firmer date has been reported but I haven't verified it.
