## 1. In Proportion, post 1: The reframe  [ALREADY POSTED]

The researchers went in expecting the clinicians to be worried. Every headline had told them a teenager naming their own depression or anxiety was a problem. Then they interviewed 16 clinicians, and most said the same surprising thing. Most of the time, it just did not matter. And often it was useful.

This is In Proportion, continued.

The study is Katie Cunningham-Rowe and Lucy Foulkes, published on the 15th of July 2026 in Child and Adolescent Mental Health. They interviewed 16 UK mental health clinicians, psychotherapists, clinical psychologists, counsellors, mental health nurses and others, and read the transcripts with reflexive thematic analysis. Worth being precise here. This is about mental disorders, depression, anxiety, bipolar. It is not about autism or ADHD. Different conversation, different evidence.

Here is what surprised them. They designed the study expecting clinicians to find adolescent self-diagnosis problematic, because that is what media and anecdote had primed everyone to expect. Instead, in most cases the clinicians said the label the young person arrived with was not the issue. Sometimes it was simply correct. Often it was useful therapeutic material, a door into the real conversation. The authors conclude that public understanding of this may be oversimplified, and overly negative.

Sit with that. The story we have all been told, that a generation is pathologising itself off TikTok, is not what the people in the room actually reported.

None of this means self-diagnosis never causes harm. It can, and later in this series I will give that its full weight, because a real minority get stuck. The authors are careful too. Sixteen clinicians is a small qualitative sample, all White, no psychiatrists and no GPs, and no young people's own voice in it. It describes what clinicians perceive. It does not count a population.

But here is the contrast that should stop you. The panic gets the front page. The clinicians in the room got a quieter finding, that a teenager reaching for a word is usually reaching to be understood, not to perform. We spent the alarm before we asked the people who do the work.

Ask the room before you trust the headline.

## 3. In Proportion, post 2: Why they do it

The researchers found something I did not expect. When a teenager reaches for a label like anxiety or depression, most of the time they are not copying a trend. They are reaching for a word because the thing underneath it is frightening, and a word makes it smaller.

This is In Proportion, continued.

One clinician in the study put it in a way I keep thinking about. The alternative to a name, they said, is worse. It is feeling like you are uniquely mad, or uniquely miserable, the only one it has ever happened to. A borrowed word is better than that, because it quietly says other people have felt this too, so maybe I am not broken.

The study is Katie Cunningham-Rowe and Lucy Foulkes, published on the 15th of July 2026 in Child and Adolescent Mental Health, 16 UK clinicians interviewed about young people who turn up having diagnosed themselves. The first thing they noticed was the need underneath it. Self-diagnosis, they said, comes from an inherent need to be understood. To make sense of a feeling, to hand it to someone, to have something to point at instead of a fog.

Once you see it that way, the blame drains out of the whole thing. A teenager who says I think I have anxiety is usually not performing for attention. They are trying to pass you their experience in a word small enough to fit across a kitchen table.

I am not going to pretend the words come from nowhere. The clinicians were honest about where kids pick them up. Social media, TikTok, the algorithm that keeps serving more of whatever you paused on. Schools and awareness campaigns too, which hand young people the vocabulary.

So here is the strongest version of the worry, and I will not dodge it. Awareness really does put labels in a child's hands they might never have found alone, and long waiting lists push them to name it themselves while they wait months for anyone qualified. Both are real.

But hold the two readings side by side. One says this child caught a diagnosis like a trend, so correct them. The other says this child was in enough pain to go looking for a word, and the world handed them one, so understand them. Same behaviour. Only one is true, and it is the one that earns your compassion.

It is the idea behind what we are building, for the parent in that moment. A child does not need a verdict, they need to be met, and the parent does not need a rule to enforce. So DiGi, the guide we are building for parents, is made to do what those clinicians did. It never tells you to accept the label or bin it. It stays curious, and hands you a calm way to be curious too.

Start with why they reached, not with what they grabbed.

## 5. In Proportion, post 3: It fades when they feel heard

Here is the finding from this study that could change a hard night in a lot of houses. When a young person genuinely feels heard, the label they arrived clutching often just loosens on its own. Nobody has to argue them out of it. In the clinicians' own words, it melts away.

This is In Proportion, continued.

The study is Katie Cunningham-Rowe and Lucy Foulkes, 16 UK clinicians, published on the 15th of July 2026 in Child and Adolescent Mental Health. One of their themes is exactly this. When adolescents feel validated, the self-diagnosis often fades over time. Not because an adult corrected it. Because the job the label was doing, the job of being understood, finally got done by a person instead of a word. Once the child feels met, they do not need the word so much.

But here is the part that is easy to get wrong, so I want to slow right down. Feeling heard is not the same as agreeing. Validation does not mean nodding along and saying, yes, you have bipolar, well spotted. It means staying curious. Taking your child seriously enough to explore the feeling with them, together, without either stamping the diagnosis as true or snatching it away.

That is a narrow path, and both sides are tempting. Dismiss the label and you confirm the exact fear that made them reach for it, that nobody will understand them, so they grip it tighter. Just accept it and you can set it in concrete before you have understood a single day of what it is actually made of. Curiosity is the road right down the middle.

And curiosity is not a polite way of disagreeing, because sometimes the young person is right. The study is clear that many self-diagnoses turn out to be accurate. So staying curious is not you talking them out of it. It is how you find out which kind you are dealing with, the feeling that fades once it is heard, or the real thing that needs proper help.

So picture the difference at the table. Correction sounds like, you are wrong, let me fix how you see yourself. Curiosity sounds like, tell me more, I am here. One makes a child defend the label like their life depends on it. The other makes the label quietly unnecessary. Same love. Opposite result.

This is the exact thing we are trying to build for parents. Not a tool that rules on your child, and not one that rubber stamps whatever they said either, because both do harm. Something that keeps you curious, and gives you the next calm thing to say. Curiosity is the thing that helps, and it is something a guide can be built to protect, and a parent can practise tonight.

Do not correct the label. Get so curious about the child that it melts.

## 7. In Proportion, post 4: Take it seriously, stay curious

Watch what the clinicians in this study did not do, because it is the whole thing. When a young person handed them a label, they did not say, no, you do not have that. And they did not say, yes, you clearly do. They took it seriously, and they stayed curious about it. That is the entire move, and it has a shape you can copy.

This is In Proportion, continued.

The study is Katie Cunningham-Rowe and Lucy Foulkes, 16 UK clinicians, published on the 15th of July 2026 in Child and Adolescent Mental Health. Read across everything they found and a simple practice falls out of it. A young person hands you a label. You do not rule on it. You get curious with them. And because they feel heard, one of two good things happens. Either the label does its job and points you at something real that needs help, or it quietly fades because the need underneath it got met. Either way, the child got the thing they actually came for, which was to be understood.

That is not a verdict. It is a pathway. A verdict is a door slamming shut, decided and final. A pathway is a walk you take together, one step at a time, staying open.

I want to be really honest about what that does and does not do, because there are a lot of people online promising more than they can deliver. Staying curious does not cure anything. It does not fix your child. It is not therapy. What it gives you is smaller and more real, a calmer conversation and a next step, instead of a slammed door or a rubber stamp. That is it. And in my experience that is a lot.

Here is the picture I would hold onto. Allow or deny is a courtroom, and a child on trial will defend their label to the death, because you have made it the thing they have to win. A pathway is a walk, and a child being walked beside will put the label down themselves the moment they no longer need to carry it. Same parent. Same love. Completely different room.

A Nature reviewer opened her review of the biggest parenting book of the decade with a warning.

She was right about the sales. Millions of copies. It changed the law in Australia. It is shaping it here.

He is right that phones in schools are a distraction. He is right that we over protect our children in the real world and under protect them online. And he is transparent, he posts his data and names his critics, which is more than most.

That gap, between the volume and the proof, is what this series is about. Not to dunk on a man who got more right than his critics admit. To get the proportion right, because our children are the ones who pay when we do not.

## 12. Louder Than The Evidence, post 2: The number is smaller than wearing glasses

Digital technology use explains at most 0.4 percent of the variance in a teenager's wellbeing.

That is not my number. It is from Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski at Oxford, published in Nature Human Behaviour, built from data on about 355,000 adolescents, using a method designed specifically to stop researchers cherry picking the result they wanted.

0.4 percent. To put it in their own comparison, the association is about the size of wearing glasses. It is smaller than the effect of being bullied. Smaller than a regular potato intake, which they included precisely to make the point.

This is the number underneath a decade of headlines telling you the phones are rewiring your child's brain.

Now the honest pivot, because I will not do to you what the panic does.

That number is an average, and averages hide things. Orben's own later work found windows where the effect is bigger, roughly ages 11 to 13 for girls and 14 to 15 for boys. And the average being small does not mean no child is harmed. Some are, badly. Wrong villain does not mean no villain.

But hold the two side by side. Two children, same three hours a day. One is a happy 12 year old making videos with friends. The other is an anxious 12 year old alone at 1am. The average says the screen barely moved either of them. The truth is the screen is doing almost nothing to the first child and sitting on top of something much heavier in the second.

The number is small because for most children the phone is not the story. For a few it is part of a story that was already being written.

If the average effect is the size of wearing glasses, why do you think the headline is always the size of an epidemic?

## 13. Louder Than The Evidence, post 3: The real cause does not fit on a poster [thesis post]

The same Nature reviewer said the quiet part out loud, and almost nobody quoted it.

Blaming social media, she wrote, "might distract us from effectively responding to the real causes of the current mental health crisis in young people."

Her name is Candice Odgers. She has followed thousands of children aged 10 to 14 since 2008. She is not a tech cheerleader. She is the person who has looked hardest and found the story is not the one we are being sold.

Here is what she means, and it is the most important thing I will post this month.

The signal often runs backwards. A child who is already struggling reaches for the phone more, the way a struggling adult does. So when you find heavy users are more anxious, you are often looking at distress causing the scrolling, not the scrolling causing the distress. A UK study last year, with proper clinical assessment of 3,340 teenagers, found exactly that pattern, and the authors were careful to say it shows no proof of cause and probably runs both ways.

And the things actually driving the crisis are the ones that do not trend. Poverty. What happens to a child at home. The 2008 crash landing hardest on the poorest families. A parent's own mental health, carried with too little help, and Odgers points out parental overdose deaths more than doubled in a decade.

None of that fits on a poster. The phone does. The phone is visible, and it is rich, so it makes a satisfying villain. The real drivers are old, expensive and awkward to film, so they sit in the dark.

I am not letting the platforms off. Their design deserves real scrutiny. But if we spend the next ten years fighting the phone, and the poverty and the adversity and the empty CAMHS waiting rooms are still there, we will have lost a generation while looking the other way.

The loudest villain is rarely the one doing the most damage.

What would change in the conversation if we treated a child's home, not their phone, as the main character?

And I know how this sounds mid crisis, so let me be plain. This is not so the phone does not matter. It is that a tired parent being told the phone is the problem is a tired parent aimed at the wrong target, while the real one goes unnamed and unfunded.

## 14. Louder Than The Evidence, post 4: Australia banned it, eight in ten stayed online

Australia banned social media for under 16s. Three months later, more than eight in ten of those teenagers were still on it.

VPNs. Borrowed logins. Fake ages. Some used a face mask to beat the age check. This is the government's own evidence, not a critic's.

I am not here to reopen the ban argument, that is settled and it is coming here too. I am here for what happens next, because that is the bit nobody prepares you for.

A ban is a locked door in a house with the windows open. The determined teenager is out the window in an afternoon. And here is the part that should stop us. The ones who climb out are now online with none of the age based protections switched on, no filters, no controls, on an anonymous account you cannot see. You have not removed the risk. You have removed your view of it.

Odgers puts it harder than I would dare. We have already pushed teenagers out of public spaces, she says, and now we are taking away the places they gather online too, because adults broke those places, and it is the children we are punishing for it. For an isolated child, a disabled child, a queer kid in a village who found their only community online, the ban does not take away a toy. It takes away a lifeline.

And the one thing that genuinely protects a child, the open conversation with a parent, is exactly what a ban shuts down. The moment it goes underground, they stop telling you. A study of around 4,000 teenagers found the ones with open communication at home had the healthiest relationship with the whole thing.

So by all means lock the door. Just do not tell yourself the job is done, because your child is already at the window, and now they are out there alone.

If a ban sends your child online alone instead of alongside you, who exactly does it protect?

It is that a ban without preparation moves the risk somewhere you can no longer see it. Tomorrow, the thing that actually works.

## 15. Louder Than The Evidence, post 5: The thing that works [CTA to /starter-pack]

There is a new study everyone in my world is about to misuse, so let me get to it first.

An Italian team followed 5,227 children through their real national test scores and found that opening a social media account at 11 rather than 14 tracked with lower maths and reading results years later. Published in Nature Human Behaviour this month. The ban camp is already waving it about as proof.

Read it properly and it says something better than a ban.

The authors do not conclude lock it away. They conclude three things together, delay, better design from the platforms, and digital education, and they are explicit that delay on its own is not enough. And the strongest thing they found was not screen hours at all. It was the phone bleeding into everything, into study, into meals, into sleep. The pattern, not the clock.

That is the whole of what I have spent this year building, and it is the quiet answer this series has been walking toward.

Not a wall, a pathway. You cannot ban your way to a capable 16 year old. You grow one, slowly, from about age four, one stage at a time, so that when the apps arrive, and they will, they arrive to a child who has already learned to see the machinery and steer it.

## 10. Founder Story, post 5: Why I am building this [carries the soft CTA]  [CTA to /starter-pack]

I did not set out to build a company. I built this for my own family first.

I hit the same wall every parent hits with my own children. Stop them, or guide them. And I was worn out by the argument the whole country was stuck in, ban or no ban, as if those were the only two doors in the room. Neither one teaches a child a single thing. I wanted a third way, a literacy way forward, something that dealt with the actual moments, the device at the dinner table, the meltdown when it goes off, the question I did not know how to answer, and took some of the weight off me while it did. So I started building it, for us, and it turned into this.

Everyone is banning. The UK has confirmed an under 16 social media ban. And I am building the thing the ban forgets.

Here is the quiet part I believe. A ban removes one visible thing from a child's life and leaves every hard thing exactly where it was. It delays the apps. It prepares no one. The day a sixteenth birthday arrives, a young person walks into all of it at once, with rules they never practised and habits they never built.

So we build the preparation. Guided Childhood is a pathway from age 4 to 16, for parents and for schools, that treats readiness as something you grow slowly, one stage at a time, long before any account exists. The ban takes the apps. We build the judgement.

And underneath all of it is a belief I do not shout, because the evidence has to earn it, not the volume. The loudest story says the phones did this to our children. I think the honest story is harder and less shareable. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are poverty, the things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with too little support. Social media gets the blame because it is visible, and because it is rich. The real drivers sit in the dark because they are old, and expensive, and awkward to film.

I cannot fix poverty from a parenting app. But I can refuse to build one more thing that points the finger at a tired parent and calls it a solution. What I can build is connection, because connection is the protection. A child who trusts you is safer than a child with every parental control ever made.

That is the whole of it. Not panic, and not a ban. Preparation, and trust, built early, at the age your child is now.
