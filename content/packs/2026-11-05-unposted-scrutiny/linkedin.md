## The three factual faults

**1. The 150,000 is not Ofcom's estimate.** GDC-052 says "Ofcom's own estimate is
that around 150,000 sixteen and seventeen year olds have neither", and GDC-050
states the number with no attribution at all. It is a CitizenCard estimate drawn
from a school survey of 8,459 sixteen and seventeen year olds that **Ofcom itself
describes as a non-representative sample**. Attributing a campaigning
organisation's non-representative estimate to the regulator is the single easiest
thing in this set for a hostile reader to take apart. Fixed in both.

**2. "A margin of error of a few years at 16" is not Ofcom's wording.** It comes
from a compliance vendor's blog. No source quotes Ofcom putting the margin in
years. What is confirmed verbatim is the remedy, not the size: services should
take a "challenge age" approach with a secondary check to account for the margin
of error. Fixed in 050 and 052.

**3. "Ofcom calls the list preliminary" is unsupported.** The word appears in no
source. Ofcom's own phrase is methods "currently capable of being highly
effective", and "currently" carries the provisionality honestly. Fixed in both.

Also corrected: the previous pass recorded that "completely robust" was not
Ofcom's text and removed it. That was wrong. ISPreview quotes Ofcom directly as
saying no age assurance method is "completely robust against circumvention by
children", so the phrase is now used, in quotation marks, as the regulator's.

The document can now be named: Ofcom, *Rapid assessment of highly effective age
assurance at 16*, 29 October 2026.

What IS corroborated independently: the sample of 1,950 children aged 10 to 17,

If Justin opens the PDF and the question wording is good, the original

# GDC-044 · A ban is already teaching. The question is what.

Body trimmed from 3,135 to inside the band. It carried **two** scenes from his own
house, the Covid YouTube admission and the Saturday football row, where the rule
is one at most. The football scene stays because it lands the thesis. The Covid
admission compresses to one line, which keeps the concession and loses the
duplication.

Nobody can tell you yet whether banning under sixteens from social media works. Australia can already tell you what it teaches.

Its regulator went back three months after the ban began and surveyed 803 children. Before it, nearly 86 percent were using at least one age restricted platform. After, still above 81 percent. A separate study in the BMJ, 436 adolescents, found insufficient evidence that use had meaningfully fallen.

Then look at how. The BMJ study found fake accounts, someone else's account, private browsing. The regulator found something blunter. Half the children who still had accounts said the platform had never checked their age, and 37 percent had simply put themselves down as sixteen or over.

That's a child's first real lesson in digital life, and a law delivered it.

Identity online is something you assert, and nobody checks.

We've been teaching it longer than the law has, mind. A 2011 survey of a thousand American parents found a third whose child had joined Facebook before turning thirteen, and two thirds of those parents helped set the account up. I'm in no position to be smug: we used YouTube as a babysitter to get through the Covid days with two small girls at home. Parents were always the teacher here. A ban only changes the syllabus.

None of which makes the ban wrong. A protected period is worth having. A deadline is not a plan, but it isn't nothing either.

What it buys is time. What nobody is answering is what the time is for.

France's constitutional council struck down the under fifteen ban at the heart of its own new law in August. A blanket ban on every under fifteen, whatever the child and whatever the service, was out of proportion. And parliament had written no safeguards for a scheme that, to work at all, makes every user prove their age, adults included.

Britain has its own version coming in 2027, and the argument will be the same one.

Both sides are counting the same thing. Whether children got in. Whether children were kept out.

Nobody is counting what a sixteen year old can actually do.

Years ago my son was watching a dance video and wouldn't come off it, and I threatened to take away Saturday football. I knew inside a minute I'd got it wrong, and what stayed with me wasn't the row. It was that I had nothing to give him instead.

A ban switches the device off. It doesn't sit down with your child.

So if the door opens on your child's sixteenth birthday, what do you want them to come and tell you first?

# GDC-045 · Fear is the engine on both sides now

Facts unchanged, all verified last pass. Register pass only: contractions in, and
two short paragraphs cut out of longer ones so the shape is lumpy.

A study everyone is quoting this week says 85 percent of Australian teenagers got round the under sixteen ban.

It doesn't say that.

The paper is in the BMJ, from the University of Newcastle. It recruited 436 adolescents and followed up with 408 of them three months after the ban began. What it found is that more than 85 percent of under sixteens were still using the platforms, mostly on accounts they already had.

Still using is not the same as broke in.

And the regulator's own follow up, 803 children, found the thing nobody is quoting. The share who actually held an account fell from 52.4 percent to 42.1 percent. eSafety calls that modest and statistically significant. It's the one measurable thing the ban has done so far, and it's missing from both arguments.

None of this means the ban works. The same paper says there isn't enough evidence of a real drop in use, its own authors say the full picture may take a decade, and it's one Australian state with self reported answers.

Jonathan Haidt made the loudest case of the decade out of alarm. A generation rewired. An epidemic with a start date. A conclusion that keeps arriving ahead of the effect sizes that would carry it.

The people who spend their days correcting him have started doing the same thing. A Psychology Today piece this month cited that BMJ paper as proof the ban failed.

The paper doesn't say the ban failed. It says we can't tell yet.

So fear is the engine on both sides now. One side is frightened of the phones. The other is frightened of the ban. Both reach past what the study will carry, because a careful answer doesn't move anybody.

Britain has this argument next and the law lands in 2027.

Which leaves a parent somewhere else entirely. The ban will do whatever it does. What happens in your house between now and the day it lands is the part nobody is legislating.

# GDC-048 · The chatbot confidant

The stiffest in the set: twelve expanded forms, no contractions, no short
paragraph. Facts unchanged, register rebuilt.

Twelve percent of American teenagers have already asked a chatbot for emotional support or advice.

Fifty four percent have used one for help with schoolwork.

Both numbers are from the Pew Research Center, 1,458 teenagers aged 13 to 17, surveyed in autumn 2025. These are American teenagers, not British ones, and "used it once" isn't "relies on it".

There are two readings of that twelve percent and I hold both on different days.

One says a teenager typing a worry into a machine at midnight is a child who didn't have, or didn't trust, a person to tell. The machine is the symptom, and it's a machine built to keep the conversation going.

The other says that for some young people, saying it first to something with no face is a way of practising. Rehearsing the sentence before they say it to a parent. That isn't nothing.

I don't think the survey can tell us which. Nobody has followed these children to find out.

What I do think is that the difference comes down to what happens next. Whether the conversation ends with a person, or ends with another conversation.

So the one question I ask about any tool a child talks to isn't whether it's safe.

It's whether it points back to a human when it matters.

Parents, which is it in your house: a place to practise, or a place to hide?

# GDC-049 · Twenty five thousand teenagers and the lag

Facts unchanged. Two fixes: the second question mark is gone, because the rule is
one closing question and nothing after it, and the register pass.

A study of 25,629 teenagers in Greater Manchester found no evidence that time on social media predicted later emotional difficulties.

It followed the same young people for three years, surveying them each autumn from 2021 to 2023. The team is at the University of Manchester, and it was published in the Journal of Public Health in March 2026.

The method matters. It separates what happens to a child from how children differ from each other, then asks one narrow thing: whether more social media this year predicts more emotional difficulty next year. For girls and for boys, the answer was no. The same went for gaming.

Now the honest part. This isn't a finding of no link at all. At the same moment in time, more social media went with slightly more emotional difficulty, and the paper calls it small. What it didn't find is that the one comes before the other.

And the limits are the authors' own. Everything was self reported. It measured hours, not what the hours were spent on. The gap between surveys was a year, which can hide shorter term effects. The average age at the start was about twelve and a half, so this says nothing about a nine year old.

The press release went further than the paper. It said social media and gaming did not cause symptoms. The paper says they may not play a causal role.

That's a smaller claim, and the right one.

Add it to the others. Different teams, different countries, a small effect each time.

This is The Wrong Villain series continued.

What it leaves open is the part parents actually live with. Not how long, but what, and who with, and what happened after.

If hours is the wrong measure, what would you want researchers to count instead?

# GDC-050 · Four ways to prove you are 16

Three factual fixes, all above: the 150,000 now carries its real provenance,
"preliminary" is gone, the margin of error is no longer stated in years, and
Ofcom's actual words on circumvention are quoted.

Ofcom has named four ways to check that a UK user is 16, and says no method is "completely robust against circumvention by children".

It published the assessment on 29 October, while Parliament works out how the under sixteen ban will run in practice.

The four are photo ID matching, digital ID services, banking based checks and facial age estimation. Ofcom's phrase for them is methods currently capable of being highly effective, and "currently" is doing work in that sentence.

Then the numbers that decide who actually gets through. An estimated 94 percent of people aged 16 and over hold a passport or driving licence. About 90 percent of sixteen and seventeen year olds hold a bank account. For those who hold neither document, the figure doing the rounds is 150,000, which comes from CitizenCard rather than Ofcom, out of a school survey Ofcom itself calls non representative. Treat it as an order of magnitude.

On face scanning, Ofcom doesn't publish an error in years. What it says is that services should use a challenge age, checking anyone who scans close to the line a second way.

To be fair, none of this says the ban can't work. A ban with real checks is a better ban than one without them, and this is a serious piece of work.

But look at what a check does. It decides who gets through the door.

It doesn't decide what a fifteen year old does with a friend's login, or what they feel when something odd lands in their feed.

Ofcom says it'll publish research on circumvention within twelve months of the ban coming into effect. Until then, what a child understands about why the rule exists is doing a lot of the work.

That's the part I build, so I'm not neutral. I'd still say the same thing without it: a deadline is not a plan.

# GDC-052 · A face scan to get into an app

Same three Ofcom fixes as 050. The "Ofcom's own estimate" attribution on the
150,000 was the worst single line in the unposted set and is gone.

A ban on social media under 16 needs a way to know who is 16, and Ofcom's shortlist is a passport, digital ID, bank check or face scan.

I'm not sure parents have been asked what they think of that.

Take each in turn. A passport or driving licence works for most people, though the estimate doing the rounds is that 150,000 sixteen and seventeen year olds hold neither, and that one comes from CitizenCard out of a survey Ofcom itself calls non representative. A bank check covers about 90 percent of that age group, which leaves around one in ten outside it. A face scan has a margin of error at 16, which is why Ofcom wants a challenge age and a second check. And a digital ID means doing the photo ID step once, then reusing it.

Each one fixes a problem and hands over something else.

A child's face, a child's bank details, or a new ID that sits behind every app.

The people who want the ban have a fair answer. Checks are the price of a rule that means something, and the alternative is the system we have now, which is a box a child ticks.

I can hold both of those at once. What I can't tell you is where the line is for your child, and neither can Ofcom. Its four methods are the ones it says are currently capable of working, and it'll consult on draft guidance once the regulations are laid in Parliament.

So the live question isn't only whether there should be a ban. It's what you'd be willing to hand over to make it work.

This is the ban series continued.

Would you let your child use a face scan or a bank check to get into an app, and what would you want promised in return?
