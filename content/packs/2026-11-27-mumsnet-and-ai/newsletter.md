## The edition

Last week I asked parents on Mumsnet to try what I'm building and tell me what was wrong with it.

The first reply said the post sounded "so ridiculously Chat GPTd that it is actually painful to read". Another said it was always dads with brilliant apps. A few more pointed out that nobody could tell from the post what the thing actually did. The thread has since come down.

I want to write about it properly, because there are three things in that thread worth more than my pride: what it says about asking for feedback, what it says about AI and writing, and what it means for parents now that almost anyone can build an app.

### The feedback was rude, and it was right

Mumsnet doesn't do polite, and it doesn't owe a stranger polite. If you walk into a parents' forum with a link and a request, you are asking busy people for their time, and they will tell you exactly what they think of the way you asked.

Strip out the tone and the thread was the most useful thing anyone said to me that week.

One parent read a line from our bedtime script and pointed out that any child would argue straight through it. She was right, and she added the bit that stuck: "Any vaguely savvy child will home in on weaknesses in the words used." Her suggestion was to get actual children to evaluate them. We've rewritten the line, and every script line now gets read to a child of the right age before it ships, with their first comeback written down.

Another parent tried it and said, "I wasn't sure where it was heading." She was right too. The page you landed on after the questions gave you advice and stopped. It now says what happens next.

A third said the app implies we need screens to help us avoid screens. That one is a fair worry, and the honest answer is in the product rather than the post: most of it happens with the phone down, and the rules apply to the adults too.

None of that would have reached me through friends being kind.

So here is the bit I'd say to any founder about to ask strangers for feedback. Say who you are in the first line. Say what you want and what you'll do with it before anything else. Give a web address people can type rather than a link they have to trust. Name one real moment from your own life that made you build it, and don't make one up. And expect to be told your baby is ugly, because sometimes it is.

### What I think about AI and writing now

The ChatGPT comment was fair, and it landed because I'd been doing something I thought was sensible.

I've always struggled with writing. I did at school and I still do. My handwriting was a mess and grammar never came easily. AI has given me tools I didn't have: I can get my thoughts down, then use it to tidy them. For someone like me that is a real help.

What I learned is where the line sits. Readers on Mumsnet drew it very clearly in their own threads about AI. Using it as an editor of your own words is mostly fine. Using it as the author is not. When the machine writes the sentences, it smooths out the person, and parents can smell that from a long way off.

That matches the wider picture. Pew Research looked at a random sample of 10,000 English web pages collected in July 2026 and found 10 percent showed significant signs of AI authorship. Pew is careful to say its detector isn't perfect and gets it wrong in both directions, so take the exact number lightly. The direction is the point: there is a lot more machine written text around, and readers are getting better at noticing the sameness.

So these are my rules now. In other people's spaces, forums and groups and replies to parents, I write it myself and the tools only check spelling and facts. If AI has helped with the words, I say so. And I never add a story, a feeling or a figure that isn't real to make something read better.

### The new apps are coming, and parents shouldn't have to find out the hard way

Here is the part that matters most to you as a parent.

I'm not a developer. I came up through accounting and small businesses, and I've always learned by building things. For years an idea like mine needed developers, money and luck. Now a tool like Claude means one person can build a working app without a team.

That is mostly good. People who understand a problem from the inside can now build for it. But it also means you're about to meet a lot more apps aimed at families, made quickly, sometimes by people who mean well and haven't thought hard about safety, and sometimes by people who know that a worried parent is an easy sale.

You shouldn't need to be an expert to tell the difference. Before you hand over your child's details to anything new, including what I'm building, here are the questions I'd ask:

- **Who is behind it?** A real name, where they are, and why they built it. If you can't find out, that tells you something.
- **Can you find it yourself?** Type the address into your browser rather than clicking a link someone sent you.
- **What happens to what you tell it?** Look for a plain answer about what is kept, for how long, and whether you can delete it. Nothing about your child should be stored unless you chose to save it.
- **What does it claim, and can it back it up?** Be wary of anything that promises to fix your child, or that leads with fear. Good tools say what the evidence does and doesn't show.
- **What does it cost, and when?** It should tell you up front, not after you've spent twenty minutes on it.

None of that needs any technical knowledge. They are the questions you'd ask before your child joined a new club: who runs it, what goes on there, and who you talk to if something goes wrong. An app aimed at your family should be able to answer them on its home page, in plain words, without you having to dig.

In the UK, online services likely to be used by children are expected to follow the ICO's Children's Code, which covers things like collecting as little data as possible and having high privacy settings by default. It's worth knowing it exists, because it gives you a standard to hold any new app to.

### Where that leaves me

I'd rather be told it reads like ChatGPT than never be told at all. The thread made the product better and it made me better at saying what it is.

If you're building something for parents, ask them, and ask properly. And if you're a parent being asked, the blunt reply is the useful one. Just know that the person on the other end is usually one person at a kitchen table, trying to get something right.
