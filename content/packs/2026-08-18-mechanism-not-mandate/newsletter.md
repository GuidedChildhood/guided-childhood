## Mechanism, not mandate

I write a lot about how Jonathan Haidt overreaches on the evidence. This week I caught myself doing his trick, and I want to walk you through it, because the fix turned out to be the clearest thing I believe.

Not his conclusion. His method. His method is not the ban, it is the order of operations. Lead with the scariest single child. Name a platform. Let the alarm land first and the evidence arrive second, so that by the time the reader meets the actual finding, their body has already decided to be afraid. I had a set of careful findings about young children and online content, and when I wrote them up, I did exactly that. Fear first. Evidence late. Platform as the monster. While disagreeing with everything Haidt builds on top of that engine.

So here is the correction, in public, because building this in the open is the whole point.

The study I was leaning on is sixteen children. It is careful work, from a clinician I respect, and it shows something real. But you do not get national policy from sixteen children, and you certainly do not get a ban. What you get from sixteen children is a reason to prepare one. Not to frighten a parent. Not to wall off a platform. To teach a child what they might meet, before they meet it. I should have said that first. I led with the frightening part instead, and that is the exact move I criticise in him.

### The rule almost nobody makes

Here is the principle I wish everyone shouting about children and screens would hold. The strength of evidence you need scales with the cost of being wrong.

Weak evidence, a small study, a mechanism seen in a handful of children, an association that explains a fraction of a percent, is enough to justify a cheap, reversible, low regret response. Teach it. Prepare for it. Train a teacher to spot it. Fund the bigger study. You lose nothing by being wrong, because a prepared child is a good outcome either way.

Weak evidence is not enough for a ban, a criminal offence, or walling a platform off. Those are expensive, they affect a child's rights, they get evaded within weeks, and they do real harm if the evidence was thin. They need strong, replicated, causal proof, which on most of these questions we simply do not have.

Haidt's mistake, in one line, is that he reaches for the expensive response on the strength of the cheap evidence. Maximal policy, modest proof. That is why his own field keeps pushing back, and why, for all that he named a real worry, his approach does not convince me.

### Why education is not a soft option, it is the logical one

Education is the only response that is safe to be wrong about. If the harm turns out to be big, you prepared the child for it. If the harm turns out to be small, you still handed them judgement they will use for the rest of their lives. There is no future in which teaching a child to understand the machine was the wrong call.

A ban has a wrong version. The eight in ten who climb over it. The child driven underground, onto an account no adult can see. The lifeline cut from the isolated kid who needed it. Education has no wrong version. That asymmetry is not a talking point. It is the whole company.

So when someone hands me a small, worrying study, I no longer ask whether it is enough to ban something. I ask whether it is enough to teach something. It almost always is. Weak evidence is a mandate to prepare, and a prohibition on prohibiting.

### On Roblox, and every platform like it

The easiest post I could write this year is "Roblox is dangerous, here is the proof, share if you agree." It would fly. I am not going to write it.

Roblox has real problems, leaky age checks, litigation, content a young child should not meet, and I will say so, because it is true. What I will not do is turn the platform into a monster and imply the answer is to keep your child away from it. Two reasons, and they are the whole of what I build.

Contact is inevitable. Your child will meet these platforms, at a friend's house, on a sibling's tablet, at sixteen when the delay lifts. A childhood walled off from the machine does not produce a safe adult, it produces a sixteen year old who meets all of it at once, with no habits and no judgement. The platform being imperfect is the argument for preparation, not against contact.

And demonising the platform teaches your child nothing, while making it forbidden fruit. The child who understands how the machine works, and who has one adult they trust, is safer inside Roblox than the child who was simply told Roblox is bad. My quarrel is never with the platform. It is with the idea that a gate is a substitute for a prepared child.

### The test I now run on everything

Does this leave a parent more frightened, or more capable? If it is more frightened, it failed, whatever the conclusion was. Say the small numbers out loud. Never make a platform the villain. Match the ask to the evidence. Keep Haidt as the honest contrast, he named the worry and reaches too far, we share the worry and respond in proportion.

We use the evidence to prepare a child, never to frighten a parent or forbid a platform. Mechanism informs education. Only strong evidence informs prohibition. That is the whole difference, and it is the whole company.
