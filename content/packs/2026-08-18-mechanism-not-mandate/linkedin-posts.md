## POST 1, I caught myself using the exact tactic I criticise.
**Hook flavour:** vulnerable, building in the open.

**Post text:**

I write a lot about how Jonathan Haidt overreaches on the evidence. This week I caught myself doing his trick.

Not his conclusion. His method.

I had a set of findings about young children and online content, real, careful, from a clinician I respect. And when I wrote them up, I led with the scariest single child. I named a platform. I let the alarm land first and the evidence arrive second. By the time you got to what I actually believe, which is teach the child, your body had already been told to be afraid.

That is Haidt's engine. It is not the ban that makes his work a problem, it is the order. Fear first, evidence late, platform cast as the monster. And I used it, while disagreeing with everything he builds on top of it.

So here is the correction, out loud, because building this in the open is the whole point.

The study I was quoting is sixteen children. Sixteen. It is a careful piece of work and it shows something real, but you do not get national policy from sixteen children, and you certainly do not get a ban. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you fear, and I will not be one of them, even by accident.

What you get from sixteen children is a reason to prepare one. Not to frighten a parent. Not to wall off a platform. To teach a child what they might meet, before they meet it.

That is the line I nearly crossed, and the one I am redrawing in public.

The test I now run on everything I write is simple. Does this leave a parent more frightened, or more capable? If it is more frightened, I have failed, whatever my conclusion was.

Have you ever caught yourself using the exact method you criticise in someone else?

**First comment:** To be specific, the study is Catherine Knibbs's doctoral research, sixteen children, qualitative. It is good work and it shows a mechanism. It cannot show how common the thing is, and I should have led with that, not with the frightening bit. The fix is in the next post.

## POST 2, You cannot build a ban on sixteen children. You can build a lesson.
**Hook flavour:** contrarian, the insight.

**Post text:**

You cannot build a ban on sixteen children. You can build a lesson. That one distinction is the whole argument, and almost nobody in this debate makes it.

Here is the rule I wish everyone shouting about kids and screens would hold.

The strength of evidence you need scales with the cost of being wrong.

Weak evidence, a small study, an association that explains a fraction of a percent, a mechanism seen in a handful of children, is enough to justify a cheap, reversible, low regret response. Teach it. Prepare for it. Train a teacher to spot it. Fund the bigger study. You lose nothing by being wrong, because a prepared child is a good outcome either way.

Weak evidence is not enough to justify a ban, a criminal offence, or walling a platform off. Those are expensive, they affect rights, they get evaded, and they do real harm if the evidence was thin. Those need strong, replicated, causal proof. Which, on most of these questions, we simply do not have.

Haidt's mistake in one line. He reaches for the expensive response on the strength of the cheap evidence. Maximal policy, modest proof. That is why his own field keeps pushing back, and why, for all that he named a real worry, his approach does not convince me.

Now here is why education wins, and it is not a slogan, it is logic.

Education is the only response that is safe to be wrong about. If the harm turns out to be big, you prepared the child for it. If the harm turns out to be small, you still gave them judgement they will use for life. There is no future in which teaching a child to understand the machine was the wrong call.

A ban has a wrong version. The eight in ten who climb over it. The child driven underground. The lifeline cut. Education has none.

So when someone hands me a small, worrying study, I do not ask "is this enough to ban something." I ask "is this enough to teach something." It almost always is.

When the evidence is thin but the worry is real, what is the response you can afford to be wrong about?

**First comment:** This is the honest reconciliation with everything I have written about the evidence being weaker than the headlines. Weak evidence is a mandate to prepare and a prohibition on prohibiting. You act, but you act in proportion. That is the whole difference between preparing a child and banning a platform.

## POST 3, I will not call Roblox a monster. Here is why that costs me reach and I do it anyway. [soft CTA]
**Hook flavour:** honest middle.

**Post text:**

The easiest post I could write this year is "Roblox is dangerous, here is the proof, share if you agree." It would fly. I am not going to write it.

Roblox has real problems. Leaky age checks, litigation, content a seven year old should not meet. I will say all of that, because it is true. What I will not do is turn the platform into a monster and imply the answer is to keep your child away from it. Two reasons, and they are the whole of what I build.

First, contact is inevitable. Your child will meet these platforms. At a friend's house, on a sibling's tablet, at sixteen when whatever delay you managed finally lifts. A childhood spent walled off from the machine does not produce a safe adult. It produces a sixteen year old who meets all of it at once, with no habits and no judgement, which is the most dangerous version of all.

Second, calling the platform evil teaches your child nothing, and makes it forbidden fruit. The child who understands how the machine works, why it serves them the next thing, and how to feel the pull and step back, is safer inside Roblox than the child who was simply told Roblox is bad.

The platform being imperfect is the argument for preparation. It is not the argument against contact. Those are two completely different responses, and the whole panic industry blurs them on purpose.

So my quarrel is never with the platform. It is with the idea that a gate is a substitute for a prepared child. A face scan a teenager beats in an afternoon is not a safety plan. A child who has been taught, and who has one adult they trust, is.

That is what we build. Not a wall, and not a fright. A pathway that prepares a child before they meet the world online, and a guide that helps a parent be the calm askable adult when they do.

If you want the calm version of all this, we made a free place to start, at guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack.

Would you rather your child met the machine walled off from it, or prepared for it?

**First comment:** This is why the demonise the platform post performs and I still will not write it. Fear travels further than proportion, in the short run. But the parents I want are the ones who can tell the difference, and a frightened parent is not a prepared one. The whole company is a bet that proportion wins in the end.
