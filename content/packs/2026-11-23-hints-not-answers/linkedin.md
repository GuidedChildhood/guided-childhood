## GDC 065, post 1. The study that went the other way

HOOKS
REVERSAL: Stanford went through 52 controlled studies of AI and schoolwork, and neither way of using it reliably improved learning.
UNCOMFORTABLE QUESTION: I built a homework rule into my product on one trial. What do I do now a second trial disagrees?
COMPARISON: In one trial hints beat answers. In a newer one answers beat hints. Both are in the same Stanford review.

CHOSEN: REVERSAL. It names the body and the finding in one sentence a stranger can finish, and it is the reviewers' own conclusion. The uncomfortable question opens on us, which did 81 impressions last time. The comparison is accurate but needs the review explained before it lands.

### The post

Stanford went through 52 controlled studies of AI and schoolwork, and neither way of using it reliably improved learning.

That includes the way I built into my own product.

In our app, a child who is stuck on homework gets a hint. Then a bigger hint. Then a worked example on a different question. The answer is held back.

I chose that because of a trial in Turkish secondary schools. Pupils given a chatbot that handed over answers did worse in the exam than pupils given a tutor that only gave hints.

The Stanford review, updated in November, keeps that trial. It also adds one that went the other way.

199 American college students practised with AI feedback. The ones given the correct answer and an explanation straight away scored higher afterwards than the ones given encouragement and hints first.

The reviewers file that pair under "conflicting". I think that is the right word.

What does hold across the review is narrower. When students used AI to produce the work, they tended to do worse later without it. When they used it to understand something, results were better, and still mixed. Their sentence: "neither type of use consistently improves learning."

The limits are large. Most of these studies are of university students. Five of the new ones were in American schools, and I could not find a British school among the new student studies. Several are not peer reviewed yet. And the review says it "still does not show whether AI tools can produce long-term learning gains."

So I am keeping the hint card, because "the work stays yours" still has the better of the evidence.

I am less sure than I was that holding back the answer is the part doing the good. The reviewers say the evidence is "still unclear on which characteristics matter most", and I have no better answer than they do.

Sources are in the first comment.

If you teach, or you have sat beside a child doing homework with a chatbot open: what did you see that told you they were learning and not copying?

### First comment

Source: Walker, Fesler, Martinez Claeys, Agnew and Loeb, "The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review", updated November 2026, SCALE Initiative, Stanford University. 52 causal papers (randomised trials and quasi experiments), up from 20 in March. It is a narrative review, so there is no pooled effect size to quote. The Turkish trial is Bastani and colleagues, PNAS 2025: the hint tutor used guidance written by teachers and withheld answers. The newer one is Cao and colleagues, 2026, a preprint: 199 US college students recruited online, both groups got AI feedback, and the hint version did reveal the answer in the end. So the two are not a clean rematch. The review also includes a trial of 6,997 US pupils in grades 6 to 8 where adding an AI tutor to a maths platform made no statistically significant difference overall on a later test. The review is funded by the Walton Family Foundation, the Overdeck Family Foundation and Google.org. Our hint card is for ages 10 and over, one card with no chat box, and under 10s are pointed to a grown up. I cannot tell you that from this, and nor can the review. The pattern that held best is that asking it to explain went better than asking it to do the work. Most of that is from university students, so I would hold it loosely for a ten year old.

## GDC 066, post 2. The parent who has said nothing

HOOKS
OVERLOOKED FINDING: RAND asked a thousand American parents what they tell their teenagers about AI and homework, and one in five had never raised it.
COMPARISON: About half of American teenagers now use AI for homework. One in five of their parents has not mentioned it.
PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE: "No AI in this house" is the shortest thing a parent can say about homework, and RAND's survey suggests it is not enough.

CHOSEN: OVERLOOKED FINDING. One body, one figure, one complete sentence, and it is from the weighted national sample. The comparison sets two different samples side by side as if they were the same households. The practical consequence leans on the small matched sample and implies a cause the survey cannot show.

### The post

RAND asked a thousand American parents what they tell their teenagers about AI and homework, and one in five had never raised it.

I am not sure which group I would have been in a year ago.

The survey ran in May. Of about 1,800 teenagers, 49 percent said they use AI for homework. A year earlier it was 40 percent.

Among the parents, 56 percent encourage it to some degree. 24 percent discourage it altogether. 20 percent have not talked about it.

The part I keep turning over is smaller and shakier.

RAND matched 533 of the teenagers to their own parents. Of those who felt their parents discouraged AI for school, 38 percent said they used it for homework anyway.

That number needs its warning. The matched group is small and unweighted, and RAND says it should not be read as all American families. It is one snapshot, so it cannot show that what a parent says changes what a teenager does. None of it is British.

RAND's own conclusion is careful: parents "likely need to be much more explicit for their messages to get through to their teens."

"No AI in this house" is one sentence. The explicit version takes longer. What it may do. What it may not. How the two of you check what it says.

We wrote ours for parents of eight to ten year olds, because I would rather the first go happened with a parent in the room. It starts "Show me how you ask it." It is one of the free scripts in the app.

Sources are in the first comment.

What have you said to your child about AI and homework, in the words you used?

### First comment

Source: Schwartz and Diliberti, "Higher-Income Parents Use AI More and Encourage Their Children to Do the Same", RAND, RR-A4742-5, 12 November 2026, sponsored by the Gates Foundation. Parents: about 1,000, nationally representative, with a child aged 12 to 17, surveyed May 2026. Teenagers: 1,810, aged 12 to 18, May 2026. RAND did not test the 40 to 49 percent change for significance and says differences between the two survey rounds may explain some of it. Most of the 56 percent who encourage AI chose the middle option: not to think for them, yes for things like brainstorming or checking work. The 38 percent comes from 533 teenagers matched to 427 parents, unweighted. In the full teenage sample, 51 percent of those who said a parent discouraged them still used it. US only.
