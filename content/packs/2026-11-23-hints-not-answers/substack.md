# Substack: Hints beat answers. Then answers beat hints.

Stanford went through 52 controlled studies of AI and schoolwork in November, and neither way of using it reliably improved learning. I read it with more interest than comfort, because one of those studies is the reason a feature in our app works the way it does.

## What I built, and why

When a child of ten or over is stuck on homework in our app, they can ask for a hint. They get a nudge. If they are still stuck, the method. After that, a worked example on a different question. The answer is held back.

I took that from a trial in Turkish secondary schools, published in PNAS in 2025. Pupils with a chatbot that gave answers did worse in the exam than pupils with a tutor that only gave hints.

## The part that cuts against me

The Stanford review keeps that trial and adds a newer one. 199 American college students practised with AI feedback. Those given the correct answer and an explanation at once did better afterwards than those given encouragement and hints first.

It is not a clean rematch. The Turkish tutor used guidance written by teachers. The newer hint version was written by the AI and gave the answer in the end. The newer study is a preprint, and its students were adults recruited online. The reviewers file the two under "conflicting", and they are right to.

There is a second awkward one. A trial with 6,997 American pupils in grades 6 to 8 added an AI tutor to a maths platform. On the later test it made no statistically significant difference overall.

## What held

"AI appears to be better for learning when students use it for learning support than when they use it for producing work, although neither type of use consistently improves learning."

That is the reviewers' sentence and I would not improve on it. Using AI to produce the work tended to cost students later, when they had to work without it. Using it to understand did better, with mixed results.

## How much weight it bears

Less than the headline. Most of these studies are of university students. Five of the new ones were in American schools. I could not find a British school among the new student studies. Several have not been peer reviewed. The review says plainly that it "still does not show whether AI tools can produce long-term learning gains." It is funded by the Walton Family Foundation, the Overdeck Family Foundation and Google.org.

## Meanwhile, at home

A RAND survey published two days later asked about 1,000 American parents what they tell their teenagers about AI and homework. 56 percent encourage it to some degree, 24 percent discourage it altogether, and 20 percent had not talked about it. Of about 1,800 teenagers, 49 percent said they use it for homework.

RAND also matched 533 teenagers to their own parents. Of those who felt discouraged, 38 percent used it anyway. That group is small and unweighted, and RAND says not to read it as all American families. A survey at one moment cannot show what a parent's words change.

Their conclusion is modest: parents "likely need to be much more explicit for their messages to get through to their teens."

## What I am doing with it

Keeping the hint card. "The work stays yours" still has the better of the evidence.

Holding the rest more loosely. The reviewers say the evidence is "still unclear on which characteristics matter most", and I have no better answer than they do.

## What to do on Sunday

Sit down with one piece of homework and say "Show me how you ask it." Ask one question as "what is the answer" and the same one as "how would I work this out". Then ask it something you both know, and see whether it gets it right.

Those words are from one of the free scripts in our app. They work without it.
