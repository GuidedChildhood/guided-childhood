# GDC-047 · Half of UK children have an AI toy

**Job: recruit strangers.** Hook 98 characters, body 1,654.

Parents in the UK say half of their children have already been given an AI toy or learning device.

Thirty eight percent own two or more.

That comes from a May 2026 survey of 1,000 UK parents by FocalData, published by the British Standards Institution. It is a parent survey, so it is what parents report, not what children say. And "AI enabled toy or learning device" covers a lot, from a spelling tutor to a bear that talks back.

So it does not mean half of children are in danger. It means a category most of us have not thought hard about is already in the house.

The talking one is what I keep coming back to. Common Sense Media, which surveyed American parents earlier this year, found nearly half had bought or considered buying one. Their advice is that no child aged five or under should use an AI toy at all, and that parents should take care with ages six to twelve.

A toy that answers every question, never gets tired and never says "not now" is teaching a child something about what a relationship is. A seven year old is not well placed to see that for themselves.

Nobody has told most parents what to look for, and the survey says as much: the BSI headline is about gaps in guidance.

Here is what I would want a parent to be able to say to a child. Who is on the other side of this. What it remembers. What it cannot do. When to go and find a person. We teach a short lesson for younger children on exactly that, and it starts from the idea that the child should be able to tell you.

An age on a box will not do that for us.

If a toy in your house could talk back tomorrow, what is the first thing you would want to know about it?

# GDC-049 · Twenty five thousand teenagers and the lag

**Job: build authority.** Hook 133 characters, body 1,695. Replaces the first 049 draft (the Australian BMJ study), withdrawn because GDC-044 and GDC-045 already carry that study.

A study of 25,629 teenagers in Greater Manchester found no evidence that time on social media predicted later emotional difficulties.

It followed the same young people for three years, surveying them each autumn from 2021 to 2023. The team is at the University of Manchester, and it was published in the Journal of Public Health in March 2026.

The method matters. It separates what happens to a child from how children differ from each other, then asks a narrow question: does more social media this year predict more emotional difficulty next year? For girls and for boys, the answer was no. The same went for gaming.

Now the honest part. This is not a finding of no link at all. At the same moment in time, more social media went with slightly more emotional difficulty, and the paper calls it small. What it did not find is that the one comes before the other.

And the limits are the authors' own. Everything was self reported. It measured hours, not what the hours were spent on. The gap between surveys was a year, which can hide shorter term effects. The average age at the start was about twelve and a half, so this says nothing about a nine year old.

The press release went further than the paper. It said social media and gaming did not cause symptoms. The paper says they may not play a causal role. That is a smaller claim and the right one.

Add it to the others. Different teams, different countries, a small effect each time.

This is The Wrong Villain series continued.

What it leaves open is the part parents actually live with: not how long, but what, and who with, and what happened after.

If hours is the wrong measure, what would you want researchers to count instead?

### First comment

The paper: Cheng, Panayiotou, Finseras, Andersen and Humphrey, "How do social media use, gaming frequency, and internalizing symptoms predict each other over time in early-to-middle adolescence?", Journal of Public Health, volume 48, issue 1, March 2026, online December 2025. Sample 25,629 from the #BeeWell cohort in Greater Manchester, three annual waves, autumn 2021 to 2023. Outcome is the emotional difficulties scale of Me and My Feelings, which is a symptom score and not a diagnosis of anxiety or depression. The lagged paths from social media to later symptoms were not significant for girls or boys (for girls, b of minus 0.001 with a 95 percent interval of minus 0.009 to 0.006). Same wave associations were small but present. Two things I could not check: the supplementary tables on active versus passive use, and anything about heavy users as a group, because the paper treats hours as a continuous measure. University of Manchester release, 14 January 2026. The sourced breakdown behind this series: guidedchildhood.com/v/li-1029-beewell

# GDC-048 · The chatbot confidant

**Job: start an argument.** Hook 98 characters, body 1,335.

Twelve percent of American teenagers have already asked a chatbot for emotional support or advice.

Fifty four percent have used one for help with schoolwork.

Both numbers are from the Pew Research Center, 1,458 teenagers aged 13 to 17, surveyed in autumn 2025. These are American teenagers, not British ones, and "used it once" is not "relies on it".

There are two readings of that twelve percent and I hold both on different days.

One says a teenager typing a worry into a machine at midnight is a child who did not have, or did not trust, a person to tell. The machine is the symptom, and it is a machine that is built to keep the conversation going.

The other says that for some young people, saying it first to something with no face is a way of practising. Rehearsing the sentence before they say it to a parent. That is not nothing.

I do not think the survey can tell us which of those it is. Nobody has followed these children to find out.

What I do think is that the difference comes down to what happens next. Whether the conversation ends with a person, or ends with another conversation.

That is why the one question I ask about any tool a child talks to is not whether it is safe. It is whether it points back to a human when it matters.

Parents, which is it in your house: a place to practise, or a place to hide?

### First comment

US only. I have not seen an equivalent UK figure I would trust and I would welcome one. I wrote the two readings in the post as equals on purpose; if the practising reading is wrong I would like to hear why.
