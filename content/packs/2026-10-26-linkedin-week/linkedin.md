# GDC-047 · Half of UK children have an AI toy

**Job: recruit strangers.** Hook 98 characters, body 1,654.

Parents in the UK say half of their children have already been given an AI toy or learning device.

Thirty eight percent own two or more.

That comes from a May 2026 survey of 1,000 UK parents by FocalData, published by the British Standards Institution. It is a parent survey, so it is what parents report, not what children say. And "AI enabled toy or learning device" covers a lot, from a spelling tutor to a bear that talks back.

So it does not mean half of children are in danger. It means a category most of us have not thought hard about is already in the house.

The talking one is what I keep coming back to. Common Sense Media, which surveyed American parents earlier this year, found nearly half had bought or considered buying one. Their advice is that no child aged five or under should use an AI toy at all, and that parents should take care with ages six to twelve.

A toy that answers every question, never gets tired and never says "not now" is teaching a child something about what a relationship is. A seven year old is not well placed to see that for themselves.

Nobody has told most parents what to look for, and the survey says as much: the BSI headline is about gaps in guidance.

Here is what I would want a parent to be able to say to a child. Who is on the other side of this. What it remembers. What it cannot do. When to go and find a person. We teach a short lesson for younger children on exactly that, and it starts from the idea that the child should be able to tell you.

An age on a box will not do that for us.

If a toy in your house could talk back tomorrow, what is the first thing you would want to know about it?

# GDC-049 · The ban and 408 teenagers

**Job: build authority.** Hook 135 characters, body 1,533.

A BMJ study of 408 Australian teenagers found little evidence that the under 16 ban had reduced their social media use three months in.

The team at the University of Newcastle surveyed the same young people before the law started in December 2025 and again about three months after.

Their conclusion, in their words: little evidence of immediate substantive reductions in reported use by adolescents under 16.

The detail is where it gets interesting. Use among 12 and 13 year olds was relatively stable. Among 14 and 15 year olds it was lower. A rule does not land evenly, and the children who were most online before are not always the ones who change.

Now the part I want to be fair about. Three months is early. This was reported use, not measured use, and not harm. The authors do not say the law has failed, and neither am I. A ban can change a norm slowly and still be worth having.

Here in the UK the government has announced the same thing for spring 2027, with the first regulations due before Parliament by the end of this year. So this is the nearest thing to an early read on what is coming to every parent I know.

What it tells me is narrow. A line in the law changes what a platform has to do. It does not decide what a fourteen year old does at ten at night, or what they say when something goes wrong.

That part still belongs at the kitchen table. The ban is the deadline. It was never the plan.

If the UK ban lands in spring, what is the first conversation you would want to have had with your child by then?

# GDC-048 · The chatbot confidant

**Job: start an argument.** Hook 98 characters, body 1,335.

Twelve percent of American teenagers have already asked a chatbot for emotional support or advice.

Fifty four percent have used one for help with schoolwork.

Both numbers are from the Pew Research Center, 1,458 teenagers aged 13 to 17, surveyed in autumn 2025. These are American teenagers, not British ones, and "used it once" is not "relies on it".

There are two readings of that twelve percent and I hold both on different days.

One says a teenager typing a worry into a machine at midnight is a child who did not have, or did not trust, a person to tell. The machine is the symptom, and it is a machine that is built to keep the conversation going.

The other says that for some young people, saying it first to something with no face is a way of practising. Rehearsing the sentence before they say it to a parent. That is not nothing.

I do not think the survey can tell us which of those it is. Nobody has followed these children to find out.

What I do think is that the difference comes down to what happens next. Whether the conversation ends with a person, or ends with another conversation.

That is why the one question I ask about any tool a child talks to is not whether it is safe. It is whether it points back to a human when it matters.

Parents, which is it in your house: a place to practise, or a place to hide?

### First comment

Pew Research Center, "How teens use and view AI", 24 February 2026: 54 percent of US teens aged 13 to 17 have used chatbots for schoolwork help and 12 percent for emotional support or advice; 1,458 teens, Ipsos KnowledgePanel, fieldwork 25 September to 9 October 2025, margin of error plus or minus 3.3 points. US only. I have not seen an equivalent UK figure I would trust and I would welcome one. I wrote the two readings in the post as equals on purpose; if the practising reading is wrong I would like to hear why.
