## Post 1. The trial that taught children plenty and changed nothing at home

**Rests on:** F1, Jones, Mitchell and Beseler (2023), cluster randomised trial
of Be Internet Awesome.

### HOOKS

- OVERLOOKED FINDING: A randomised trial of Google's online safety lessons found children learned more. They did not talk to their parents more.
- REVERSAL: The best tested online safety lesson I know of worked. Just not on the thing I care about most.
- UNCOMFORTABLE QUESTION: What is an online safety lesson supposed to change?

**CHOSEN: the overlooked finding.** It names the study and the finding in the
first line, which is what worked on 11 November. The reversal hides the study
behind a tease, and the question gives a stranger nothing to stop for.

### The post

A randomised trial of Google's online safety lessons found children learned more. They did not talk to their parents more.

The programme is Be Internet Awesome. Researchers at the University of New Hampshire tested it with 1,072 children in 14 American schools, roughly our Years 5 to 7. Seven schools taught it and seven did not.

The children who had the lessons knew more of the new safety ideas, and felt more able to handle a problem online. That part worked.

The trial found no effect on privacy habits, on how civil children were online, on cyberbullying, or on talking with parents about a problem.

That last result is the one I keep coming back to.

When most of us picture an online safety lesson going well, we picture a child coming home and saying something. This trial suggests a decent lesson can raise what a child knows and leave that conversation exactly where it was.

The limits, plainly. One programme, one country, children around ten. A null result does not prove lessons can never reach home, only that this one did not show it. And the authors say no rigorous outcome test of these programmes existed before theirs, so this is close to a first look, not a verdict.

I have stopped asking whether online safety teaching works. The answer depends on what you count.

If you were judging an online safety lesson, what would you count as success?

### First comment

The trial: Jones, Mitchell and Beseler, Contemporary School Psychology, 2023. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40688-023-00465-5

The strongest objection is that talking to parents was never the programme's main aim. Fair. But almost every school letter about online safety promises it, so I think it is worth knowing when a lesson does not deliver it on its own.

## Post 2. What secondary schools are not given the time to teach about the feed

**Rests on:** F8, Molly Rose Foundation and University of Bristol, June 2026.

### HOOKS

- COMPARISON: Only 42 percent of secondary staff who teach social media say algorithms get good coverage. For bullying it is 86.
- PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE: A third of the staff teaching our teenagers about social media have had no training in it at all.
- MISSING VARIABLE: Everyone agrees teenagers should understand the feed. Hardly anyone has been given the hours to teach it.

**CHOSEN: the comparison.** Two numbers from one named survey in one
sentence, and the gap between them is the story. The training line is true
but reads as blame on teachers. The missing variable has no number and no
source.

### The post

Only 42 percent of secondary staff who teach social media say algorithms get good coverage in their school. For bullying it is 86 percent.

That is from a survey by the Molly Rose Foundation and the University of Bristol, published in June.

The gap is not about priorities. 92 percent of the same staff said it was very or extremely important that pupils understand how recommender algorithms work.

The rest of the survey shows why it is not happening. 32 percent felt confident teaching about algorithms, and 27 percent about AI on social media. A third had never had any training in social media literacy. The most common amount of scheduled lesson time was one to three hours a year, and it drops off from Year 10.

Now the caveat, and it is a big one. 133 staff who chose to take part were surveyed last winter, and the coverage figures come from the 90 or so of them who teach social media. The researchers say themselves it probably overestimates what happens nationally. So the real picture is likely thinner, not better.

I think it still points at the right gap.

A ban decides when a child can open an account. It cannot explain why the feed served them that video, or what to do when it keeps serving more. Someone has to teach that, and on these numbers most teachers have not been given the time or the training to do it.

I would rather a fifteen year old understood the feed than was kept away from it for one more year.

What would you take out of a secondary timetable to find those hours?

### First comment

The briefing: Molly Rose Foundation, How do secondary schools teach about social media, algorithmic recommendations and harmful content? June 2026, with Dr Lizzy Winstone at Bristol Medical School. https://mollyrosefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/MRF-Briefing_How-do-schools-teach-about-social-media-algorithms-and-harmful-content_Jun26.pdf

Worth saying: the Molly Rose Foundation campaigns for tougher regulation of platforms. We do not agree on everything. On this point, that teenagers need to be taught how the feed works, we do.

## Post 3. The ban arrives well over a year before the new curriculum

**Rests on:** F7, the government's full response, July 2026, with F6 for the
RSHE fact.

### HOOKS

- OVERLOOKED FINDING: The government's own ban document says schools are the best place to reach most children. Then it dates the new curriculum to September 2028.
- COMPARISON: The under 16 ban is due in spring 2027. The new curriculum is first taught in September 2028.
- REVERSAL: Read the government's ban response to the end and the ban is not the plan. The classroom is.

**CHOSEN: the overlooked finding.** It names the document, quotes it, and puts
the date gap in the second sentence. The comparison drops the quote that makes
it more than a timetable point. The reversal is the conclusion, not the
finding, and it frightens nobody into reading.

### The post

The government's own ban document says schools are "the best place to reach most children". Then it dates the new curriculum to September 2028.

That is the full response to the national consultation, published in July. Schools, it says, are "at the centre" of its media literacy plan.

The under 16 restrictions are expected in spring 2027. The new national curriculum, with media literacy across subjects and AI in computing, is first taught in September 2028.

By the government's own timetable, then, the ban arrives around a year and a half before the curriculum it says is central.

To be fair to it, that is not the whole picture. Revised RSHE guidance has been in force since this September, and it already asks secondary schools to teach about fake accounts, deepfakes and where to go for help. And dates in a policy document move.

But the document is telling us something about its own logic. The same response says parents "remain one of the most trusted influences" on children's online lives. Schools and parents. That is where it places the work.

The ban is a date. What a child is able to do when that date passes is a different job, and on the government's own timetable the main part of it comes last.

I would like those months to teach children more than how to wait.

If you were planning that year and a half for a ten year old, where would you start?

### First comment

The government response, July 2026: https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/growing-up-in-the-online-world-a-national-consultation/outcome/growing-up-in-the-online-world-government-response-july-2026

The RSHE guidance in force from 1 September 2026: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education

The strongest objection is that the ban and the curriculum do different jobs and need not arrive together. I agree they do different jobs. My point is that the response itself names schools as central, and then the central part comes last.

## Post 4. The home sheet nobody sends

**Rests on:** F2 (family tips in 4 percent of eighth grade lessons), F3 (parent
related programmes, very small effects, mode made no difference), F6 (RSHE
paragraph 56).

### HOOKS

- OVERLOOKED FINDING: An evaluation of free US online safety lessons found the family tips were used in 4 percent of eighth grade lessons.
- UNCOMFORTABLE QUESTION: Every online safety scheme says it involves parents. How many of the home sheets ever leave the classroom?
- PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE: If the home part of an online safety lesson is optional, it will lose to the bell.

**CHOSEN: the overlooked finding.** A named evaluation and a number in one
sentence. The question accuses schools before giving any evidence. The
consequence is my opinion, which belongs later in the post.

### The post

An evaluation of free US online safety lessons found the family tips were used in 4 percent of eighth grade lessons.

The University of New Hampshire published it last month: the Common Sense Media digital citizenship curriculum, 15 middle schools, nearly 4,000 students. The lessons raised knowledge in every year group. They came with family tips and family activities. In eighth grade, the tips were used in five lessons, the activities in two.

I do not read that as teachers failing. A sheet marked optional, at the end of a lesson of half an hour or so, loses to the bell.

Researchers will push back on what comes next, and they would be right to. The evidence that adding parents makes school programmes work better is thin. A 2022 meta analysis of eleven parent related cyberbullying programmes found very small effects, and the way parents were involved made no measurable difference.

So treat this as a hypothesis. Maybe the home part fails because it is built as an extra: a leaflet for the parent to read, rather than a job for the child to do.

England's RSHE guidance now suggests schools help parents "continue conversations started in class". In the lessons I have written, every one ends with a question the child takes home and teaches back to the adult. In Reception it is "Can you teach me the star pause?"

Whether that changes anything at home is untested. I would like it tested, properly, by someone who is not me.

Tell me where this falls down. What would you measure to know the conversation happened?

### First comment

The evaluation, October 2026 (teacher survey on page 39): https://www.unh.edu/ccrc/sites/default/files/media/2026-10/COMMON%20SENSE%20DIGITAL%20CITIZENSHIP%20EVAL%20FINAL%20REPORT%20FINAL.pdf

The meta analysis: Wang and Jiang, Trauma, Violence and Abuse, 2022. https://doi.org/10.1177/15248380221137065 An earlier review of 17 studies (Ng, Chua and Shorey, 2020) also found parental involvement did not change how well bullying programmes worked.

The 4 percent is eighth grade only and counts lessons, and the evaluation did not ask teachers why. It is a quasi experiment with a two month follow up, which the authors say limits it.

## Post 5. The one that names it

**Rests on:** F6 (RSHE paragraphs 55 and 56), F3 (the honest limit), F5 in the
first comment. Lead framing from the Oak call notes: PSHE and parent
engagement, and the staff time back. Oak is not named or quoted.

### HOOKS

- OVERLOOKED FINDING: England's RSHE guidance suggests schools help parents "continue conversations started in class". Very little I have seen is built for it.
- VULNERABLE: I have written 34 online safety lessons for schools. The part I am least sure of happens after the bell.
- PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE: Every RSHE lesson now comes with a duty to the parents. Someone in every school has to write that letter home.

**CHOSEN: the overlooked finding.** It opens on the named document and its
own words, the opening that has worked, and it earns the product mention by
starting with a duty every school now carries. The vulnerable hook leads with
the product. The consequence is true but sounds like a sales line.

### The post

England's RSHE guidance suggests schools help parents "continue conversations started in class". Very little I have seen is built for it.

The revised guidance came into force this September. It suggests schools show parents what is taught, and support them "in managing conversations with their children". In most schools that lands on a PSHE lead who already has more to do than time to do it.

My wife is a teacher, which is part of why I started building on the schools side at all.

So that is what Guided Childhood for schools is built around. 34 lessons on growing up online, Reception to Year 13. Every lesson ends with a parent note that goes home in the book bag the same day: what was taught, and one question for tea, usually the child teaching the adult.

The school does not have to write any of it. A parent pack gathers every one of those questions into one page it can print or share, with no login. Each note carries a code that opens the same passport page a family can follow at home. A class lesson marks it as met. Only the child's own check at home ticks it.

What I cannot tell you yet is whether it changes what happens at home. The research on involving parents in school programmes is mixed, and nothing I have built has been tested against it. Pilots come first, and I would rather report what they find than promise it now.

Parents, what is the last thing a school sent home that you actually read to the end?

### First comment

The schools site, with every lesson open to read and no login: schools.guidedchildhood.com

The RSHE guidance, paragraphs 55 and 56 on openness with parents: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/relationships-education-relationships-and-sex-education-rse-and-health-education

Why the lessons sit inside relationships education and not as standalone internet danger lessons: Finkelhor and colleagues (Trauma, Violence and Abuse, 2021) found online safety programmes often mismatch what the harm research shows, and argued for teaching it alongside the established work on bullying and relationships. https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838020916257
