## Rules you must follow

1. Use only the facts in the FACT SHEET below. Do not add any statistic, percentage, sample size, date, quote or study that is not in this pack. Do not round, combine or restate a number in a way that changes it. If a card needs a number that is not here, leave it out.

3. No dashes of any kind anywhere in the card text. No em dash, no en dash, no hyphen between words. Use a comma or a full stop instead.
4. Voice: warm, plain, direct, UK spelling. No stock marketing phrases or hype words; write it the way a parent would say it at the school gate. No "It is not X, it is Y" sentences. No lists of three adjectives.
5. Never frighten first. No warning signs, no red alerts, no worried child imagery. The reader should finish more capable, not more scared.
6. Never say any lesson, app or programme makes a child safe, and never claim social media causes mental illness.
7. No faces of children, no synthetic parent, no invented app screen, no logos of the platforms or of Google, Common Sense, the University of New Hampshire, Molly Rose Foundation or the government.
8. Only Card 5 may mention Guided Childhood. Cards 1 to 4 never name or picture the product.

## FACT SHEET (the only facts you may use)

**Card 1. Be Internet Awesome trial**
- A cluster randomised controlled trial of Google's Be Internet Awesome lessons.
- 1,072 children, US grades 4 to 6 (roughly Years 5 to 7 in England), 14 schools: 7 taught the lessons, 7 did not.
- Children who had the lessons knew more of the new online safety ideas and felt more able to handle problems online.
- No effect found on online privacy or civility, cyberbullying, or talking with parents about online problems.
- Source: Jones, Mitchell and Beseler, Contemporary School Psychology, 2023.
- Limitation: one programme, one country, children around ten; a null result is not proof lessons can never reach home.

**Card 2. How secondary schools teach about algorithms**
- Of secondary staff in England who teach about social media, 42 percent say algorithms and recommended content get good or comprehensive coverage. For bullying it is 86 percent.
- 92 percent say it is very or extremely important that pupils understand how recommender algorithms work.
- 32 percent feel confident teaching about algorithms; 27 percent about AI on social media.
- 33 percent have had no training in social media literacy.
- The most common scheduled time is 1 to 3 hours a year.
- Source: Molly Rose Foundation and University of Bristol, June 2026.
- Limitation: 133 staff who chose to take part (the coverage figures come from the roughly 90 who teach social media); the researchers say it likely overestimates practice nationally.

**Card 3. The ban and the curriculum timetable**
- The government's July 2026 response says schools are "the best place to reach most children" and are "at the centre" of its media literacy approach.
- Under 16 social media restrictions are expected in spring 2027.
- The new national curriculum is first taught from September 2028.
- Revised RSHE guidance has been in force since September 2026.
- The same response says parents "remain one of the most trusted influences" on children's online behaviour and wellbeing.
- Source: Growing up in the online world, government response, July 2026.
- Limitation: dates in a policy document can move.

**Card 4. The home sheet**
- An October 2026 University of New Hampshire evaluation of the Common Sense Media digital citizenship curriculum: 15 US middle schools, 3,908 students.
- In eighth grade, the family tips were used in 4 percent of lessons (5 lessons) and the family activities in 1 percent (2 lessons).
- A 2022 meta analysis of 11 parent related cyberbullying programmes found very small effects, and the way parents were involved made no measurable difference.
- England's RSHE guidance suggests schools help parents "continue conversations started in class".
- Sources: Jones and colleagues, University of New Hampshire, October 2026; Wang and Jiang, Trauma, Violence and Abuse, 2022; DfE RSHE guidance, July 2025.
- Limitation: the 4 percent is eighth grade only, counts lessons, and teachers were not asked why; the evaluation is quasi experimental with a two month follow up.

**Card 5. Guided Childhood for schools (the only product card)**
- 34 lessons on growing up online, Reception to Year 13.
- Every lesson ends with a parent note that goes home the same day: what was taught, and one question for tea, usually the child teaching the adult.
- A parent pack gathers every question into one page a school can print or share, with no login.
- Each note carries a code that opens the same passport page a family can follow at home. A class lesson marks it as met; only the child's own check at home ticks it.
- England's RSHE guidance suggests schools support parents "in managing conversations with their children" (an example step, not a requirement).
- Limitation to show: whether it changes what happens at home has not been tested yet. Pilots come first.
- Do not show any app screen. Type and simple shapes only.
