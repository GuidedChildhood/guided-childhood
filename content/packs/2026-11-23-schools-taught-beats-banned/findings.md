## F1. A randomised trial of Be Internet Awesome: knowledge up, talking to parents unchanged

- **Claim.** A school digital citizenship curriculum raised children's
  knowledge of new online safety ideas and their confidence handling online
  problems. It showed no effect on privacy or civility online, cyberbullying, or
  talking with parents about online problems.
- **Exact figures.** 1,072 children in US grades 4 to 6 (roughly Years 5 to 7
  in England), 14 schools, 7 taught the programme and 7 were controls. The
  abstract reports the direction and significance of effects, not a headline
  effect size, so no effect size is quoted in the posts.
- **Source.** Jones, L. M., Mitchell, K. J. and Beseler, C. L. (2023). The
  Impact of Youth Digital Citizenship Education: Insights from a Cluster
  Randomized Controlled Trial Outcome Evaluation of the Be Internet Awesome
  (BIA) Curriculum. Contemporary School Psychology. Published 5 April 2023.
  https://doi.org/10.1007/s40688-023-00465-5 (abstract read through the
  Crossref record of the DOI; the Springer page redirects to a login).
- **Design.** Cluster randomised controlled trial, multilevel models.
- **Limitation.** One programme, one country, children around ten. A null
  result is not proof the lessons cannot reach home, only that this trial did
  not detect it. The authors say no rigorous outcome evaluation of these
  programmes existed before theirs, so this is close to a first test.
- **Note.** Be Internet Awesome is Google's programme. That is public
  knowledge rather than a line in the abstract; drop the word "Google's" from
  the post if Justin would rather it rested on the abstract alone.
- **Nearest post.** GDC 062 (how 13 to 18 year olds protect themselves, talking
  to an adult) is about self reported coping by age. F1 is about whether a
  lesson changes behaviour. Different finding, related theme, so they should
  not run in the same week.
- **Verdict: USE.** Post 1.

## F2. Common Sense digital citizenship, evaluated: knowledge everywhere, behaviour in the older years, family tips barely used

- **Claim.** The first large outcome evaluation of the Common Sense Media
  Digital Citizenship Curriculum for US middle school found knowledge gains in
  all three year groups, protective behaviour changes in grades 7 and 8, and
  knowledge only in grade 6. Teachers rarely used the family materials.
- **Exact figures.** 15 middle schools (7 implementation, 8 control), 3,908
  students, baseline in fall 2024, follow up two months later. Lessons mostly
  ran 21 to 40 minutes. Among 8th grade lessons, 22 percent used any
  supplementary material, mostly Extend the Lesson; "Very small numbers used
  Family Tips (n = 5, 4%) or Family Activities (n = 2, 1%)." In grade 6 over a
  third of teachers used some supplementary material, Family Tips among the
  most common.
- **Source.** Jones, L. M., Colburn, D., Puchlopek Adams, A. and Beseler, C.
  (October 2026). A Quasi Experimental Evaluation of the Common Sense Media
  Digital Citizenship Curriculum. Crimes Against Children Research Center,
  University of New Hampshire. Executive summary, Section 5 (teacher surveys,
  page 39) and Section 6 (limitations, page 43).
  https://www.unh.edu/ccrc/sites/default/files/media/2026-10/COMMON%20SENSE%20DIGITAL%20CITIZENSHIP%20EVAL%20FINAL%20REPORT%20FINAL.pdf
- **Design.** Quasi experimental (schools not randomised), student self report
  survey, two month follow up, plus teacher surveys.
- **Limitation.** The authors name it: lower than planned enrolment and the
  quasi experimental design limited control for pre existing differences and
  reduced power, and the follow up was short. The 4 percent is the 8th grade
  figure only, counted in lessons, and the reasons teachers skipped the family
  materials were not asked.
- **Verdict: USE WITH CAVEAT.** Post 4. Say "eighth grade" and "lessons", never
  "teachers" in general.

## F3. Parent related cyberbullying programmes: very small effects, and the way parents were involved made no difference

- **Claim.** Programmes with a parent element reduce cyberbullying by a very
  small amount, and the mode of parental involvement does not moderate the
  effect.
- **Exact figures.** 11 studies. Standardised mean difference of 0.17 in the
  right direction for perpetration (95 percent CI 0.09 to 0.26) and 0.17 for
  victimisation (95 percent CI 0.10 to 0.24). Shorter programmes did better on
  victimisation; programmes with a theoretical basis did better.
- **Source.** Wang, L. and Jiang, S. (2022). Effectiveness of Parent Related
  Interventions on Cyberbullying Among Adolescents: A Systematic Review and
  Meta Analysis. Trauma, Violence and Abuse. Published online 2 December 2022.
  https://doi.org/10.1177/15248380221137065 (abstract read through Crossref).
- **Design.** Systematic review and random effects meta analysis.
- **Limitation.** Eleven studies is a small base, cyberbullying is one outcome
  among many, and the abstract was read, not the full text.
- **Verdict: USE WITH CAVEAT.** Post 4, as the honest objection to our own
  idea.

## F4. Educational anti bullying programmes: parental involvement did not moderate the effect

- **Claim.** Across 17 studies of educational interventions on bullying and
  cyberbullying, effects were very small to small, and the presence of parental
  involvement did not moderate effectiveness.
- **Exact figures.** 17 studies, 35,694 adolescents aged 10 to 18. SMD 0.30
  (traditional bullying perpetration), 0.16 (cyber perpetration), 0.18
  (traditional victimisation), 0.13 (cyber victimisation), all in the right
  direction. Cyberbullying programmes did better when delivered by technology
  savvy content experts than by teachers.
- **Source.** Ng, E. D., Chua, J. Y. X. and Shorey, S. (2020). The
  Effectiveness of Educational Interventions on Traditional Bullying and
  Cyberbullying Among Adolescents: A Systematic Review and Meta Analysis.
  Trauma, Violence and Abuse. https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838020933867
  (abstract read through Crossref).
- **Design.** Systematic review and meta analysis.
- **Limitation.** Published 2020, older than the brief prefers, and like F3 it
  measures bullying rather than online safety learning in general.
- **Verdict: USE WITH CAVEAT, background only.** Supports F3 in the first
  comment of Post 4. Not quoted in a post body.

## F5. Online safety teaching often mismatches what the harm research shows

- **Claim.** A review of 12 multi topic internet safety programmes against the
  research on six online harms found mismatches between programme messages and
  the dynamics of the harms, especially on sexual exploitation and sexting, and
  argued for folding online safety into established, evidence based programmes
  for the related offline harms (bullying, dating abuse, sexual abuse
  prevention).
- **Exact figures.** 12 programmes; review articles on cyberbullying (19),
  online sexual exploitation (23), sexting (19), fraud and hacking (6), suicide
  and self harm promotion (18), overuse (15).
- **Source.** Finkelhor, D., Walsh, K., Jones, L., Mitchell, K. and Collier,
  A. (2021, online April 2020). Youth Internet Safety Education: Aligning
  Programs With the Evidence Base. Trauma, Violence and Abuse.
  https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838020916257 (abstract read through Crossref).
- **Design.** Critical review, not a meta analysis.
- **Limitation.** A narrative review of programme content, so it says what is
  taught and what the research suggests, not what a new programme achieves.
- **Verdict: USE WITH CAVEAT, background.** Supports the first comment of Post
  5 (online safety taught inside relationships education). Not quoted in a
  body.

## F6. The 2025 RSHE statutory guidance (in force 1 September 2026)

- **Claim and exact wording.**
  - Principle (d), careful sequencing: pupils "supported and equipped with the
    knowledge to navigate different experiences in a positive way before they
    occur, and to prevent harms."
  - Principle (f): education "which aims to support and not to alarm pupils."
  - Paragraph 55: steps including "supporting parents in managing
    conversations with their children about RSHE topics."
  - Paragraph 56: schools should show parents a representative sample of
    resources, "enabling parents to continue conversations started in class."
  - Secondary online safety content includes fake accounts and posts made with
    AI, deepfakes, and where to go for advice and support.
  - On knife crime: "avoid using fear as an educational tool."
- **Source.** Department for Education (July 2025). Relationships Education,
  Relationships and Sex Education (RSE) and Health Education: statutory
  guidance. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687a3d473f4bde279ef4528c/RSHE_statutory_guidance_-July_2025.pdf
  (read in full as text).
- **Design.** Statutory guidance, not evidence of effectiveness.
- **Limitation.** Guidance says what schools should do. It says nothing about
  whether doing it works, so it is quoted as the duty, never as proof.
- **Verdict: USE.** Posts 3, 4 and 5.

## F7. The government's full response on the under 16 ban (July 2026)

- **Exact wording and figures.**
  - "The government agrees that schools are the best place to reach most
    children within the UK, so they are at the centre of our media literacy
    approach."
  - "parents and carers remain one of the most trusted influences on
    children's online behaviour and wellbeing."
  - Restrictions on under 16s expected "to enter into force in spring 2027."
  - "A new curriculum will follow from 2027, with first teaching from
    September 2028." Media literacy embedded across subjects, computing to
    cover AI.
  - More than 116,000 responses; "Nine in ten parents backed a ban."
  - 34 percent of children's consultation respondents opposed age
    restrictions, 47 percent wanted under 16 access to some platforms.
- **Source.** DSIT and DfE (July 2026). Growing up in the online world:
  government response.
  https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/growing-up-in-the-online-world-a-national-consultation/outcome/growing-up-in-the-online-world-government-response-july-2026
  (page text read directly).
- **Design.** Policy document and consultation summary. Consultation
  respondents are self selecting.
- **Limitation.** Dates in a policy document can move. The consultation figures
  are not a representative poll. GDC 067 already used the YouGov parent split,
  so the parent figure is not the point of any post here.
- **Verdict: USE.** Post 3.

## F8. How English secondary schools teach about algorithms (Molly Rose Foundation and University of Bristol)

- **Exact figures.** Of staff who teach about social media: 42 percent report
  good or comprehensive coverage of algorithms and recommended content (54
  percent minimal or basic), against bullying 86, relationships 77, self image
  and identity 73. AI on social media 46. 92 percent say it is very or extremely
  important pupils understand recommender algorithms. Confident teaching
  algorithms 32 percent, AI 27 percent. Training: 11 percent lots, 56 percent
  some, 33 percent none. Most commonly 1 to 3 hours of scheduled time a year
  (25 to 56 percent depending on year group), fewer than one in ten 10 hours
  or more, dropping from Year 10.
- **Source.** Molly Rose Foundation (June 2026). How do secondary schools teach
  about social media, algorithmic recommendations and harmful content?
  Research briefing, survey led by Dr Lizzy Winstone, Bristol Medical School.
  https://mollyrosefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/MRF-Briefing_How-do-schools-teach-about-social-media-algorithms-and-harmful-content_Jun26.pdf
  (read in full as text).
- **Design.** Exploratory survey, winter 2025, self selecting sample of 133
  staff.
- **Limitation.** The authors say the sample likely overestimates practice
  nationally. The hours question had 32 to 50 answers per year group. Molly
  Rose Foundation campaigns for tougher regulation, which, if anything, makes
  it a fair witness for a post arguing for teaching.
- **Nearest posts.** GDC 058 (the feed, not the clock) and GDC 063 (TikTok news
  from accounts not followed, slot 30 November). Both are about what the feed
  does. F8 is about whether schools teach it. Keep Post 2 at least two weeks
  after 063.
- **Verdict: USE WITH CAVEAT.** Post 2.

## Killed, and why

- **Ofcom, Children's Media Literacy 2025, online safety lessons** (92 percent
  of 8 to 17s recall at least one lesson, 29 percent regular lessons, 45
  percent "very useful"). Ofcom and its mirror returned 403 on every page, so
  the figures were seen only in Twinkl and Computing at School summaries.
  Killed until the PDF can be read.
- **Li, Zhang and Xie (2025), Enhancing digital literacy in children and
  adolescents, a meta analysis of school based interventions**, Information,
  Communication and Society, DOI 10.1080/1369118X.2025.2584142. The paper
  exists (Crossref), but the abstract returned 403 and Crossref holds none. The
  "10 studies, 6,250 children, SMD 0.16" in our August pack could not be
  checked against it. Killed. Note for whoever revisits the August pack: it
  calls 0.16 "moderate", which is small by any convention.
- **Fear appeals in online safety teaching.** No 2022 to 2026 peer reviewed
  evaluation found; the results were blogs, a 2014 content analysis and a 2015
  LSE blog. The anti fear line in this pack rests on the RSHE guidance (F6)
  instead, which is the duty, not an effect. Killed as a finding.
- **Finland and media literacy.** The usual evidence is a country ranking (the
  Open Society Institute Sofia index), which correlates national scores and
  cannot show that teaching caused them. Not opened. Killed on design.
- **NSPCC and NASUWT 2022, 46 percent of secondary teachers not confident
  teaching RSE.** Older than the brief and only seen in a summary. Killed.
- **BBC Teach and Censuswide 2025, 98 percent of primary teachers find online
  safety a challenge.** A commissioned poll seen only in a summary. Killed.
- **Huang, Espelage, Polanin and Hong (2019), anti bullying programmes with a
  parent component.** Crossref holds no abstract, so its effect sizes were not
  read. Killed; F3 and F4 cover the point.

## Independent citation check, 23 November 2026
A second agent re-read every primary source: 13 items, 8 confirmed, 5 corrected, 0 demoted. Corrections applied to the posts and the ChatGPT pack:
1. F8: 133 took part, but the coverage figures (42 and 86 percent) come from the roughly 87 to 91 staff who teach social media literacy (MRF briefing, figure 1 base).
2. F7: spring 2027 to September 2028 is 16 to 18 months, so the posts now say "around a year and a half" or "well over a year".
3. F2: the report says family tips were "used" in 4 percent (5) of eighth grade lessons, not that they "went home" (UNH report p39).
4. F6: para 55 lists "supporting parents in managing conversations" as a step a school "might" take, so the posts say "suggests", not "asks".
5. F6: para 56's "continue conversations started in class" is about showing parents a representative sample of resources, so "suggests schools help parents" is the fair wording.
F3 note: Wang and Jiang's effect sizes are negative (SMD -0.17), meaning reductions; the direction above is right.
