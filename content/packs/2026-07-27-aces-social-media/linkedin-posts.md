# Wounds of the Past — ACEs and social media. LinkedIn posts.

Built from Kovacic M and Orso C E, "Wounds of the past, screens of the present: how childhood adversities shape social media behaviours in adulthood," Review of Economics of the Household (2025), 23:1323 to 1369, DOI 10.1007/s11150-025-09796-z. Run through the European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC), the first EU wide, individual level survey of its kind across all 27 member states.

Four posts carrying the hidden thread with rigorous, credible data. Evidence discipline held throughout: the study is a large observational survey with mediation analysis, so it shows association and a plausible pathway, not a randomised causal proof, and it is about adults' use predicted by their childhood, which is stated plainly. No em dashes. LinkedIn length. One soft close on the final post, no hard CTA.

Key findings used (all from the paper):
- Adults whose close relatives had severe drinking or serious mental health problems in childhood are significantly more likely to spend over 2 hours a day on social media, use it more passively, and neglect work and family duties because of it.
- Loneliness mediates about 25 percent of the effect of childhood household mental illness on overuse, 30.4 percent on passive use, and up to 43 percent on neglecting work or family.
- Patterned: the childhood illness and mother links show for men but are not significantly different from zero for women; strongest for those aged 35 and under; alcohol in the family drives it in Nordic and Eastern Europe, closeness to a mother matters most in Southern Europe.

## POST 1 — The wound, not the screen [the plain thesis]
**Angle:** The vulnerability comes first. The screen is where it lands.

**Post text:**

A new EU study followed the thread almost everyone skips. It did not ask what social media does to you. It asked what you bring to it.

The study is Kovacic and Orso, published this year in the Review of Economics of the Household and run through the European Commission's Joint Research Centre. The first EU wide survey of its kind, individuals across all 27 member states, matching how people use social media as adults against what they lived through as children.

Here is what they found. Adults who grew up with a close relative who had a severe drinking problem or serious mental illness are significantly more likely to overuse social media now. More than two hours a day. More passive scrolling. More neglect of work and family because of it. The childhood wound did not go away. It found a new place to show up.

Sit with the direction of that. The story we are sold runs one way, the app harms the person. This is a large piece of evidence for the other way, the person arrives already carrying something, and the app is where it lands. The screen is not the wound. It is where the wound shows.

I have said this quietly for a long time, and been called a tech apologist for it. So let me say it plainly, with an EU Commission study behind me this time. The biggest drivers of harm are not in the phone. They are poverty, and what happens to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with too little help. Social media gets the blame because it is visible, and because it is rich. The real drivers sit in the dark.

None of this lets the platforms off the hook. It sharpens the question. If the harm concentrates in the people already hurt, then blanket bans miss them, and prevention has to start years earlier, at the wound, not the screen.

What did you carry into your own scrolling that had nothing to do with the app.

## POST 2 — Loneliness is the bridge
**Angle:** The mechanism in the middle. Connection is the protection.

**Post text:**

The same EU study found the thing in the middle, and it changes what you would actually do about it.

Kovacic and Orso did not stop at the link between a hard childhood and heavy social media use as an adult. They asked what carries it across all those years. The answer was loneliness.

Loneliness explained about a quarter of the effect of a childhood marked by family mental illness on later social media overuse. Up to 43 percent when it came to letting work and family slide because of it. In plain terms, the early adversity leaves an adult lonelier, and the lonely adult reaches for the endless feed.

Read that as a parent and it reframes the whole thing. If loneliness is the bridge from an early wound to a harmful habit, then the lever was never the app. It is the loneliness. A young person who is connected, seen, and not left alone with what they carry is a young person with far less need to disappear into a screen at midnight.

This is why I keep saying the same unfashionable thing. Connection is the protection. Not the parental control, not the ban, not the app blocker. The relationship. This study just put a number on it.

The honest, uncomfortable bit. You cannot install connection. It is slow, and human, and it asks far more of us than a setting does. But it is the thing that actually sits between a hard start and a harmful habit.

Who made you feel less alone as a teenager, and what did they actually do.

## POST 3 — Not how much, but who
**Angle:** The tail, not the average. Patterned vulnerability, so blanket rules miss.

**Post text:**

The EU study drew a line most of the debate refuses to draw. Not how much time, but how, and by whom.

The harm in Kovacic and Orso was not simply screen time. It was passive use, the silent scrolling, and it clustered in specific people. Adults who grew up with a relative's drinking or mental illness. Men more than women for the childhood illness link. Those aged 35 and under more than older cohorts. Even regional patterns, alcohol in the family driving it in Nordic and Eastern Europe, closeness to a mother mattering most in the south.

That patterning matters more than it looks. It means this is not a universal law of screens. It is a vulnerability, shaped by what a person carried and where they grew up. The average tells you almost nothing here. The tail tells you almost everything.

And that is the whole reason a blanket ban misses. A rule aimed at everyone lands hardest on the average child, who was mostly fine, and slips straight past the vulnerable one it was meant to protect, because their risk was never really the screen. It was the wound the screen found.

Aim at the average and you miss. Aim at the vulnerability, the loneliness, the child already carrying something, and you are finally pointed at the thing doing the damage.

If the risk is not spread evenly, why do we keep reaching for solutions that are.

## POST 4 — What you do with a finding like this
**Angle:** Prevention upstream. The ban misses the cause. Subtle build reference, soft close.

**Post text:**

So what do you do with a finding like this.

The Kovacic and Orso study opens on the Australian under 16 ban and the European rules that followed it. Then it spends forty careful pages quietly explaining why, on their own, they will not be enough. If the harm concentrates in the people already carrying a childhood wound, and travels through loneliness, then a rule that removes the app removes the symptom and leaves the cause exactly where it was.

I am not against holding platforms to account. I am against pretending a birthday or a ban is prevention. Real prevention starts years earlier and further upstream, with the things that are awkward to fund and impossible to photograph. Support for families under strain. A child who is not left alone with what they carry. Early, honest digital education, so that when a vulnerable young person does arrive online, they arrive prepared, not just blocked.

That is the whole reason I build what I build. Not to police the screen, but to strengthen the child and the family around it, so the wound has somewhere to go that is not a feed at midnight.

The evidence keeps pointing at the same place. Not the device. The person, and what we did or did not do to help them, long before they ever held it.

If prevention had to start ten years before the phone, what would you change first.
