# Wounds of the Past — ACEs and social media. LinkedIn posts (full length).

Built from Kovacic M and Orso C E, "Wounds of the past, screens of the present: how childhood adversities shape social media behaviours in adulthood," Review of Economics of the Household (2025), 23:1323 to 1369, DOI 10.1007/s11150-025-09796-z. The first EU wide, individual level survey of its kind, across all 27 member states, run through the European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC).

## POST 1 — The wound, not the screen [the plain thesis]

**Post text:**

A new EU study followed the thread almost everyone in this debate skips. It did not ask what social media does to you. It asked what you bring to it. And the answer should change how we talk about all of this.

The study is Kovacic and Orso, published this year in the Review of Economics of the Household and run through the European Commission's Joint Research Centre. It is the first survey of its kind, individuals across all 27 EU member states, matching how people actually use social media as adults against what they lived through as children. Not a headline. Not a small lab study. A serious, EU wide look at the whole chain.

Here is what they found. Adults who grew up with a close relative who had a severe drinking problem or a serious mental illness are significantly more likely to overuse social media now. More than two hours a day. More passive scrolling, the silent kind where you are consuming, not connecting. And more neglect of work and family because of it. The childhood wound did not heal and vanish. Years later, it found a new place to show up.

Sit with the direction of that arrow, because it runs the opposite way to the story we are sold. The headline version says the app harms the person. This is a large, careful piece of evidence for the other version. The person often arrives already carrying something, and the app is simply where it lands. The screen is not the wound. It is where the wound shows.

I have said this quietly for years, and been called a tech apologist for it. So let me say it plainly, with an EU Commission study behind me this time. The biggest drivers of harm are not inside the phone. They are poverty, and the things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health carried with far too little help. Social media takes the blame because it is visible, and because it is rich enough to make headlines. The real drivers sit in the dark, because they are old, expensive and awkward to film.

None of this lets the platforms off the hook. It sharpens the question we should be asking. If the harm concentrates in the people who were already hurt, then a blanket ban sails straight past them, and real prevention has to start years earlier, and much further upstream. At the wound. Not the screen.

What did you carry into your own scrolling that had nothing to do with the app.

## POST 2 — Loneliness is the bridge

**Post text:**

The same EU study found the thing sitting in the middle, the bit between a hard childhood and a harmful adult habit. And once you see it, it changes what you would actually do about any of this.

Kovacic and Orso did not stop at the link between growing up around a relative's drinking or mental illness and overusing social media decades later. They asked the harder question. What carries it across all those years? What is the bridge? Their answer was loneliness.

The numbers are precise, and they are not small. Loneliness explained about a quarter of the effect of a childhood marked by family mental illness on later social media overuse. Around 30 percent of its effect on passive, silent scrolling. And up to 43 percent of the effect on letting work and family slide because of it. In plain English, the early adversity leaves an adult lonelier, and the lonely adult reaches for the endless feed to fill a gap that was never made by a phone.

Read that as a parent and it quietly rearranges your priorities. If loneliness is the bridge from an old wound to a harmful habit, then the lever was never the app in the first place. It is the loneliness. A young person who is connected, who feels seen, who is not left alone with the heavy thing they are carrying, is a young person with far less need to disappear into a screen at midnight.

This is why I keep repeating the same unfashionable line. Connection is the protection. Not the parental control. Not the ban. Not the app blocker. The relationship. And for once it is not me asserting it. A European Commission study just put a number on it.

Here is the honest, uncomfortable part, because I will not pretend otherwise. You cannot install connection. There is no setting for it. It is slow, and human, and it asks far more of a tired parent than flicking on a control ever will. But it is the one thing that genuinely sits between a hard start in life and a harmful habit later. Almost everything else is downstream of it.

Who made you feel less alone as a teenager, and what did they actually do.

## POST 3 — Not how much, but who

**Post text:**

The EU study drew a line that most of this debate flatly refuses to draw. Not how much time on the screen, but how it is used, and by whom. And that second question is the one that quietly breaks the whole ban argument open.

The harm in Kovacic and Orso was not simply screen time, the number everyone loves to quote. It was passive use, the silent scroll where nothing is created and no one is really met. And crucially, it did not fall evenly across everyone. It clustered, tightly, in specific people. Adults who grew up with a relative's drinking or serious mental illness. Men more than women, at least for that childhood illness link. Those aged 35 and under far more than older cohorts. There were even regional patterns, alcohol in the family driving overuse across Nordic and Eastern Europe, while emotional closeness to a mother mattered most in the south.

That patterning matters far more than it first looks. It means this is not a universal law of screens that lands on every child the same way. It is a vulnerability, shaped by what a person carried and where they grew up. Which means the average, the figure that fills the news, tells you almost nothing about the real harm. The tail, the smaller group already hurt, tells you almost everything.

And that, right there, is the whole reason a blanket ban misses. A rule aimed at everyone lands hardest on the average child, who was mostly going to be fine, and slips straight past the vulnerable one it was written to protect, because that child's real risk was never the screen. It was the wound the screen found, and a ban does nothing to the wound.

Aim at the average and you miss the people who matter most. Aim at the vulnerability, the loneliness, the child already carrying something too heavy, and for the first time you are pointed at the thing actually doing the damage.

If the risk is not spread evenly, why do we keep reaching for solutions that are.

## POST 4 — What you do with a finding like this [soft close]

**Post text:**

So what do you actually do with a finding like this. Because it is easy to nod at a study and then change nothing.

The Kovacic and Orso paper opens, pointedly, on the Australian under 16 social media ban and the European rules that followed it. Then it spends forty careful pages quietly explaining why, on their own, they will not be enough. If the harm concentrates in the people already carrying a childhood wound, and travels across the years through loneliness, then a rule that simply removes the app removes the symptom and leaves the cause sitting exactly where it always was.

Let me be clear about where I stand, because this gets misread. I am not against holding platforms to account. Design regulation, algorithm transparency, real duties of care, all of it, yes. What I am against is pretending that a birthday or a ban is prevention. It is not. Real prevention starts years earlier and much further upstream, with the things that are awkward to fund and impossible to put in a photograph. Proper support for families under strain. A child who is not left alone with what they carry. And early, honest digital education, so that when a vulnerable young person does arrive online, and they all eventually do, they arrive prepared, not simply blocked.

That is the whole reason I build what I build. Not to police the screen, but to strengthen the child and the family standing around it, so that when an old wound goes looking for somewhere to land, it finds a person, and a plan, and a next step, instead of a feed at midnight.

The evidence keeps arriving at the same place, study after study. Not the device. The person. And what we did, or did not do, to help them, long before they ever held it.

If prevention had to begin ten years before the phone, what is the first thing you would change.
