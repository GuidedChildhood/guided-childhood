**Article text:**

The most useful thing we have built is not really a feature. It is a warning that
arrives a few weeks early.

In the last piece I argued that a phone meets three real needs, connection,
information and autonomy, so the honest job is not prohibition but preparation, and
that preparation has a timeline a ban can never give you. This is the thing we built
to give you that timeline.

Everyone in this space builds a screen time counter. A dashboard of hours that goes
red after the fact. I built the opposite, a calendar, and the insight it runs on is
almost embarrassingly simple. A child's birthday tells you their exact school year.
The England school calendar is fixed and identical for every family in the country.
Put those two facts together and you can tell a parent what is coming, and when,
without knowing a single private thing about their child.

Year 4 in April means the multiplication tables check is about six weeks out. Year 1
means the phonics screening is near. And Year 6 in October means the secondary
school application deadline falls at the end of the month, the one genuinely
immovable date in the whole of primary school, and the one I watch parents find out
about a week too late, every single year.

No test. No score. The product will never tell you whether your child is on track,
and it says so out loud, because I cannot know that without testing them, and the
moment you test a child they quietly stop telling you what they found hard. What we
have that nobody else does is not a judgement. It is the order, and the timing.

The single highest value moment the whole product has is the move from Year 6 into
Year 7. That is when the first phone usually arrives, and it is the exact readiness
moment the last piece was about. Here is the thing almost nobody gets right. The
same rules land completely differently depending on when you set them. Rules agreed
a fortnight before the phone are simply how it works. The identical rules bolted on
a fortnight after feel like a punishment, like something taken away. Same words.
Completely different child on the other side of them. Timing is not a detail. Timing
is the whole thing.

So the Year 6 bridge does one job. It taps you on the shoulder in the spring, while
there is still time to do the first phone well, and it hands you the order to do it
in, never a verdict on whether to buy it. It triggers on the school year, not the
age, because two children the same age, one in Year 6 and one in Year 7, are in
completely different situations, and only the year group can tell them apart.

There is a homework decoder built on the same principle. Paste in tonight's
homework and instead of a verdict you get the statutory line sitting behind it, what
that line is for, and one thing you could do this evening to help. It needs nothing
about your child, only their year group and the curriculum, which is exactly why it
can exist while a real on track score cannot.

I want to be honest about what this is and is not. It is not surveillance. It does
not watch your child. It watches the calendar, which is public, and it does the one
thing a busy parent cannot do for themselves, hold the whole map of the year in mind
and say, quietly, this is coming, get ready. It is anticipatory guidance, the thing
a good health visitor or a wise grandparent used to do, built into a product.

And notice, again, what is not at the centre of it. Not the AI. This is a calendar,
a transition card and a lookup. Plain, almost boring plumbing, built because the
research said the value was in the timing, and nobody was giving parents the timing.

The ban gives you a wall and a date. This gives you the one thing preparation
actually needs, which is warning. A few weeks, at the right moment, while it still
counts.

What is the next big moment coming down your child's school year, and would it help
to be told about it a month before it arrived rather than a week after?
