## FEED 1 (Research) — Autonomy builds self control

**Post text:**

The child with the strictest screen rules is often the worst at handling a screen.

It sounds wrong, but the research keeps landing there. In the short term, hard
limits work like a cast on a broken arm. The trouble is the muscle underneath. A
skill a child is never allowed to practise is a skill they never build.

A 2025 study in Cyberpsychology found the parents who raised the most capable
digital citizens, the teens who behaved well online unwatched, were not the
strictest. They paired structure with autonomy support, real say, real reasons,
real choices handed over as the child grew. The thing doing the work in the middle
was self regulation. The autonomy did not replace self control. It grew it.

To be clear, this is not no rules. Structure matters, boundaries matter, an
involved parent matters enormously. The point is subtler. The rules should be
scaffolding, built to be climbed and then outgrown, not a fence kept the same
height forever.

And notice what that does to the ban. A blanket ban is pure restriction, the cast
left on for a decade. It manages the moment and builds the muscle not at all, then
comes off on one birthday and we act surprised the arm cannot lift anything.

Autonomy support is harder. Locking an app takes ten seconds. Growing a child's
judgement takes ten years. But only one of those is still working the day your
child turns sixteen and closes the door.

So stop asking how do I control the screen, and start asking how do I grow the self
control, one stage at a time.

If the muscle only grows by being used, what is the first decision you could hand
your child that you currently make for them?

## FEED 2 (Build) — Five stages, not one birthday

**Post text:**

Everyone hands a child the whole internet on a single birthday. We refused to build
it that way.

If autonomy handed over in steps is what builds a child's self control, then a
product should hand it over in steps. So the core of what we built is not an app
that blocks things. It is a pathway from age four to sixteen, in five stages,
Foundation, Builder, Explorer, Shaper, Independent.

They are not age gates. A birthday does not unlock the next one. They are bands of
readiness, and each hands over a little more, a new skill, a new freedom, a new
conversation. By the time a real account is on the table, a child has been
practising the underlying judgement for a decade.

The part I am proudest of is the passport. It makes the growing visible. A child
earns stamps as they move through a stage, so the slow, invisible thing, the
growing of judgement, becomes something you can see and talk about. Progress you can
point to beats a limit you have to police. It even shows an amber warning if a
device is set up ahead of where the child actually is, because a phone handed over
two stages early is a skill demanded before it was taught.

Notice what is not at the centre. Not a screen time counter. Not a lock. And
deliberately, not the AI. The spine is the staged pathway, scaffolding built to be
climbed and outgrown, which is exactly what the research says a good structure
should be.

The ban delays the apps. This builds the judgement.

If readiness grew in visible stages, like a passport filling up, what is the one
skill you would want stamped before your child's first real account?

## FEED 3 (Research) — A phone is a need, not a toy

**Post text:**

Take a teenager's phone away and you have not confiscated a toy. You have removed
three things they genuinely need.

We treat phones like sweets, a treat you can withhold at no cost but a sulk. A 2025
paper in Pediatric Research says that is the wrong model. For a teenager a phone is
mainly how three real developmental needs get met. Connection, staying woven into
the friendships that are the whole emotional weather of adolescence. Information,
the questions they are too unsure to ask a person. And autonomy, the growing sense
of a life that is theirs. Those are not indulgences. They are the work of growing up.

Here is why it matters for the ban. If a phone were a toy, removing it costs a
tantrum. If it is the main channel for connection, information and autonomy,
removing it without replacing those channels does not remove the need. The need
goes looking somewhere else, usually somewhere worse, and somewhere you cannot see.

None of this means hand a nine year old a phone. Readiness is real, and the rough
milestone the research circles, around thirteen to fourteen, tracks the developing
prefrontal cortex. So the honest question was never whether, but when, and what you
did in the years before to get them ready.

That word before is where almost everyone loses it. We treat the phone as a thing
to delay, then the moment arrives and we have prepared nothing, because delay was
never preparation.

It is a preparation problem, not a prohibition problem. And preparation has a
timeline a ban can never give you.

Which of the three, connection, information or autonomy, are you helping your child
meet well right now, and which are you only delaying?

## FEED 4 (Build) — A warning that arrives early

**Post text:**

The most useful thing we built is not a feature. It is a warning that arrives a few
weeks early.

Everyone builds a screen time counter, a dashboard of hours that goes red after the
fact. I built a calendar. The insight is almost embarrassingly simple. A child's
birthday tells you their exact school year, and the England school calendar is
fixed and identical for every family. Put those together and you can tell a parent
what is coming, and when, without knowing one private thing about their child.

Year 4 in April means the tables check is six weeks out. Year 6 in October means
the secondary school deadline is at the end of the month, the one immovable date in
primary school, and the one I watch parents find out about a week too late.

No test. No score. It will never tell you if your child is on track, because
scoring a child just teaches them to stop telling you what they found hard. What we
have that nobody else does is not a judgement. It is the order, and the timing.

The highest value moment is Year 6 into Year 7, when the first phone usually
arrives. Rules agreed a fortnight before the phone are just how it works. The same
rules bolted on a fortnight after feel like a punishment. Same words, completely
different child, depending only on timing.

It is not surveillance. It watches the calendar, which is public, and does the one
thing a busy parent cannot, hold the whole year in mind and say, quietly, this is
coming, get ready.

The ban gives you a wall and a date. This gives you warning, at the right moment,
while it still counts.

What is the next big moment in your child's school year, and would it help to know a
month early rather than a week late?

## FEED 5 (Research) — The child who makes

**Post text:**

We spend all our worry on what a screen does to a child. Almost none on what a child
can do with a screen. The evidence says the second question is where a lot of the
good lives.

Not all screen time is the same thing, and treating it as one lump of hours hides
the most useful finding we have. A 2025 study in the Journal of Medical Internet
Research pulled apart what young people actually do on their phones and found that
active, connective use, posting, messaging, a group chat, making something, tracked
with lower depression, lower anxiety, less loneliness and more belonging than
passive scrolling. Same device, sometimes the same hours, very different outcome.

I want to be careful, the neat active good, passive bad line is contested, and
context and the child matter too. But even cautiously, a child creating is in a
different position from a child consuming. And learning is the plainest good of all.
A run of 2025 work found good learning apps at home lift early maths and literacy.
The National Academies has catalogued the real benefits of social media too,
support, connection, identity, especially for isolated young people.

If some uses help and some hurt, then counting hours is measuring the wrong thing.
An hour coding a game or keeping a friendship alive is not the hour spent scrolling
in silence, and a policy that treats them the same gets it wrong both ways.

So the job is not to guard the clock. It is to make the good uses easier to reach
than the passive ones.

What is one genuinely good thing your child does on a screen that you would like to
make easier to reach than the scroll?

## FEED 7 (Research) — The rules a child helps write

**Post text:**

The screen rules that actually work are usually not the ones you set. They are the
ones your child helped write.

We are all told to make a family media plan. So we find a template, fill it in,
stick it on the fridge, and within a fortnight it is invisible. The plan was fine.
The way we made it was the problem.

In December 2024, Pediatrics put a finger on why. The plans that work are not the
generic ones copied off a website. They are customised, and crucially co created
with the child. When a young person helps build the rule, understands the why, has a
real say, it gets followed. Not because they became compliant, but because the
mechanism changed. Ownership.

It is not mysterious, it is human. A rule done to you, you test and climb over. A
rule you helped write, you defend, because it is partly yours. Any of us who has
kept a promise we made and broken a rule we were given knows this.

And it sits on the thing I keep coming back to. Connection is the protection. The
safest child is not the one with the most locks, it is the one who will actually
tell you when something goes wrong rather than hide it.

Notice what this does to the ban. A national ban is the ultimate imposed rule, a
plan written by strangers for a child with no say and no relationship attached. It
is built the exact opposite way to the thing that works.

So stop setting the screen rules for your child. Start setting them with your child.
Same boundaries, very possibly. But the content matters far less than who held the
pen.

When did a rule in your house last stick, and had your child any hand in making it?

## FEED 9 (Research) — It was the pattern, not the hours

**Post text:**

After a decade of screen time studies, the honest answer is almost boring. It is not
the hours. It is the pattern, and the life around it.

A 2025 study in JAMA followed more than four thousand young people for four years.
The total hours on a screen did not predict the harm everyone fears. What did, and
strongly, was a shape of use, the compulsive kind, cannot put it down, anxious
without it, filling a gap. Two children, the same hours, completely different
outcomes, depending on the pattern underneath.

So what sets the pattern. Here is the part I usually only imply. The shape of a
child's screen use is downstream of the shape of a child's life. A child who is
connected, who sleeps, who has enough offline, tends to use a screen in the balanced
way. A child who is lonely or hurting reaches for it in the compulsive way, because
the feed is nearest when a need is not met elsewhere. The screen is often not the
wound. It is where the wound shows.

Said plainly, once. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health
are poverty, the things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental
health carried with too little support. Social media takes the blame because it is
visible and rich. The real drivers sit in the dark, old, expensive, awkward to put
in a headline.

One hard line. Sleep. The phone out of the bedroom at night, because sleep sits
upstream of almost everything. If you do one thing, do that.

The rest is not a rule you set, it is a balance you build. Enough sleep, enough real
life, enough connection, so the screen is one good thing among many.

What is the one thing missing from your child's offline week that the screen is
quietly filling instead?
