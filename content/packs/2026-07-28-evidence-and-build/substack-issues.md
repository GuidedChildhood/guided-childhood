## ISSUE 1

**Subject line:** Why the strictest screen homes raise the least prepared teenagers

**Preview text:** The counterintuitive research, and the pathway we built because of it.

The child with the strictest screen rules is often the worst at handling a screen.

It sounds wrong, so let me show the working. In the short term, hard limits work
like a cast on a broken arm. The trouble is the muscle underneath. A skill a child
is never allowed to practise is a skill they never build.

**The research.** A 2025 study in Cyberpsychology found the parents who raised the
most capable digital citizens, the teens who behaved well online without being
watched, were not the strictest. They paired structure with autonomy support, clear
expectations but also real say, real reasons, real choices handed over as the child
grew. The thing doing the work in the middle was self regulation. The autonomy did
not replace the child's self control, it grew it. This sits on decades of self
determination theory, which says the same in plainer words. People learn to govern
themselves by being given, in careful steps, something to govern.

Be careful here, because it gets misread. This is not no rules. Structure matters,
boundaries matter, an involved parent matters enormously. The point is subtler. The
rules should be scaffolding, built to be climbed and then outgrown, not a fence kept
the same height forever. And notice what that does to the ban. A blanket ban is the
purest possible restriction, the cast left on for a decade, then off on one birthday,
and we act surprised the arm cannot lift anything.

**What we built.** If autonomy handed over in steps is what builds self control, a
product should hand it over in steps. So the core of what we built is not an app
that blocks things. It is a pathway from age four to sixteen, in five stages,
Foundation, Builder, Explorer, Shaper, Independent. Not age gates, bands of
readiness. Each hands over a little more, a new skill, a new freedom, a new
conversation, so that by the time a real account is on the table a child has been
practising the underlying judgement for a decade.

The part I am proudest of is the passport. It makes the growing visible. A child
earns stamps as they move through a stage, so the slow, invisible thing becomes
something a family can see and talk about. It even shows an amber warning when a
device is set up ahead of where the child is, because a phone handed over two stages
early is a skill demanded before it was taught. Not a screen time counter, not a
lock, and deliberately not the AI. Scaffolding, built to be climbed and outgrown,
which is exactly what the research says a good structure should be.

The ban delays the apps. This builds the judgement.

If readiness grew in visible stages, what is the one skill you would want stamped
before your child's first real account?

## ISSUE 2

**Subject line:** A phone is not a toy. So a ban is not a plan.

**Preview text:** What a phone actually does for a teenager, and the tool we built around the timing.

Take a teenager's phone away and you have not confiscated a toy. You have removed
three things they genuinely need.

**The research.** A 2025 paper in Pediatric Research gives the balanced picture. For
a teenager a phone is mainly how three developmental needs get met. Connection, the
friendships that are the whole emotional weather of adolescence. Information, the
questions they are too unsure to ask a person. And autonomy, the growing sense of a
life that is theirs. Ask children directly, as a second 2025 study did, and they
name the benefits and the risks perfectly well. They live inside the trade.

Here is why it matters for the ban. If a phone were a toy, removing it costs a
tantrum. If it is the main channel for connection, information and autonomy,
removing it without replacing those channels does not remove the need. The need goes
looking somewhere else, usually worse, usually where you cannot see. None of which
means hand a nine year old a phone. Readiness is real, and the milestone the research
circles, around thirteen to fourteen, tracks the developing prefrontal cortex. So
the honest question was never whether, but when, and what you did in the years before
to get them ready. Delay was never preparation, though it felt like it.

**What we built.** Preparation has a timeline a ban can never give you, so we built
the timeline. Everyone builds a screen time counter that goes red after the fact. I
built a calendar. A child's birthday tells you their exact school year, the England
school calendar is fixed, and together they let you tell a parent what is coming, and
when, without knowing one private thing about their child. Year 6 in October means
the secondary school deadline is at the end of the month, the one immovable date in
primary school, and the one parents find out about a week too late.

The highest value moment is Year 6 into Year 7, when the first phone usually
arrives. Rules agreed a fortnight before the phone are just how it works. The same
rules bolted on a fortnight after feel like a punishment. Same words, different
child, depending only on timing. So the product taps you on the shoulder in the
spring, hands you the order to do it in, and never a verdict on whether to buy the
phone. It is not surveillance, it watches the public calendar and does the one thing
a busy parent cannot, holds the whole year in mind and says, quietly, this is
coming, get ready.

The ban gives you a wall and a date. This gives you warning, at the right moment.

Which of the three, connection, information or autonomy, are you helping your child
meet well right now, and which are you only delaying?

## ISSUE 3

**Subject line:** Stop counting screen time. Start counting what is on the screen.

**Preview text:** The benefit hiding in plain sight, and the tools we built to reach for it.

We spend all our worry on what a screen does to a child. Almost none on what a child
can do with a screen. The evidence says the second question is where a lot of the
good lives.

**The research.** Not all screen time is the same thing, and one lump of hours hides
the most useful finding we have. A 2025 study in the Journal of Medical Internet
Research found that active, connective use, posting, messaging, a group chat, making
something, tracked with lower depression, anxiety and loneliness and more belonging
than passive scrolling. Same device, sometimes the same hours, very different
outcome. The neat active good, passive bad line is contested, hold it honestly, but
even cautiously a child creating is in a different position from a child consuming.
And learning is the plainest good of all. A run of 2025 work found good learning apps
at home lift early maths and literacy, and the National Academies has catalogued the
real benefits of social media, support, connection, identity, especially for
isolated young people. If some uses help and some hurt, counting hours is measuring
the wrong thing.

**What we built.** So the job is not to guard the clock, it is to make the good uses
easier to reach than the passive ones. Paste your child's homework in, and our tool
refuses to tell you if it is right. On purpose. The homework decoder gives you the
statutory line behind tonight's homework, what it is for, and one thing to do this
evening, and never a verdict, because the goal is not to mark your child, it is to
hand you a way in. The lessons run as star lessons a parent sends on a link, an
interactive thing a child does, not a lecture. And the printables and craft packs
pull a child off the screen entirely and pay out inside the reward loop, so the
device sends them to the kitchen table and rewards them for staying there.

None of it is a lock, none of it counts hours, and none of it is the AI. Plain tools
that take the finding, what a child does matters more than how long, and make the
good kind the easy kind.

What is one genuinely good thing your child does on a screen that you would like to
make easier to reach than the scroll?

## ISSUE 4

**Subject line:** The screen rules that work are the ones your child writes

**Preview text:** Why co created rules stick, and the low tech thing we built around it.

The screen rules that actually work are usually not the ones you set. They are the
ones your child helped write.

**The research.** We are all told to make a family media plan, so we find a
template, fill it in, stick it on the fridge, and within a fortnight it is invisible.
In December 2024, Pediatrics put a finger on why. The plans that work are not the
generic ones copied off a website. They are customised, and crucially co created
with the child. When a young person helps build the rule, understands the why, has a
real say, it gets followed, because the mechanism changed. Ownership. It is not
mysterious, it is human. A rule done to you, you test and climb over. A rule you
helped write, you defend, because it is partly yours. And it sits on the thing I keep
coming back to. Connection is the protection. The safest child is not the one with
the most locks, it is the one who will tell you when something goes wrong rather than
hide it. A national ban, by contrast, is the ultimate imposed rule, written by
strangers for a child with no say, built the exact opposite way to the thing that
works.

**What we built.** So we built the most direct answer to that finding, and it is not
software at all. The family phone agreement. A deal, not a decree, five sections
calibrated to your child's stage. You build it together, and the child writes their
half of the promises in their own hand, because a promise a child writes in their own
words is defended by them, while a rule you typed is argued with. It is a ceremony,
not a lecture, so the night of the first phone feels like joining something, not
signing surrender papers. And it grows, reviewed every term, loosening where trust
has been kept, so it never becomes a stale relic on the fridge.

Almost aggressively low tech, built by a technology company, because the evidence
said the thing that works is ownership and a conversation, not another screen telling
a family what to do. The best parenting tool we make might be the one with no code in
it at all.

What is one screen rule you could hand your child the pen on this week?

## ISSUE 5

**Subject line:** After all the studies, the answer is boring

**Preview text:** It was never the hours. It was the pattern, and the life around the screen. Plus the whole of what I believe.

After a decade of screen time studies, the honest answer is almost boring. It is not
the hours. It is the pattern, and the life around it. And it is time I said the whole
of what I believe, plainly, once.

**The research.** A 2025 study in JAMA followed more than four thousand young people
for four years. The total hours on a screen did not predict the harm everyone fears.
What did, and strongly, was a shape of use, the compulsive kind, cannot put it down,
anxious without it, filling a gap. Two children, the same hours, completely different
outcomes, depending on the pattern underneath. Set it next to Przybylski's Goldilocks
finding, moderate use beating both zero and heavy, and the simple dose response the
panic assumes falls apart.

So what sets the pattern. The shape of a child's screen use is downstream of the
shape of a child's life. A child who is connected, who sleeps, who has enough
offline, uses a screen in the balanced way. A child who is lonely or hurting reaches
for it in the compulsive way, because the feed is nearest when a need is not met
elsewhere. The screen is often not the wound, it is where the wound shows. Said
plainly, once. The biggest drivers of the crisis in children's mental health are
poverty, the things that happen to a child at home, and a parent's own mental health
carried with too little support. Social media takes the blame because it is visible
and rich. The real drivers sit in the dark. One hard line, sleep, the phone out of
the bedroom at night, because sleep sits upstream of almost everything. If you do one
thing, do that.

**What we built.** The rest is not a rule you set, it is a balance you build, so we
built for the balance. The balance report, every week, counts the other side of the
ledger, the jobs, the printables, the time outside, set beside the screen time, and
you improve it by adding to the good side, not just subtracting from the bad. And the
scripts, more than a hundred, for the real moments no dashboard helps with at half
past six on a Tuesday, the device at the dinner table, the meltdown when it goes off.
Because balance is not built in a policy, it is built in a hundred small
conversations, and most of us do not have the words ready.

Plain, human, almost analogue tools, built by a technology company that decided the
answer to too much screen was not more screen, and not a cleverer lock, but a fuller
life and better words. That is the whole series, in one line. Not the device, and not
the ban. The pattern, and the life around it, from age four.

If that is how you want to raise your own, we made a free place to start, at
guidedchildhood.com/starter-pack. It begins, as all of it does, with one ordinary
conversation.

What is one thing in the offline column of your child's week that you could add, so
the screen has to earn its place beside it?
