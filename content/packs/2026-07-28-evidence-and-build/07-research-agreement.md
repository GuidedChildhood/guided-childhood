# Article 7 (Research) — The screen rules that work are the ones your child helped write

**Article text:**

The screen rules that actually work are usually not the ones you set. They are the
ones your child helped write. And the research on why is one of the most practical
things I have read as a parent.

We are all handed the same advice. Make a family media plan. Set clear rules. And
so we do, we find a template online, we fill it in, we stick it on the fridge, and
within a fortnight it is invisible, ignored, a monument to a Sunday when we tried.
The plan was fine. The way we made it was the problem.

In December 2024, Pediatrics published a piece on the family media plan that puts a
finger on exactly this. The plans that work are not the generic ones copied off a
website. They are the ones customised to the actual family, and crucially, co
created with the child. When a young person helps build the rule, understands the
why, has a real say in the shape of it, the rule behaves completely differently. It
gets followed. Not because the child suddenly became compliant, but because the
mechanism changed. Ownership.

Think about why that is, because it is not mysterious, it is human. A rule handed
down to you is something done to you, and the natural response to a rule done to you
is to test it, resent it, climb over it the second the back is turned. A rule you
helped write is something you are part of. You defend it, because it is partly yours.
Any of us who has ever kept a promise we made and broken a rule we were given knows
this in our bones. Children are not different. They are just more honest about it.

This lands right on top of a belief I keep coming back to. Connection is the
protection. The safest child is not the one with the most locks, it is the one with
the most trust, the one who will actually tell you when something goes wrong online
rather than hide it because telling you has only ever meant losing the device. A co
created agreement is connection made concrete. It is a rule and a relationship in the
same object. The imposed rule protects the moment. The shared rule protects the
relationship, and the relationship protects the child for years.

There is a harder truth folded into this, and I will not pretend otherwise. Co
creating is slower and more uncomfortable than imposing. It means sitting down,
actually listening to what your twelve year old thinks is fair, negotiating, letting
them win some of it, holding the line on the things that matter while genuinely
bending on the things that do not. It is far easier to just declare the rule and
walk off. But the declared rule is the one on the fridge that nobody follows. You can
have the easy version that does not work, or the hard version that does.

And notice what this does to the ban, one more time. A national ban is the ultimate
imposed rule. It is a media plan written by strangers, for a child who had no say,
delivered with no relationship attached. Everything the research says makes a rule
stick, ownership, understanding, a voice, a why, is precisely what a ban removes by
design. It is not just that a ban is a blunt instrument. It is that it is built the
exact opposite way to the thing that actually works.

So the practical shift is small and enormous at once. Stop setting the screen rules
for your child. Start setting them with your child. Same boundaries, very possibly,
you are still the parent, some things are not up for vote. But arrived at together,
written down together, and revisited together as they grow. The content of the rule
matters far less than who held the pen.

That is a lovely idea and a hard thing to actually do on a wet Tuesday with a tired
child. So we built something to make it easy, almost ceremonial, and impossible to
fudge. That is the next piece.

When did a rule in your house last stick, and had your child had any hand at all in
making it?
