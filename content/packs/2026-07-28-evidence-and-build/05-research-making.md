# Article 5 (Research) — The child who makes is not the child we worry about

Series: The evidence and the build. Pairs with Article 6 (the homework decoder,
lessons and printables). Image: a real photo of a child making something, or Justin
with the kids doing something creative. No dashes. Roughly 800 words.

**Hook:** We worry about what a screen does to a child. We forget what a child can do with a screen.

---

**Article text:**

We spend all our worry on what a screen does to a child. We spend almost none on
what a child can do with a screen. And the evidence says that second question is
where a lot of the good actually lives.

Start with a distinction most headlines flatten. Not all screen time is the same
thing, and treating it as one lump, one number of hours, hides the most useful
finding we have. A 2025 study in the Journal of Medical Internet Research pulled
apart what young people are actually doing on their phones and found that active,
connective use, posting, messaging, taking part in a group chat, making something,
tracked with lower depression, lower anxiety, less loneliness and more of a sense of
belonging than passive scrolling did. Same device. Same hours, even. Very different
outcome, depending on whether the child was making and connecting or just consuming.

I want to be careful, because this is where enthusiasm runs ahead of the evidence.
The neat active good, passive bad line is contested, and serious researchers argue
it is too simple, that context and the child matter as much as the mode. Hold it
honestly. But even at its most cautious, the research supports a claim that the
screen time panic keeps missing. A child creating is in a different position from a
child consuming, and the reason to reach for a device can be a genuinely good one.

Then there is the plainest good of all, which we somehow argue about anyway.
Learning. A run of 2025 work found that good learning apps used at home lift young
children's early maths and literacy and help prepare them for school. Not passive
babysitting apps, good ones, used well. The National Academies, hardly a cheerleader
for big tech, has catalogued the real benefits of social media too, information,
support, connection, identity, especially for young people who are isolated offline.
The upside is not a marketing fantasy. It is in the literature, sitting quietly next
to the risks, waiting for someone to hold both at once.

Here is why this is not just a feel good point. If some uses genuinely help and some
genuinely hurt, then the whole strategy of counting hours is measuring the wrong
thing. An hour a child spends coding a game, editing a video, doing maths practice
that actually lands, or keeping a real friendship alive, is not the same hour as one
spent scrolling in silence, and a policy that treats them identically will get the
answer wrong in both directions. It will restrict the good and wave through the bad,
because it never looked at what was on the screen, only how long.

And this reframes what a parent is actually for. If the mode matters more than the
minutes, then the job is not mainly to police the clock. It is to tilt the balance,
to make the good uses easier to reach and more rewarding than the passive ones, so
that when your child does pick up the device, the making, the learning and the
connecting are right there, more inviting than the endless feed.

That is a much more hopeful job than guarding a timer, and a much harder one,
because you cannot automate it. It asks you to know what your child is doing, not
just for how long, and to put good things in easy reach. But it is the job the
evidence actually describes.

None of this denies the harm. Passive, compulsive use is real, and for a child
already struggling a screen can clearly make things worse. The point is narrower and
more useful. We have spent a decade asking how much, when the better question, the
one the research keeps rewarding, is what kind, and can I make the good kind the
easy kind.

The child who makes things, who learns things, who reaches out to a friend, is not
the child we lie awake worrying about. So the interesting question becomes, how do
you build more of that child. That is what the next piece is about, because it is
exactly what we tried to build.

What is one genuinely good thing your child does on a screen that you would like to
make easier for them to reach than the scroll?
