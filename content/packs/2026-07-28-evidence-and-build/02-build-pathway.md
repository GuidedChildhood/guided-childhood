# Article 2 (Build) — We hand it over in five stages, not one birthday

**Article text:**

Everyone hands a child the whole internet on a single birthday. We refused to build
it that way.

In the last piece I made the research case that autonomy, handed over in steps,
builds the self control a child actually needs, and that pure restriction, however
well meant, trains the wrong thing. This is what we built once we believed it.

The core of Guided Childhood is not an app that blocks things. It is a pathway from
age four to sixteen, split into five stages, and the whole design does one job. It
hands a child a widening circle of digital autonomy in deliberate steps, years
before the apps arrive, so that readiness is grown rather than granted.

The five stages are Foundation, roughly four to seven, Builder, eight to ten,
Explorer, eleven to thirteen, Shaper, thirteen to fifteen, and Independent, sixteen
and up. They are not age gates. A birthday does not unlock the next one. They are
bands of readiness, and each one hands over a little more, a new skill practised, a
new freedom earned, a new conversation that a four year old is not ready for and a
fourteen year old badly needs. By the time a child reaches the stage where a real
account is on the table, they have been quietly practising the underlying judgement
for a decade.

The part I am proudest of is the passport. Every family has one, and it makes the
growth visible. As a child moves through a stage they earn stamps, small marks of a
thing understood or a habit built, and the passport shows both what has been done
and what this stage is really about. It turns an invisible, slow process, the
growing of judgement, into something a parent and a child can actually see and talk
about. Progress you can point to beats a limit you have to police, every time.

And it is honest about time. The passport shows an amber warning if a device has
been set up ahead of where the child actually is, because a phone handed over two
stages early is not a treat, it is a skill demanded before it has been taught. The
product will tell you that to your face rather than sell you the upgrade.

Here is the design principle underneath all of it, and it comes straight out of the
research in the last piece. We are not trying to keep the circle small. We are trying
to widen it at the right speed. Too fast and the child is handed decisions they
cannot yet make. Too slow and you get the cast left on too long, the sixteen year
old with no practice. The whole product is really one long, careful answer to a
single question. What is the next right thing to hand over, and is now the time.

Notice what is not at the centre of this. Not a screen time counter. Not a lock. And
deliberately, not the AI. People assume our product is the chatbot, and the guide is
one part of it, but the spine is this staged pathway, the passport, the stamps, the
readiness bands. It is scaffolding. It is built to be climbed and then, stage by
stage, outgrown, which is exactly what the autonomy research says a good structure
should be.

I did not build this because it was clever. I built it because the alternative, the
thing everyone else builds, felt like a trap. Hold everything back, hold everything
back, hold everything back, then hand it all over at once and hope. A pathway does
the opposite. It spends ten years making the handover small, and boring, and
practised, so that the big moment, when it comes, is not a cliff. It is just the next
step on a road the child has been walking the whole time.

The ban delays the apps. This builds the judgement. That is the whole difference, and
it is the reason a staged pathway, not a stricter lock, is what I think this job
actually needs.

If your child's readiness grew in visible stages, like a passport filling up, what
is the one skill you would want stamped before their first real account?
