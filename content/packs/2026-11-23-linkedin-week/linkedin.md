## GDC-060 · Europe's draft law would let a 13 year old have a social media account, capped at one hour a day. Britain says 16.

**Job: recruit strangers.** Hook 97 characters, body 2386.

### The post

Europe's draft law would let a 13 year old have a social media account, capped at one hour a day.

Britain's plan for the same child is no account until 16.

Same worry. Two very different answers.

The European Commission put forward its proposal, the EU KIDS Act, on 17 September. It reads like a staircase. Under 13, no account. At 13 and 14, a service can let a parent set up a limited one, and the parent stays in control, with parental tools always on, contacts they can approve first and a daily limit of no more than an hour. From 15, a young person can open their own.

It goes after design as well as age. For under 18s, services couldn't use infinite scroll or endless autoplay without real breaks, streaks that penalise a child for missing a day, or notifications built to pull them back. AI companions couldn't simulate a human relationship in ways likely to create emotional dependency.

The UK went the other way in June. Social media banned for under 16s, expected in spring 2027 once the rules have been through Parliament.

I should say what the proposal doesn't show.

Nobody has tested whether 13, 15 or one hour are the right numbers. The proposal gives its reasons: 13 follows a developmental approach recommended by the Commission's expert panel, and 15 is one age to replace national limits that run from 13 to 16. Those are reasons. They aren't results. Parliament and the Council can change every line of it, and no child has lived under it yet, so I can't tell you the staircase beats the wall.

What I can tell you is that both leave the same gap.

A law decides when a child gets in. It has nothing to say about what they can do once they're there. A 14 year old with an hour a day still needs to know why the feed chose that video, what to do when a group chat turns, and when something has to come back to an adult. So does a 16 year old on the first morning after the ban stops applying to them.

That's the part I work on. Our pathway is staged by what a child can show they can do, because a birthday teaches nobody anything.

The Commission is still taking feedback from parents, teachers and children. The details are in the comments.

If you know a head or a parent who'd have a view, repost this. A consultation is only as good as the people who answer it.

If your child's access came in steps, what would you want them able to do before each one?

### First comment, post immediately

The Commission's own explainer of the EU KIDS Act:

https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained

Feedback is open to children, parents, guardians, teachers and educators until 3 December 2026, midnight Brussels time. One Commission page still says 26 November, but the consultation itself shows 3 December.

The strongest objection is that a UK parent has no stake in an EU proposal. Fair. I'm using it as a comparison. Two governments looked at the same worry and one drew a staircase while the other drew a wall. It's a proposal and may change, and under it a service may offer the supervised account at 13 and 14 but doesn't have to. The staircase and wall framing is mine.

## GDC-067 · 77 percent of parents back the ban on social media for under 16s. They split 45 to 46 on whether it will work.

**Job: start an argument.** Hook 129 characters, body 2042.

### The post

77 percent of parents told YouGov they back a ban on social media for under 16s, and they split 45 to 46 on whether it will work.

Three in four want it. Fewer than half expect it to do the job.

YouGov published the figures on 15 June, the day the government confirmed the ban. Among British parents with a child under 18, 77 percent were in support and 14 percent against. Asked whether it would be effective, 45 percent said yes and 46 percent said no.

I think those parents are being realistic. You can want a rule and still know your own child, and your child's friends, and the older cousin with a spare phone.

The limits first.

A poll measures what people expect, and expecting something to fail is no evidence that it will. The parents were asked a few days before the announcement, so nobody was reacting to the detail. YouGov hasn't published how the two answers overlap, which means I can't tell you how many parents hold both views at once, only that on those numbers it's unlikely to be a handful. And the support comes from somewhere real: around four in ten parents in the same research said social media had affected their own children badly.

Put the two numbers side by side, though, and I think you can see who is answering. A parent who wants the law and expects to be doing the work anyway.

The government says the protections should take effect in spring 2027. For a ten year old that leaves six years on the far side of the rule, and one birthday at the end of it when the rule stops.

Nothing in the law says what happens in those six years. That was never its job.

It's the bit I spend my days on. Ten minutes a day, parent and child together, working through what a child should be able to do before the account arrives, with the words for the arguments along the way.

The poll is in the comments.

If this is the conversation at your school gate, repost it. I'd like to hear from parents on both sides of that split.

If you back the ban and doubt it will work, what are you planning to do between now and 16?

### First comment, post immediately

The YouGov article, 15 June 2026:

https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/54969-eight-in-ten-parents-say-social-media-use-has-a-negative-impact-on-children

Fieldwork was 9 to 11 June 2026 with adults in Great Britain, including about 840 parents of under 18s. YouGov's article gives the 77 percent and the 45 to 46 split but its published tables don't show those two questions, so I can't give you the exact wording or sample for them.

The strongest objection is that doubting a law is a reason to enforce it better. I agree. Nothing here argues against the ban. My point is smaller: the parents who support it are already telling us they expect to carry part of the load themselves.
