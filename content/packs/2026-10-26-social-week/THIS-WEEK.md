**Instagram caption**

Paper, felt tips and a tablet that is shut.

For a long time that was our answer. Justin was sure screens were mostly bad for children, and that paper and pens were the safer way to learn.

Then our children started learning things from screens. A language. A craft. Some science. School moved onto them too.

The other half was just as real. Switching a screen off could start an argument. A tablet could make the afternoon easier and build a habit that was hard to undo.

So the question changed. Not whether screens are good or bad, but what this one is for, and how a child learns to use it well.

The paper and pens are still on the table. They just have company.

Did you ever hold a firm view about screens that real life changed?

**Instagram caption**

Send this to the parent who has just found out their child talks to an AI chatbot.

Fair point first: using a chatbot is not the worry. In a UK survey of 1,000 children aged 9 to 17, many used one for schoolwork and revision, and that is ordinary.

The part worth a gentle question is how it feels. Of the children who use one, 35% said talking to it is like talking to a friend. Among the children the study counted as vulnerable, it was about half.

That is what children said about how it feels. It is a survey, so it cannot tell us whether a chatbot caused anything, or whether it is helping or hurting any one child. Feeling like a friend is not proof of harm.

What it does tell us is why a child might lean on one. It is always there, it is warm and it agrees. That is easy to reach for when a child is lonely or having a hard time. So the useful move is not to take it away. It is to make sure a person is easier to reach.

Tonight: ask your child to show you the best thing it has helped them with. Then ask who they tell when something is really hard. Listen first.

**Instagram caption**

Send this to the parent who thinks the ban will sort it out.

Fair point first: for some families it will be a relief. A rule that applies to every child takes the "everyone else has it" fight away, and that is a real thing.

The government has said it will ban under 16s from a named list of social media apps, due in spring 2027. Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, YouTube, Facebook and X have been named. Messaging apps such as WhatsApp are left out, and the final list is still being set.

Which means the group chat, the game and the late night watching are all still there. A ban on named apps does not teach a child to use any of them well. That is the start line, not the finish.

So here are the words, before it starts:

"The apps are going. That does not fix what is underneath them. I am not here to police it. I want us to work out what your online life looks like now."

Not: "Good, finally." A parent who sounds relieved is a parent a teenager finds hard to tell about a workaround.

And one more, for the day it comes up: "If you ever find a way round it, I will not explode. I want to understand what you were looking for."

What would you most want your child to tell you, if they found a way round it?
