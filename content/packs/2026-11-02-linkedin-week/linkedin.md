# GDC-050 · Four ways to prove you are 16

**Job: recruit strangers.** Hook 126 characters. Proof path: ban neutral stance, `social_media_law` flag (docs/11), the stage check.

Ofcom has found four ways to check that a UK user is 16, and says the risk of children getting round them cannot be entirely eliminated.

It published the assessment on 29 October, while Parliament works out how the under 16 ban will run in practice.

The four are photo ID matching, digital ID services, banking based checks and facial age estimation. Ofcom calls the list preliminary.

Then the numbers that decide who actually gets through. An estimated 94 percent of UK residents aged 16 and over hold a passport or driving licence. Around 150,000 sixteen and seventeen year olds have neither. About 90 percent of sixteen and seventeen year olds hold a bank account. And some face scanning tools have a margin of error of a few years at 16, so Ofcom suggests a second check for anyone close to the line.

To be fair, none of this says the ban cannot work. A ban with real checks is a better ban than one without them, and this is a serious piece of work.

But look at what a check does. It decides who gets through the door. It does not decide what a fifteen year old does with a friend's login, or what they feel when something odd lands in their feed.

Ofcom says it will publish research on circumvention within twelve months of the ban coming into effect. Until then, what a child understands about why the rule exists is doing a lot of the work.

That is the part I build, so I am not neutral. I would still say the same thing without it: a deadline is not a plan.

This is the ban series continued.

If your child is twelve now, what do you want them to understand by the day the checks start?

# GDC-051 · What Australian children do with AI

**Job: build authority.** Hook 121 characters. Proof path: lesson `ks1-33-who-is-on-the-other-side`; DiGi and the child side never chat (CI guard).

Australia's online safety regulator asked 1,950 children aged 10 to 17 about AI, and 78 percent had used an AI assistant.

Only 8 percent had used an AI companion.

The survey is eSafety's "Talking to machines", fielded online between 2 February and 4 March 2026. It is Australian children, not British ones, and I would not carry the percentages across.

Here is the number I keep returning to. Of the children who had used an assistant or a companion, 54 percent said they had used it for personal or social reasons. One in five of those users were on it daily or more.

Now the honest part. "Personal or social" is a wide bucket. It could be a chat about a game as easily as a worry, and I have not seen the exact question the children were asked. These are children's own reports, a single country, a single month.

Even so, the picture is not the one the headlines draw. Most children are not on a companion app. They are on the ordinary assistant, the one parents think of as homework help, and more than half of the users have taken it somewhere personal.

So a rule about companion apps misses the tool most children actually use.

What does carry across any country is the habit of asking who is on the other side, and knowing when to go and find a person. We teach a short lesson on exactly that for younger children, and it starts from the idea that the child should be able to explain it to you.

This is the children and AI series continued.

When your child is stuck or worried, who do you think they ask first?

# GDC-052 · A face scan to get into an app

**Job: start an argument.** Hook 135 characters. Proof path: ban neutral stance (`social_media_law`), the positive pathway doctrine in THE-STORY.md section 3.

A ban on social media under 16 needs a way to know who is 16, and Ofcom's shortlist is a passport, digital ID, bank check or face scan.

I am not sure parents have been asked what they think of that.

Take each in turn. A passport or driving licence works for most people, but Ofcom's own estimate is that around 150,000 sixteen and seventeen year olds have neither. A bank check covers about 90 percent of that age group, which leaves around one in ten outside it. A face scan can be wrong by a few years at 16. And a digital ID means doing the photo ID step once, then reusing it.

Each one fixes a problem and hands over something else. A child's face, a child's bank details, or a new ID that sits behind every app.

The people who want the ban have a fair answer. Checks are the price of a rule that means something, and the alternative is the system we have now, which is a box a child ticks.

I can hold both of those at once. What I cannot tell you is where the line is for your child, and neither can Ofcom. Its list is preliminary, and it says it will consult on draft guidance in the coming months.

So the live question is not only whether there should be a ban. It is what you would be willing to hand over to make it work.

This is the ban series continued.

Would you let your child use a face scan or a bank check to get into an app, and what would you want promised in return?
