# Module 4: The highlight reel

**13+ Social Media Literacy curriculum. Built in all three versions, following the Module 1 template.** British English, no dashes anywhere. Extends the existing 092 parent lesson "Real life is not a highlight reel" by going deeper.

**The big idea:** a feed shows you other people's edited peaks, and it hides everyone's ordinary behind the scenes. Comparing your whole self to their best moment is a rigged contest, and it is one you can opt out of.

**Learning outcome (one line, all versions):** I can explain why comparing myself to a feed is unfair to me, and one thing I do about it.

**Mental health link, held calmly:** social comparison is one of the core mechanisms that links heavy use to lower mood, anxiety and loneliness. As fear of missing out rises, how good you feel about your own life tends to fall. Tying your worth to likes and follows is a real vulnerability, because those numbers are set by a machine that profits when you keep checking. None of this means you are shallow or weak. It means the contest is unfair, and seeing that is the first move out of it.

**Evidence anchors:** social comparison as a mechanism linking use to lower wellbeing (Child Mind Institute on comparison and teen mental health). Fear of missing out linked to lower subjective wellbeing in adolescents (a FOMO and adolescent wellbeing source, keep any figure attributed). Framing per Odgers (preparation over fear, effects are small on average and causation is unproven), Livingstone 4 Cs (this module sits mostly in Content and Conduct), Przybylski (the Goldilocks amount, moderate use beats zero and heavy). Pair any learner facing usage claim with a UK anchor (Ofcom Children and Parents 2025).

**Cast:** first taught at 13 to 14, so KS3, host Orbit. DiGi is the through line guide.

# VERSION A. SCHOOLS IN DEPTH

**Stage:** KS3, Years 8 to 9, ages 13 to 14. **Length:** one lesson, about 55 minutes. **Format:** seven beat Rosenshine.

**Statutory and framework mapping:**
- RSHE (statutory): the impact of viewing harmful or unrealistic content online, how information and material online can be exaggerated or curated, the effect of comparison and self image on mental wellbeing, and where and how to seek support. Aligns to the 2026 guidance emphasis on curated and unrealistic online content.
- UKCIS Education for a Connected World strands: Self image and identity, Health wellbeing and lifestyle, Managing online information.
- PSHE Association: mental health and emotional wellbeing, media and digital literacy.
- Computing national curriculum KS3: using technology safely, respectfully and responsibly.

**Ground rules (set before teaching, every lesson in this curriculum):** we talk about the feeds and the scenarios, not about each other. No personal disclosures, and no naming anyone's account. Questions are always welcome. If anything here worries you about yourself or a friend, the end of the lesson shows exactly where to go.

**Baseline check (Rosenshine, start where they are):** one question on entry, "when you scroll and everyone seems to be having a better time than you, what is the feed actually showing you?" Collect three answers on the board, return to them at the end.

### Beat 1 · Recall and Do Now (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Last time we saw the feed is built to hold your attention, not to look after you. Quick recall on your whiteboards: give me one design trick that keeps you scrolling. Now hold that thought, because today is about what you are scrolling past, and why it can quietly make you feel worse without you noticing.
**On screen.** The lesson title and the entry question, what is the feed actually showing you.
**Purpose.** Retrieval, activates prior schema, links to Module 1.

### Beat 2 · New input in small steps (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Four ideas today, one at a time. One, the highlight reel. What you post is your best five seconds out of a whole day. So is everyone else's. The feed is a wall of everybody's best five seconds, stitched together, and never the boring bits in between. Two, lifestyle envy. Because you only see the peaks, other lives look shinier than yours, and yours looks flat by comparison, even though you are comparing your everything to their edited something. Three, fear of missing out, FOMO. That tug when you see a party or a trip you were not part of, the feeling that life is happening somewhere else without you. Research finds that as FOMO goes up, how good young people feel about their own lives tends to go down. Four, likes and follows as worth. The app turns you into a number and invites you to feel that the number is you. It is not. It is a score set by a machine that makes money when you keep checking it.
**On screen.** Four labelled cards revealed one at a time: The highlight reel, Lifestyle envy, FOMO, Likes as worth.
**Purpose.** Small steps, one idea per card, respects working memory.

### Beat 3 · Worked example, model it aloud (8 min)
**Teacher script.** Watch me pull one post apart. Here is a made up holiday photo, someone on a perfect beach, caption "best day ever". My first thought is the honest one, their life is better than mine. Now I slow it down. I am seeing one frame. I am not seeing the eight hour flight, the argument in the car, the ninety photos they deleted to get this one, or the ordinary Tuesday they had last week that looked exactly like mine. I compared my whole messy day to their single best frame, and I lost a contest that was never fair. Naming that, out loud, as it happens, is the skill. That is the highlight reel. My life is not worse. I am just seeing their edit and living my uncut version.
**On screen.** A single polished post, then the hidden behind the scenes revealed around it, the deleted takes, the ordinary days.
**Purpose.** Worked example, models the reframe before pupils try it.

### Beat 4 · Check for understanding, hinge question (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Hinge question, hold up A, B, C or D. You feel low after scrolling through a friend's amazing weekend. What is the unfair part of the comparison? A you did fewer fun things than them, B you compared your ordinary week to their edited highlights, C your weekend was actually bad, D you do not have enough followers. Show me. If most say B, we move on. If not, we look again.
**On screen.** The multiple choice hinge.
**Purpose.** Whole class check, do not proceed below about 80 percent.

### Beat 5 · Guided practice, we do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Together now. In pairs you have three scenario cards. One, someone posts every good moment and privately feels their real life is dull. Two, a person keeps refreshing to see if a post got enough likes and feels flat when it does not. Three, someone sees a group hang out they were not invited to and spirals. For each, name what is really going on, highlight reel, likes as worth, or FOMO, and name one move that takes control back. I will take the first with you, then you do two and three.
**On screen.** The three scenario cards and a two column frame, what is really going on, my move.
**Purpose.** Guided practice with high success, scaffolded.

### Beat 6 · Independent practice, you do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** On your own, and this stays yours. Think of a recent scroll that left you feeling a bit worse. No names, no detail I would collect. Write down which of the four was at work, the highlight reel, lifestyle envy, FOMO or likes as worth. Then write one honest reframe you could say to yourself next time, and one small move, unfollowing accounts that make you feel small, following ones that show real life, muting during revision, or deciding likes are not the score of your day. I am collecting the analysis, not the personal detail.
**On screen.** The independent task and a reminder of the four ideas.
**Purpose.** Independent application, the skill transferred to their own life.

### Beat 7 · Review, consolidate, signpost (7 min)
**Teacher script.** Back to the board. Our entry answers on what the feed shows you, which one holds up now? The feed shows everyone's edited peaks and hides the ordinary bits. Say the headline back to me, I am comparing my whole self to somebody's highlight reel, and that contest is not fair, so I can step out of it. One honest note before we finish. Social media is not all bad here, staying in touch and finding people like you is real and good, the trouble is only the comparing. So the aim is not to quit, it is to scroll with your eyes open. If any of this made you think about your own mood or a friend's, you can always talk to a trusted adult here, or Childline on 0800 1111. Next lesson, Nova takes us into bodies and filters.
**On screen.** The one line summary, a moderation note, a forward hook to Module 5, and the support signpost.
**Purpose.** Spaced review, Goldilocks stretch, links forward, help routes named.

**Endpoint assessment:** compare entry and exit answers to the "what is the feed showing you" question, plus the independent Beat 6 analysis, against the outcome, can the pupil explain why feed comparison is unfair and name one countermove. **Differentiation:** support, the four cards stay visible and pairs get sentence starters ("what I saw... what I did not see... so my move is..."); stretch, ask why moderate, eyes open use can still be healthy (Przybylski Goldilocks) and why the answer is curating a feed rather than quitting it, so the lesson never tips into abstinence messaging. **DSL note:** not a formally sensitive module, but comparison and low mood sit close to the surface here. If a pupil's independent work discloses persistent low mood, self worth tied hard to likes, or distress, follow the school's normal pastoral route.

# VERSION B. KID (interactive lesson)

**Stage:** KS3, ages 13 to 14. **Host:** Orbit. **Format:** the seven beat explainer, about 4 minutes, then one interactive choice. Non judgemental, agency first, the young person is the decision maker.

### Beat 1 · Hook
**Narration.** You are scrolling, and it hits you. Everyone is having a better time than you. Better weekend, better mates, better everything. You put the phone down feeling a bit smaller than when you picked it up. Here is the thing nobody tells you. You just lost a contest that was completely rigged.
**On screen.** A phone glowing with perfect photos, then the person holding it shrinking slightly with each swipe.

### Beat 2 · Big idea and the guide
**Narration.** Orbit here. One idea today. What you post is your best five seconds of the whole day. So is everyone else's. The feed is a wall of everybody's best five seconds, and it hides all the boring, normal, human bits in between. You are comparing your whole messy day to their single best frame.
**On screen.** Orbit stitching together a row of glowing "best moments", then peeling back one to show the ordinary Tuesday hidden behind it.

### Beat 3 · The tricks, in small steps
**Narration.** It works in a few ways. The highlight reel, you only ever see the peaks. Lifestyle envy, so their life looks shiny and yours looks flat. FOMO, that tug when you see something you were not part of, like life is happening somewhere else without you. And likes, the app turns you into a number and dares you to believe the number is you. It is not.
**On screen.** Four quick icons, a highlight reel, a shiny life next to a flat one, a party seen through a window, a like counter ticking.

### Beat 4 · Wrong then right
**Narration.** The trap sounds like this, their life is just better than mine. Here is the move. Ask one question. What am I not seeing? The deleted takes, the flight delay, the argument in the car, the plain days that look exactly like yours. The second you remember the edit, the photo loses its grip, because now you can see it for what it is.
**On screen.** Split, left a perfect beach post with "their life is better", right Orbit revealing the ninety deleted photos and the boring bits behind it.

### Beat 5 · The twist
**Narration.** Here is the part they hope you never work out. Likes and follows are not a score of how much you are worth. They are a score of how well the app held someone's attention for a second. A machine set that number, and the machine makes money every time you check it. Your worth was never in there.
**On screen.** A like counter, then Orbit gently lifting the number away from the person, who is fine without it.

### Beat 6 · Your move (interactive choice)
**Narration.** Pick one thing to try this week. Unfollow a few accounts that always make you feel small. Follow some that show real, normal life. Or decide, before you scroll, that likes are not the score of your day. Choose one now.
**On screen.** Three tappable cards, unfollow the ones that hurt, follow real life, likes are not the score. The young person taps one and Orbit reflects it back as their plan. No wrong answer.

### Beat 7 · Close
**Narration.** One line to keep. I am comparing my whole self to somebody's highlight reel, and that contest is not fair, so I can step out of it. Social media is not the enemy here, staying close to your people is real and good. It is only the comparing that stings, and now you can catch it. Next time, Nova opens up bodies and filters. And if the comparing ever gets heavy, tell a trusted adult, or Childline on 0800 1111, they are good at this.
**On screen.** The chosen card glows as "my move this week". Orbit winks. A small preview of a filter sliding across a face, hinting at the next module.

# VERSION C. PARENT (co view guide)

**Stage band:** Explorer to Shaper (ages 11 to 15), extends the existing 092 "Real life is not a highlight reel" spine, one layer deeper. **Format:** the 092 lessons pattern, a short guide plus conversation starters, for the `public.lessons` table. Stance, curious not critical, coach not judge.

**the_idea.** A feed shows your child everyone else's edited peaks and hides everyone's ordinary behind the scenes. So other lives look shinier and theirs looks flat, even though they are comparing their whole day to somebody's single best frame. Add the tug of missing out, and the habit of reading likes as a score of their worth, and you have the quiet mechanism that can pull a young person's mood down without anyone noticing.

**why_it_matters.** Social comparison is one of the main ways heavy scrolling links to lower mood, anxiety and loneliness in teenagers. As fear of missing out rises, how good a young person feels about their own life tends to fall. This is not about a weak child, it is about an unfair contest running quietly in the background. When you understand the mechanism, you can teach your child to see it, which protects them far more than banning the app, which they will only resent. The point is judgement, not fear.

**try_this.** Scroll alongside your child, no phone grab, no lecture. When a too perfect post comes up, ask lightly, "what do you reckon they left out of this one?" Let them list the deleted takes, the boring bits, the bad day before. Then ask the honest question, "does looking at this ever leave you feeling a bit worse?" If yes, thank them for saying so and do not fix it, just sit with it. Agree one small thing together, unfollowing a couple of accounts that always sting, following some that show real life, or deciding that likes are not the score of a day. And say the true thing out loud, that you compare too, adults do it constantly, so they know it is human, not a failing.

**key_message.** Your child is comparing their whole self to other people's highlight reels, and that contest is rigged. Your job is not to police the scrolling, it is to help them see the edit, so a perfect post loses its power over how they feel.

**digi_prompt (for DiGi, calibrated pathway, never allow or deny).** When a child says everyone's life looks better than theirs, DiGi does not tell them to log off and does not brush it away. It names the highlight reel calmly, asks what that perfect post probably left out, and hands them one small move plus a trusted adult to try it with.
