# Module 5: Bodies and filters

**13+ Social Media Literacy curriculum. Built in all three versions, following the Module 1 template.** British English, no dashes anywhere. New module, no existing 092 version. Mild sensitivity, so it carries a light DSL note and signposts help.

**The big idea:** the faces and bodies you scroll past are edited, and so is your own filtered selfie. When you forget that, you start measuring your real body against images that no real body matches, and that gap is where the harm sits.

**Learning outcome (one line, all versions):** I can explain why filtered and edited images set an impossible bar, and one thing I do about it.

**Mental health link, held calmly:** appearance focused content drives body dissatisfaction, especially when we treat edited images as if they were real. The strongest evidence to teach here is hopeful and practical. When teenagers cut back on social media, their feelings about their weight and appearance improved (APA 2023). That is a lever the young person actually holds. We also name honestly that almost half of girls report worrying often or always about their bodies, so nobody in the room feels alone in it. The eating disorder strand is handled calmly and without any how to detail, and every version closes on where to get help.

**Evidence anchors:** reducing social media use improved teens' feelings about weight and appearance (APA 2023, keep attributed). Appearance content and filters linked to body dissatisfaction (a body image review, keep attributed). The "almost half of girls worry often or always about their bodies" figure to be kept attributed to its source and confirmed before publication. Framing per Odgers (preparation over fear), Livingstone 4 Cs (Content, with a Contact edge where the algorithm surfaces harmful content), Przybylski (the Goldilocks amount, moderate use beats zero and heavy). Pair any learner facing usage claim with a UK anchor (Ofcom Children and Parents 2025).

**Cast:** first taught at 14, so KS3 into KS4, host Nova. DiGi is the through line guide.

# VERSION A. SCHOOLS IN DEPTH

**Stage:** KS3 to KS4, Years 9 to 10, age 14. **Length:** one lesson, about 55 minutes. **Format:** seven beat Rosenshine.

**Statutory and framework mapping:**
- RSHE (statutory): the impact of viewing harmful content, that images and reputations online can be manipulated or edited, the effect of unrealistic body ideals on self image and mental wellbeing, and where and how to seek support including for eating and body image concerns. Aligns to the 2026 guidance on edited and unrealistic online content.
- UKCIS Education for a Connected World strands: Self image and identity, Health wellbeing and lifestyle, Managing online information.
- PSHE Association: mental health and emotional wellbeing, healthy lifestyles, media and digital literacy.
- Computing national curriculum KS3 to KS4: understanding how digital images can be altered, using technology safely and responsibly.

**Ground rules (set before teaching, this lesson especially):** we talk about images and how they are made, not about anyone's body in this room, ever. No comments on appearance, our own or anyone's. No personal disclosures. Questions are always welcome. If anything today worries you about how you feel about your body, or about eating, the end of the lesson shows exactly where to go, and you can always speak to a member of staff.

**Baseline check (Rosenshine, start where they are):** one question on entry, "when you see a flawless face or body on your feed, how much of it do you think is real?" Collect three answers on the board, return to them at the end.

### Beat 1 · Recall and Do Now (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Last time we saw the feed is a highlight reel, everyone's best five seconds. Today we go one layer deeper, because the peaks are not just carefully chosen, they are often edited. Quick recall on whiteboards, name one way a photo can be changed before it is posted. Take three, no comment yet, we are about to see how far it goes.
**On screen.** The lesson title and the entry question, how much of it is real.
**Purpose.** Retrieval, links to Module 4, surfaces prior knowledge.

### Beat 2 · New input in small steps (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Three ideas today, calmly, one at a time. One, body image and the reality gap. A huge share of what you scroll past is edited, filtered, angled, lit and posed, and a lot of it is filtered in real time so you never see the before. When you measure your ordinary body against images no real body matches, the gap between them is where you start to feel bad. Two, the filter trap. Filters can be fun, and that is fine. The trouble starts in two places, when you begin to prefer your filtered face to your real one, and when you forget that everyone else's photos are edited too, so you assume their skin, their jaw, their shape are simply real. They usually are not. Three, and I will keep this brief and calm. The algorithm does not only show you filtered selfies. If someone lingers on content about weight or dieting, it can start pushing more extreme material, sometimes content that dresses up an eating disorder as a lifestyle or an aspiration. That content is not inspiration, it is a health risk, and knowing the algorithm can steer you toward it is exactly how you steer back out.
**On screen.** Three labelled cards revealed one at a time: The reality gap, The filter trap, When the feed pushes too far. The third card is plain text, no images.
**Purpose.** Small steps, one idea per card, the sensitive strand named without any graphic detail.

### Beat 3 · Worked example, model it aloud (8 min)
**Teacher script.** Watch me think this through. Here is a made up post, a flawless close up, perfect skin, sharp jaw. My gut says, my face does not look like that, something is wrong with mine. Now I slow it down. Question one, is this edited? Almost certainly, a filter smooths skin and reshapes a face in real time, and it is on by default in a lot of apps. Question two, am I comparing my real, moving, three dimensional face to a still, filtered, best angle image? Yes. Question three, would I lose if I compared my filtered photo to their unfiltered one? Of course, everyone would. So the comparison was never real. Here is the strong part, and it is proven. When teenagers cut back on this kind of scrolling, researchers found their feelings about their weight and appearance actually improved. That is not a lecture, it is a lever you are holding.
**On screen.** A filtered face beside the same face unfiltered, then the APA finding stated plainly as a hopeful fact.
**Purpose.** Worked example, models the reframe and lands the evidence lever.

### Beat 4 · Check for understanding, hinge question (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Hinge question, hold up A, B, C or D. You feel worse about your own face after scrolling. Which of these is the strongest, evidence backed thing you can actually do about it? A get better at editing your own photos, B cut back on appearance content, because teens who did reported feeling better about how they look, C never take a selfie again, D compare yourself only to people who look worse. Show me. If most say B, we move on. If not, we look again.
**On screen.** The multiple choice hinge.
**Purpose.** Whole class check, do not proceed below about 80 percent.

### Beat 5 · Guided practice, we do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Together now, in pairs, three scenario cards. One, someone starts to prefer their filtered photos and feels uneasy in real mirrors. Two, a person assumes a friend's edited pictures are just how they naturally look, and feels worse by comparison. Three, someone's feed keeps serving more and more content about dieting and bodies. For each, name what is going on, the filter trap, the reality gap, or the feed pushing too far, and name one move that takes control back, including, for the third, telling a trusted adult and using the app's not interested and report tools. I will take the first with you.
**On screen.** The three scenario cards and a two column frame, what is going on, my move.
**Purpose.** Guided practice with high success, and it rehearses help seeking for the sensitive strand.

### Beat 6 · Independent practice, you do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** On your own, and this stays yours, I collect the analysis and no personal detail. Write down one honest thing about how edited images affect how you feel, no names, nothing you would not want read. Then write one small move you will try, turning beauty filters off sometimes so you get used to your real face, unfollowing accounts that leave you feeling worse about your body, following a wider mix of real bodies and faces, or the proven one, cutting back on appearance content for a bit and noticing if you feel better. If your feed has been pushing content about weight or eating that worries you, the move is to tell a trusted adult, and I will show you exactly where to go in a moment.
**On screen.** The independent task and a reminder of the three ideas, plus a line pointing to the support slide next.
**Purpose.** Independent application, help seeking normalised as one of the options.

### Beat 7 · Review, consolidate, signpost (7 min)
**Teacher script.** Back to the board. Our entry answers on how much is real, which one holds up now? Far less than it looks, most of it is edited, and a lot is filtered before you ever see it. Say the headline back, the faces and bodies on my feed are edited, so measuring my real self against them is a bar nobody can meet, and cutting back genuinely helps. One honest, balanced note. Filters can be fun and social media has real good in it, this is not "delete everything", it is "know what you are looking at and cut back when it stings". And the important bit, if the way you feel about your body or about eating is getting heavy, please talk to someone. A trusted adult here, or Childline on 0800 1111. For worries about eating specifically there is a charity called Beat, and I will give you their details, they are kind and they know exactly what to say. Next lesson, Nova looks at influencers and the sell.
**On screen.** The one line summary, a moderation note, the support signpost (trusted adult, Childline 0800 1111, and Beat, helpline number to be confirmed before display), and a forward hook to Module 6.
**Purpose.** Spaced review, Goldilocks stretch, help routes named clearly for a sensitive topic.

**Endpoint assessment:** compare entry and exit answers to the "how much is real" question, plus the independent Beat 6 analysis, against the outcome, can the pupil explain why edited images set an impossible bar and name one evidence backed move. **Differentiation:** support, the three cards stay visible, pairs get sentence starters, and the sensitive strand is kept to naming and help seeking only; stretch, discuss why the answer is cutting back and curating rather than quitting (Przybylski Goldilocks), and why filters are not inherently bad, so the lesson never tips into shame or abstinence. **DSL note (this module carries one, mild sensitivity):** body image and eating content can touch pupils personally. Before teaching, know your school's referral route and who the DSL is. Watch for disclosures during or after the lesson, in independent work or quietly at the end, about body distress or disordered eating. Do not probe, do not promise secrecy, follow the school's safeguarding process the same day, and make sure the support slide (trusted adult, Childline, Beat) is visible and given as a takeaway. Confirm the Beat helpline number and hours against Beat's website before displaying it.

# VERSION B. KID (interactive lesson)

**Stage:** KS3 to KS4, age 14. **Host:** Nova. **Format:** the seven beat explainer, about 4 minutes, then one interactive choice. Non judgemental, agency first, calm on the sensitive strand, and it closes on help.

### Beat 1 · Hook
**Narration.** You scroll past a face so perfect you feel a bit rubbish about your own. Here is what you were not told. That face is probably edited, smoothed and reshaped by a filter before it was ever posted. You just compared your real face to something that does not exist.
**On screen.** A flawless close up, then Nova sliding the filter off it to reveal an ordinary, real face underneath.

### Beat 2 · Big idea and the guide
**Narration.** Nova here. One idea today. A massive amount of what you scroll past is edited, filtered, angled and posed, and loads of it is filtered live so you never see the before. So when you measure your real, moving, ordinary face against a still, perfect, filtered one, the bar is impossible. Nobody's real face clears it. Not theirs either.
**On screen.** Nova holding up a filtered photo next to a real face, the two clearly different, a small "edited" tag popping onto the photo.

### Beat 3 · The tricks, in small steps
**Narration.** It works in a few ways. The reality gap, you compare your real self to edited images. The filter trap, part one, you start to prefer your filtered face to your actual one. The filter trap, part two, you forget everyone else is filtered too, so you assume their skin and jaw are just real. And sometimes the feed goes further, if you linger on stuff about weight or dieting, it can start pushing more and more, sometimes content that makes an eating disorder look like a goal. That is not a goal, it is a health risk, and I will show you what to do about it.
**On screen.** Three calm icons, a real face next to an edited one, a filter toggle, and a plain "when the feed pushes too far" card with no images.

### Beat 4 · Wrong then right
**Narration.** The trap sounds like this, something is wrong with how I look. Here is the move, and this one is proven. When teenagers cut back on this kind of scrolling, researchers found they started feeling better about their weight and their looks. Nothing changed about their bodies. What changed was how much they were feeding the comparison. That is a lever, and it is in your hand, not the app's.
**On screen.** Split, left a spiral of perfect faces with "something is wrong with me", right Nova turning the volume down on the feed and the person visibly lifting.

### Beat 5 · The twist
**Narration.** Here is the part worth knowing. You are not weak for feeling this. Almost half of girls say they worry about their bodies often or always, so if you feel it, you are in a big crowd, not alone. And the images doing it are mostly not real. Knowing that does not fix everything, but it takes the power out of the perfect photo, because now you can see the filter on it.
**On screen.** Nova showing a crowd, lots of people quietly carrying the same worry, then the filters lifting off the images around them.

### Beat 6 · Your move (interactive choice)
**Narration.** Pick one thing to try this week. Turn beauty filters off sometimes, so you get comfortable with your real face. Unfollow the accounts that leave you feeling worse, and follow a wider mix of real faces and bodies. Or try the proven one, cut back on appearance content for a bit and notice if you feel better. Choose one now.
**On screen.** Three tappable cards, filters off sometimes, follow real bodies, cut back and notice. The young person taps one and Nova reflects it back as their plan. No wrong answer.

### Beat 7 · Close
**Narration.** One line to keep. The faces and bodies on my feed are edited, so measuring my real self against them is a bar nobody can meet, and cutting back genuinely helps. Filters can be fun, this is not about hating them, it is about knowing what you are looking at. And one important thing. If how you feel about your body, or about eating, is getting heavy, please tell someone. A trusted adult, or Childline on 0800 1111. If it is about eating, a charity called Beat is there for exactly this. You are not meant to carry it on your own. Next time, I take you into influencers and the sell.
**On screen.** The chosen card glows as "my move this week". Nova gives a warm nod. The support signpost stays on screen, trusted adult, Childline 0800 1111, Beat.

# VERSION C. PARENT (co view guide)

**Stage band:** Explorer to Shaper (ages 12 to 15). **Format:** the 092 lessons pattern, a short guide plus conversation starters, for the `public.lessons` table. New lesson, no 092 version. Stance, curious not critical, coach not judge, and gentle because the topic is tender.

**the_idea.** Most of the faces and bodies your child scrolls past are edited, filtered, angled and posed, and a lot of it is filtered live so the before is never shown. When they measure their real self against images no real body matches, that gap is where body worry grows. Two extra risks sit alongside it, they can start preferring their own filtered face to their real one, and the algorithm can push appearance and dieting content harder the more it is watched, occasionally surfacing content that frames an eating disorder as a goal.

**why_it_matters.** Appearance focused content drives body dissatisfaction, and this is not a small thing, almost half of girls report worrying about their bodies often or always. The most useful fact to hold is a hopeful one. When teenagers cut back on social media, researchers found their feelings about their weight and appearance improved (APA 2023). That means the strongest protection is not a ban and not a lecture about self esteem, it is helping your child see that the images are edited and gently reduce the appearance content that stings. This is a lever they can hold themselves.

**try_this.** Look at some posts together, lightly, no comments about anyone's body including your own. Point at a too perfect image and wonder aloud, "how much of this do you reckon is a filter?" Try one together, notice how it smooths and reshapes in real time, and how the before looked just fine. Share the hopeful fact plainly, that teens who cut back on this kind of content started feeling better about how they look, nothing about their bodies changed. Agree one small thing together, filters off sometimes, unfollowing accounts that leave them feeling worse, following a wider mix of real bodies, or a quiet cut back to test the effect. And keep the door open, "if you ever feel bad about your body or about eating, you can always tell me, and I will not make a fuss, I will just help."

**key_message.** The images your child compares themselves to are edited, so the bar is impossible, and cutting back genuinely helps. Your job is not to police mirrors or meals, it is to help them see the edit and to be the safe person they tell if it ever gets heavy.

**digi_prompt (for DiGi, calibrated pathway, never allow or deny).** When a child says they hate how they look next to people online, DiGi does not brush it off and does not diagnose. It names the reality gap calmly, reminds them the images are edited, offers one small evidence backed move, and if the worry sounds heavy, gently points them to a trusted adult and to Childline, and for eating worries to Beat. If a child describes anything about not eating or harming themselves, DiGi always routes to a trusted adult and named help, never handles it alone.
