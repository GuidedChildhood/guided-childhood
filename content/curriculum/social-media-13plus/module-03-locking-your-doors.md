# Module 3: Locking your doors

**13+ Social Media Literacy curriculum. Built in all three versions, following the Module 1 template shape.** British English, no dashes anywhere. Extends the existing 092 lesson "The settings that keep you private", going deeper for 13+, not repeating it.

**The big idea:** you get to decide who can see you and who can reach you. Setting your own doors, high privacy, contact controls, location off, block and report, turns a worry about being watched into a choice you control.

**Learning outcome (one line, all versions):** I can explain the four doors I control, who sees me, who can contact me, my location, and how to block and report, and set at least one of them myself.

**Mental health link, held calmly:** a lot of low level anxiety online comes from exposure, the feeling that anyone can see you, reach you or judge you at any time. That is a heavy thing to carry. The answer is not to hide or come off everything. It is that when you set the doors yourself, you turn that worry into control. You decide who is in and who is out. That is agency, and it feels a lot lighter than being watched.

**House rule on the settings walk (non negotiable 6):** the actual click by click, per platform settings walk lives in the database, in the scripts table, because it dates fastest as apps change their menus. So the schools and kid lessons here teach the principle, high privacy by default, know who can reach you, location off, know how to block and report, and point to the live settings walk for the current steps. No current menu paths are hardcoded in these lessons.

**Evidence anchors, UK and disciplined:** Ofcom Children and Parents 2024 and 2025 on children with public profiles and exposure; the ICO Children's Code, which sets high privacy by default and geolocation off by default for children; Ofcom on default contact control changes that make it harder for adults to message children they do not know. Framing per Odgers (preparation over fear), Livingstone 4 Cs (this module works the Contact and Privacy space). No invented statistics, teach the principle where a precise UK figure is not to hand.

**Cast:** first taught at 13, so KS3, host Orbit. DiGi is the through line guide.

# VERSION A. SCHOOLS IN DEPTH

**Stage:** KS3, Years 8 to 9, ages 13 to 14. **Length:** one lesson, about 55 minutes. **Format:** seven beat Rosenshine.

**Statutory and framework mapping:**
- RSHE (statutory): how to use social media safely, the risks of sharing material online, keeping personal information private, and where and how to report concerns and get support.
- UKCIS Education for a Connected World strands: Privacy and security, Online relationships, Managing online information.
- Computing national curriculum KS3: using technology safely, securely and responsibly, and protecting personal information.
- PSHE Association: managing online safety and personal information, help seeking.

**Ground rules (set before teaching, every lesson in this curriculum):** we talk about the settings and the scenarios, not about each other. No personal disclosures, nobody has to share who has messaged them. Questions are always welcome. If anything here worries you about yourself or a friend, the end of the lesson shows exactly where to go.

**Baseline check (Rosenshine, start where they are):** one question on entry, "right now, who can see what you post, and who can send you a message?" Collect three answers on the board, return to them at the end.

### Beat 1 · Recall and Do Now (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Last time we read the deal, you hand over data and a post can last. Quick recall on your whiteboards, name one thing you are really agreeing to when you make an account. Take three. Now today. If a house has doors, you decide who comes in. Your accounts have doors too. Today we find them and learn to lock the ones you want locked. First question up on the board.
**On screen.** The lesson title and the entry question, who can see you and who can reach you.
**Purpose.** Retrieval, links to Module 2, surfaces the misconception that defaults are always safe.

### Beat 2 · New input in small steps (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Four doors, one at a time, and we learn the principle for each because the exact taps change with every app update. One, who can see you, public or private. A public account can be seen by anyone. A private one means you approve who follows you. Ofcom's work on children and parents shows a lot of young people still have public profiles without meaning to. Two, who can contact you. Apps have settings for who can send you a message, and the safest default is that strangers cannot just slide into your messages. Regulators have pushed apps to tighten this for under 18s for exactly that reason. Three, location and metadata. Photos and posts can carry where you are, and the ICO Children's Code says location should be off by default for children. So the principle is, location off unless you have a real reason. Four, block and report. Every app has both, block stops someone reaching you, report flags them to the platform. Now the honest bit, plenty of people never use these because it feels awkward, like you are making a fuss. You are not. It is a normal tool, like locking a door.
**On screen.** Four labelled cards revealed one at a time: Who can see you, Who can contact you, Location, Block and report.
**Purpose.** Small steps, principle not menu path, names the friction on blocking and reporting honestly.

### Beat 3 · Worked example, model it aloud (8 min)
**Teacher script.** Watch me set the doors on a made up account. First door, who can see me. I make it private, now I approve followers, so strangers cannot just watch me. Second, who can contact me. I set messages so only people I follow can reach me, the front door is shut to strangers. Third, location. I turn location sharing off, because there is no reason a photo needs to broadcast where I am. Fourth, I hit a problem, someone I do not know keeps messaging. I do not argue, I do not screenshot and stew. I block, and I report. Done, calmly. Notice I did not memorise a single menu, I knew what I wanted each door to do and found it. The live settings walk shows the current taps for each app.
**On screen.** Four doors closing one at a time as the teacher narrates, then a "see the live settings walk" pointer.
**Purpose.** Worked example, models the principle and the calm block and report before pupils try it.

### Beat 4 · Check for understanding, hinge question (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Hinge question, hold up A, B, C or D. According to the ICO Children's Code, for a child's account, location sharing should be. A on by default so friends can find you, B off by default, C decided by the app, D only off if a parent asks. Show me. If most say B, we move on. If not, we look again.
**On screen.** The multiple choice hinge.
**Purpose.** Whole class check on the default principle, do not proceed below about 80 percent.

### Beat 5 · Guided practice, we do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Together now. In pairs you have three scenario cards. One, someone realises their account is public and strangers are watching. Two, a photo posted from a bedroom that quietly shows the location. Three, a person who keeps getting messages from someone they do not know and feels rude blocking them. For each, name which door is open, and name the move that closes it, plus where you would find the live steps. I will take the first with you, then you do two and three.
**On screen.** The three scenario cards and a two column frame, the open door, my move.
**Purpose.** Guided practice with high success, scaffolded onto the four doors, normalises blocking.

### Beat 6 · Independent practice, you do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** On your own, nothing personal you do not want to write. Make your own "doors checklist", the four doors and what you would set each one to, and why. Public or private, who can message me, location on or off, and one line on when I would block or report and why that is not rude. If you want, you can use the live settings walk to actually set one door tonight. I am collecting your checklist and your reasoning, not who has contacted you.
**On screen.** The independent task, the four doors, and a pointer to the live settings walk.
**Purpose.** Independent application, the principle turned into the pupil's own plan.

### Beat 7 · Review, consolidate, signpost (7 min)
**Teacher script.** Back to the board. Our entry answers on who can see and reach you, would you change them now? Here is the headline, say it back to me, I decide who sees me and who reaches me, and I can set those doors myself. This is not about locking yourself away or coming off everything, plenty of being online is good and worth staying open to. It is about you choosing the doors instead of leaving them on whatever the app set. Next lesson we look at the highlight reel, why everyone else's life looks better than yours online. And blocking and reporting is never rude, and if someone is reaching you in a way that worries you, tell a trusted adult here, or Childline on 0800 1111.
**On screen.** The one line summary, a forward hook to Module 4, and the support signpost.
**Purpose.** Spaced review, Goldilocks balance, links forward, help routes named, normalises reporting.

**Endpoint assessment:** compare entry and exit answers on who can see and reach them, plus the Beat 6 doors checklist, against the outcome, can the pupil explain the four doors and set at least one. **Differentiation:** support, the four door cards stay visible and pairs get sentence starters, "the open door here is ...", "the move is ..."; stretch, ask why apps often set the doors open by default and who benefits, and why the aim is sensible control rather than locking everything down (Przybylski Goldilocks) so the lesson never tips into "come off social media". **DSL note:** not a sensitive module, but blocking and reporting scenarios can surface real contact concerns. If a pupil discloses unwanted contact, grooming signs, or that someone will not leave them alone, follow the school's normal safeguarding route to the DSL straight away.

# VERSION B. KID (interactive lesson)

**Stage:** KS3, ages 13 to 14. **Host:** Orbit. **Format:** the seven beat explainer, about 4 minutes, then one interactive choice. Non judgemental, agency first, the young person is the decision maker. This is the child facing re cut of the same idea.

### Beat 1 · Hook
**Narration.** Your house has a front door, and you decide who comes in. Your accounts have doors too. Most people never touch them, they just leave them on whatever the app set. Today you find them, and you decide.
**On screen.** A row of doors on a phone screen, some wide open, a hand reaching to close the ones it wants shut.

### Beat 2 · Big idea and the guide
**Narration.** Orbit here. One idea today. You get to decide who can see you and who can reach you. That feeling of being watched by everyone, that eases a lot when you realise you hold the keys. Four doors. Who sees you, who can message you, your location, and block and report. Let us walk them.
**On screen.** Orbit standing by four labelled doors, ready to open each one.

### Beat 3 · The four doors, in small steps
**Narration.** Door one, who sees you. Public means anyone. Private means you approve who follows. Door two, who can message you, you can set it so strangers cannot just message you. Door three, location, your posts can quietly show where you are, so the safe setting is location off. Door four, block and report, block stops someone reaching you, report flags them to the app. The exact taps change every update, so the live settings walk always has the current steps.
**On screen.** Four quick icons, an eye, a message, a location pin with a line through it, a shield.

### Beat 4 · Wrong then right
**Narration.** Here is the thing people get wrong. They think blocking or reporting is rude, like making a fuss, so they just put up with it. Here is the move. Blocking a stranger is not rude, it is you locking your own door. You do not owe anyone access to you. If someone you do not know keeps messaging, you do not argue, you do not stew. You block, and if it is dodgy, you report. Calm and done.
**On screen.** Split, left someone anxiously staring at a stranger's messages, right Orbit calmly tapping block and the messages gone.

### Beat 5 · The twist
**Narration.** Here is the part worth knowing. The app often sets your doors wide open by default, because open is better for them, more views, more reach. Nobody comes and locks them for you. So the doors stay open until you decide to set them. That is not scary, it is just your job, and it takes about two minutes.
**On screen.** A row of doors all swinging open on their own, then Orbit handing over a set of keys.

### Beat 6 · Your move (interactive choice)
**Narration.** Pick one door to set this week. Go private so you choose who sees you. Set your messages so strangers cannot reach you. Or turn your location off. Use the live settings walk for the exact taps. Choose one now.
**On screen.** Three tappable cards, go private, lock my messages, location off, each linking to the live settings walk. The young person taps one and Orbit reflects it back as their plan. No wrong answer.

### Beat 7 · Close
**Narration.** One line to keep. I decide who sees me and who reaches me, and I can set those doors myself. This is not about hiding away, staying connected is good. It is about you holding the keys instead of the app. And blocking or reporting someone is never rude, if anyone is reaching you in a way that worries you, tell a trusted adult, or Childline on 0800 1111. Next time, Orbit looks at why everyone else's life seems better than yours online.
**On screen.** The chosen door clicks shut and glows as "my move this week". Orbit gives a small nod, keys jingling.

# VERSION C. PARENT (co view guide)

**Stage band:** Explorer to Shaper (ages 11 to 15), extends the existing 092 spine. **Format:** the 092 lessons pattern, a short guide plus conversation starters, for the `public.lessons` table. Stance, curious not critical, coach not judge.

**the_idea.** Your child controls four doors on every account, who can see them, who can contact them, whether their posts share their location, and how they block and report. Apps often leave these doors open by default, because open means more reach for the platform. Nobody sets them for your child. Setting them is a two minute job that turns a vague worry about being watched into real control.

**why_it_matters.** A lot of the anxiety teenagers carry online comes from exposure, the sense that anyone can see them, reach them or judge them at any moment. Ofcom's work shows plenty of young people have public profiles without really meaning to. The fix is not to ban the app or make them come off. It is to help them set their own doors, because agency is the thing that lifts the worry. And there is a safeguarding layer, contact controls and knowing how to block and report calmly are exactly what protects a child from unwanted contact. The ICO Children's Code even sets high privacy and location off as the default children should get.

**try_this.** Do it together, as a team, not an inspection. "Shall we do a two minute doors check on your account?" Walk the four together, who can see you, who can message you, location, and how you would block and report. The click by click steps change constantly, so use the live settings walk in the app rather than trusting a menu you half remember. Make one point clearly, blocking or reporting someone is never rude, it is locking your own door, and they never owe a stranger access. Set one door together tonight and leave the rest for them to finish. A door they set themselves is one they understand.

**key_message.** Your child holds the keys to four doors, who sees them, who reaches them, their location, and block and report. Your job is not to lock everything down for them, it is to hand them the keys and make sure they know blocking and reporting is normal, not rude.

**digi_prompt (for DiGi, calibrated pathway, never allow or deny).** When a child feels exposed, watched, or bothered by someone they do not know, DiGi does not judge and does not lecture, and it never simply says lock it all down or leave it. It names which door is open, points to the live settings walk for the current steps, makes clear that blocking and reporting is not rude, and hands them one small move plus a trusted adult to try it with.
