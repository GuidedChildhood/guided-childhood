# Module 12: Sleep, attention and the body

**13+ Social Media Literacy curriculum. Built in all three versions, on the approved Module 1 template.** New module. British English, no dashes anywhere.

**The big idea:** the phone does not just take your time, it reaches into your sleep and your focus, mostly at night and mostly without you noticing. A few concrete moves take that back. The biggest one is simple, the phone leaves the bedroom.

**Learning outcome (one line, all versions):** I can name how night time and constant use affect my sleep and attention, and one concrete move I make about it.

**Mental health link, held calmly:** two plain findings sit under this. Using a phone at bedtime is linked to more disturbed sleep, and poor sleep tracks with more anxiety and low mood, so the night phone is doing more than costing you an hour. And constant switching between apps and notifications trains the brain toward distraction, with heavier, more compulsive use linked to more attention problems. This is not about willpower and it is not a lecture. It is about a couple of settings and where the phone sleeps.

**Evidence anchors:** night time use delays, shortens and fragments sleep; a large UK study of adolescents aged 13 to 15 (the Millennium Cohort Study) found very high users more likely to report late sleep onset, late waking and trouble getting back to sleep. Attention and fragmentation, constant switching trains the brain toward distraction. The phone at night as the specific pressure point. Concrete moves taught directly, phone out of the bedroom, notifications off at night. Framing per Odgers (preparation over fear), Przybylski (Goldilocks, moderate use beats both extremes). UK anchor for usage and support: Ofcom Children and Parents 2025.

**Cast:** first taught at 13 to 14, so KS3, host Orbit. DiGi is the through line guide.

# VERSION A. SCHOOLS IN DEPTH

**Stage:** KS3, Years 8 to 9, ages 13 to 14. **Length:** one lesson, about 55 minutes. **Format:** seven beat Rosenshine.

**Statutory and framework mapping:**
- RSHE (statutory): the importance of sufficient good quality sleep for good health and how a lack of sleep can affect weight, mood and ability to learn, and how time online can affect wellbeing. Aligns to the 2026 guidance emphasis on healthy online habits.
- UKCIS Education for a Connected World strands: Health wellbeing and lifestyle, Managing online information, Privacy and security.
- PSHE Association: healthy lifestyles, mental health and emotional wellbeing, media and digital literacy.
- Sits inside the school's KCSIE safeguarding duty.

**Ground rules (set before teaching, every lesson in this curriculum):** we talk about the design and the scenarios, not about each other. No personal disclosures. Questions are always welcome. If anything here worries you about yourself or a friend, the end of the lesson shows exactly where to go.

**Baseline check (Rosenshine, start where they are):** one question on entry, "where does your phone spend the night, and what time is your last scroll?" Collect three answers on the board, no names, return to them at the end.

### Beat 1 · Recall and Do Now (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Earlier we learned the app has no natural stop, you bring the stop. Tonight is where that bites hardest. Quick recall on your whiteboards, name one thing that makes it hard to put the phone down at bedtime. Take three, no comment yet. We are about to explain why night is the worst time for the machine to have you, and what to do about it.
**On screen.** The lesson title and the entry question, where does the phone sleep.
**Purpose.** Retrieval, activates prior schema, brings the real bedtime scenario into the room.

### Beat 2 · New input in small steps (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Three ideas today, one at a time. One, sleep. Using the phone at night delays when you fall asleep, shortens the total, and breaks it into pieces. A large UK study of adolescents your age, the Millennium Cohort Study, found the very heaviest users were more likely to report a late sleep onset, waking late, and trouble getting back to sleep once woken. Two, attention. Every notification is a tap that pulls your focus, and constant switching trains the brain to expect the next interruption, so sitting with one thing gets harder. Heavier, more compulsive use is linked to more of that. Three, the phone at night specifically. This is the pressure point, because the pull to check is strongest exactly when your body needs to wind down, and one buzz can cost you an hour.
**On screen.** Three labelled cards revealed one at a time: Sleep, Attention, The phone at night.
**Purpose.** Small steps, one idea per card, respects working memory.

### Beat 3 · Worked example, model it aloud (8 min)
**Teacher script.** Watch me run a normal night. Lights off, phone on the pillow, one last check. A notification buzzes, I answer it, that is the switch, now I am awake again. Autoplay carries me into videos, no natural stop, so falling asleep slips later and later. At 2am a buzz wakes me and I check it, now I am wide awake and cannot drop back off, that is the fragmentation the UK study describes. Morning, I am flat and I cannot focus in first period. Notice I did nothing dramatic. The phone in the room did the work. The fix is not willpower at 2am, it is deciding at 9pm where the phone sleeps.
**On screen.** A night timeline, phone on the pillow, buzz, delayed onset, a 2am wake, a groggy morning, each labelled.
**Purpose.** Worked example, models the reasoning before pupils try it.

### Beat 4 · Check for understanding, hinge question (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Hinge question, hold up A, B, C or D. Someone keeps waking at night to a buzzing phone by the bed and cannot get back to sleep. What is the single most effective move? A scroll until tired, B keep the phone by the bed on silent, C charge the phone in another room overnight, D reply faster so notifications stop. Show me. If most say C, we move on. If not, we look again.
**On screen.** The multiple choice hinge.
**Purpose.** Whole class check, do not proceed below about 80 percent.

### Beat 5 · Guided practice, we do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Together now. In pairs you have three short scenario cards, someone who cannot fall asleep because there is always one more video, someone whose sleep breaks up because their phone buzzes on the pillow all night, someone who cannot focus in lessons because they are used to checking every few minutes. For each, name what is going on, and name one concrete move that fixes it. I will take the first with you, then you do two and three.
**On screen.** The three scenario cards and a two column frame, what is going on, my move.
**Purpose.** Guided practice with high success, scaffolded.

### Beat 6 · Independent practice, you do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** On your own. Design your own night plan. Write where the phone will charge tonight, what time notifications go off, and one attention move for the day, like keeping the phone out of sight while you do one task. Then write the one you think you will actually keep, and why. This is yours. I am not collecting personal detail, I am collecting the plan.
**On screen.** The independent task and a reminder of the three ideas.
**Purpose.** Independent application, the skill transferred to their own life.

### Beat 7 · Review, consolidate, signpost (7 min)
**Teacher script.** Back to the board. Our entry answers on where the phone sleeps, what changes now? Say the headline back to me, the phone reaches my sleep and my focus at night, and the fix is where it sleeps and whether it buzzes. Two moves do most of the work, phone out of the bedroom, notifications off at night. This is not about giving up your phone, moderate daytime use is completely fine, it is about protecting the night. And if you are lying awake worrying, or your sleep or mood has been rough for a while, that is worth telling someone. A trusted adult here, or Childline on 0800 1111.
**On screen.** The one line summary, the two concrete moves, and the support signpost.
**Purpose.** Spaced review, Goldilocks stretch, help routes named.

**Endpoint assessment:** compare entry and exit answers on where the phone sleeps, plus the Beat 6 night plan, against the outcome, can the pupil name a sleep or attention effect and one concrete move. **Differentiation:** support, the three cards stay visible and pairs get sentence starters and a ready made night timeline; stretch, ask why the point is the night and the heaviest use, not phones in general (the UK study flags the very highest users, and Przybylski's Goldilocks says moderate use is fine) so the lesson never tips into "phones ruin your health". **DSL note:** wellbeing content, handled calmly. If a pupil's independent work discloses ongoing sleeplessness, night time distress or low mood, follow the school's normal pastoral and safeguarding route.

# VERSION B. KID (interactive lesson)

**Stage:** KS3, ages 13 to 14. **Host:** Orbit. **Format:** the seven beat explainer, about 4 minutes, then one interactive choice. Non judgemental, agency first, the young person is the decision maker. This is the child facing re cut of the same idea.

### Beat 1 · Hook
**Narration.** It is late. You meant to sleep an hour ago. One more video, one more reply, and the phone is right there on the pillow, buzzing. Morning comes and you are flat and foggy and you cannot focus. That is not you being lazy. That is the phone doing its job in the worst possible room.
**On screen.** A dark bedroom, a phone glowing on a pillow, a clock sliding past midnight, a groggy morning face.

### Beat 2 · Big idea and the guide
**Narration.** Orbit here. One idea today. The phone does not just take your time, it reaches into your sleep and your focus, and it does the most damage at night. Fix the night, and you fix a surprising amount of the rest.
**On screen.** Orbit lifting a phone off a pillow and setting it in another room, the bedroom going calm.

### Beat 3 · What it does, in small steps
**Narration.** Here is what happens. A phone at night makes you fall asleep later, sleep less, and wake in pieces, a big UK study of teens your age found exactly that in the heaviest users. In the day, every notification is a tug on your focus, and after enough of them your brain expects the next interruption, so concentrating gets harder. None of it is a flaw in you. It is the pull of the phone in the wrong place at the wrong time.
**On screen.** Three quick icons, a delayed clock, a broken sleep line, a brain jumping between pings.

### Beat 4 · Wrong then right
**Narration.** The trap sounds like this, I will just keep it on silent by the bed. But silent still glows, still tempts, and one 2am glance costs you an hour. Here is the move. The phone charges in another room. Out of reach means out of mind, and your sleep gets the night back.
**On screen.** Split, left a phone buzzing on the pillow and a spiral of wake ups, right the phone charging across the room and a smooth sleep line.

### Beat 5 · The twist
**Narration.** Here is the part they hope you never notice. The app does not know it is night, and it does not care. It pulls just as hard at midnight as at noon. So the boundary has to come from you. And this is not about giving up the phone, using it in the day is fine, it is about guarding the eight hours that put you back together.
**On screen.** An app pulling equally hard on a day and night dial, then Orbit drawing a bright line around the night.

### Beat 6 · Your move (interactive choice)
**Narration.** Pick one thing to try this week. Charge the phone outside the bedroom. Turn notifications off at night. Or set a last scroll time and keep it. Choose one now.
**On screen.** Three tappable cards, phone out of the room, notifications off at night, a last scroll time. The young person taps one and Orbit reflects it back as their plan. No wrong answer.

### Beat 7 · Close
**Narration.** One line to keep. The phone reaches my sleep and my focus at night, and the fix is where it sleeps and whether it buzzes. If you are lying awake worrying, or sleep has been rough for a while, that is worth telling someone, a trusted adult, or Childline on 0800 1111. Guard the night, and the days get easier.
**On screen.** The chosen card glows as "my move this week". Orbit dims the room to a calm night. The support line stays on screen.

# VERSION C. PARENT (co view guide)

**Stage band:** Ranger to Explorer (ages 11 to 14), a new addition to the 092 spine. **Format:** the 092 lessons pattern, a short guide plus conversation starters, for the `public.lessons` table. Stance, curious not critical, coach not judge.

**the_idea.** The phone does not only cost your child time, it reaches into their sleep and their attention, and it does the most harm at night. Used in bed it delays sleep, shortens it and breaks it up, and a large UK study of teenagers found the heaviest users struggling most with falling asleep, waking late and getting back off after a wake up. In the day, constant notifications train the brain to expect the next interruption, so focus gets harder. The fix is not a battle over the phone, it is a couple of concrete moves, mostly about the night.

**why_it_matters.** Sleep is the quiet engine under mood, weight and learning, so protecting the night protects a lot at once. The point is not that phones are bad, moderate daytime use is genuinely fine, it is that night time and the heaviest use are where the harm concentrates. Two moves do most of the work, the phone charges outside the bedroom, and notifications go off at night. Because these are small and specific, they are easy to agree and easy to keep, far more than a vague "less screen time".

**try_this.** Skip the lecture, make it practical and shared. Ask, without blame, "where does your phone sleep, and does it wake you?" Then explain the why in one line, a phone in the room means later nights and broken sleep, and broken sleep means rough mornings. Offer to make the change together and to do it yourself too, your phone charges downstairs, so does theirs. Set it up as a family move, not a punishment aimed only at them. Agree one small thing, phone charging in the hall, notifications off after a set time, or a last scroll time you both keep. A rule they help write, and see you keep too, is a rule that sticks.

**key_message.** The phone reaches your child's sleep and focus, and mostly at night. Your job is not to fight the phone all day, it is to guard the night with a couple of concrete moves you set up together.

**digi_prompt (for DiGi, calibrated pathway, never allow or deny).** When a child says they cannot sleep or cannot focus, DiGi does not judge and does not lecture. It asks where the phone spends the night and what the last hour looks like, explains the sleep link plainly, and hands them one concrete move, phone out of the room or notifications off at night, and a trusted adult to set it up with. If sleep or worry has been hard for a while, DiGi calmly names Childline on 0800 1111 and a trusted adult as the next step.
