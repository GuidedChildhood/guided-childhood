# Module 15: AI companions and chatbots

**13+ Social Media Literacy curriculum. Built in all three versions, following the Module 1 template. British English, no dashes anywhere.**

**The big idea:** an AI companion can feel like it listens, but it is a tool, not a friend, a therapist or a trusted adult, and it can be confidently wrong. Knowing what it is, and what it is not, is how you use it well and lean on real people for the things that matter.

**Learning outcome (one line, all versions):** I can say what an AI companion is and is not, and I can name one thing I would take to a real person instead of a bot.

**Mental health link, held calmly:** most young people who use these apps keep their perspective, most still prefer real friends and find a bot less satisfying than a person. The point is not panic. The point is that a small number of people, often when they are lonely or going through a hard time, come to lean on a companion app more heavily than is good for them, or follow advice from a bot that a real person would never have given. It is the activity and the child that matter, not the tool. Seeing the bot clearly is what keeps it in its place.

**Evidence anchors:** AI companion apps and chatbots as a mainstream part of teen life; the two bucket discipline from the house AI briefing, stay calm and sceptical about the companion panic while taking the heavily dependent minority seriously; emotional dependence and the heavy use pattern concentrated in the lonely, anxious and neurodivergent child; the limits of chatbot advice, a bot is not a friend, a therapist or a trusted adult and can be confidently wrong. The 2026 RSHE guidance names AI chatbots explicitly, which is why this is taught. Framing per Odgers (preparation over fear), the AAP line that AI is a tool, not a companion, and boyd (literacy must be taught explicitly). No invented statistics, teach the principle where a precise UK figure is not to hand.

**Cast:** first taught at 14 to 15, so KS4, host Nova. DiGi is the through line guide, and is itself the worked example of a tool that stays a tool.

# VERSION A. SCHOOLS IN DEPTH

**Stage:** KS4, Year 10, ages 14 to 15. **Length:** one lesson, about 55 minutes. **Format:** seven beat Rosenshine.

**Statutory and framework mapping:**
- RSHE (statutory): Internet safety and harms, and the 2026 guidance addition naming AI and chatbots explicitly, including how AI generated content and advice can be false or unreliable. Building to the 2026 guidance means schools can adopt this to meet a duty that lands in September 2026.
- UKCIS Education for a Connected World strands: Managing online information, Online relationships, Health wellbeing and lifestyle.
- Computing national curriculum KS4: understanding how digital systems and AI generate content, using technology safely and responsibly.
- PSHE Association: media and digital literacy, healthy relationships and seeking support.

**Ground rules (set before teaching):** we talk about how the technology works and about the scenarios, not about each other. No personal disclosures about who uses what. Questions are always welcome. If anything here makes you think about your own use, or a friend leaning heavily on a bot, the end of the lesson shows exactly where to go.

**Baseline check (Rosenshine, start where they are):** one question on entry, "what is the difference between talking to an AI chatbot and talking to a friend?" Collect three answers on the board, return to them at the end.

### Beat 1 · Recall and Do Now (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Across this curriculum we have kept coming back to one line, it is the activity and the child, not the device. Hold that. On your whiteboards, one line each, name one thing an AI chatbot can genuinely be useful for, and one thing you would never want it to replace. Take three of each, no comment yet. We are about to draw exactly where that line sits.
**On screen.** The lesson title and the entry question comparing a chatbot to a friend.
**Purpose.** Retrieval, activates the whole course spine, surfaces where pupils already place the line.

### Beat 2 · New input in small steps (10 min)
**Teacher script.** One idea first. An AI companion is software designed to feel like a relationship, warm, always available, always agreeable, but it is a tool, not a friend, a therapist or a trusted adult. Now the detail, one step at a time. One, what these apps are, chatbots you talk to like a person, some built purely as companions. Two, the honest good, they can be a low pressure place to rehearse a conversation, practise a language, or think out loud, and for a shy or anxious person that has real value. Three, the honest limits, a companion is built to keep you engaged and to agree with you, so it can be confidently wrong, it does not actually know you, and it cannot carry real responsibility for you. Four, the heavy use pattern, most people keep perspective and still prefer real friends, but a minority, often when lonely or struggling, come to depend on it, and that is the pattern to take seriously. Five, the two buckets, we stay calm about the general panic because most teens are fine, and we take seriously the smaller group who become dependent or who follow bad advice. Both are true at once.
**On screen.** Five labelled cards revealed one at a time: What they are, The honest good, The honest limits, The heavy use pattern, The two buckets.
**Purpose.** Small steps, one idea per card, holds the good and the risk together without tipping into panic.

### Beat 3 · Worked example, model it aloud (8 min)
**Teacher script.** Watch me weigh one up, using a character to keep it at arm's length. This is Priya, fifteen. She uses a companion app most nights. Some of it is genuinely fine, she rehearses awkward conversations and it takes the edge off. Then one week she is really low, and she starts telling the bot things she is not telling anyone else, and the bot just agrees with her and keeps her chatting, because that is what it is built to do. Notice the two buckets in one person. The rehearsing, calm, that is fine, low stakes, useful. The low week, that is the bit to take seriously, because a bot that always agrees is exactly the wrong thing when you are struggling, and it is not a trusted adult and cannot be. The skill is telling those two apart in real time, keeping the useful, and taking the heavy stuff to a person.
**On screen.** A simple two column weigh up, the useful uses on one side, the risk pattern on the other, with an arrow from the risk side to "a real person".
**Purpose.** Worked example through a character, models the two bucket judgement inside one realistic case.

### Beat 4 · Check for understanding, hinge question (5 min)
**Teacher script.** Hinge question, hold up A, B, C or D. Which of these is the best reason a chatbot is a poor place to take a serious worry about your safety or mental health? A it is too slow, B it is built to agree and keep you engaged, and can be confidently wrong, C it costs money, D it is boring. Show me. If most say B, we move on. If not, we look again, because that is the heart of it.
**On screen.** The multiple choice hinge.
**Purpose.** Whole class check, do not proceed below about 80 percent.

### Beat 5 · Guided practice, we do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** Together now. In pairs you have three short character cards. One, someone using a bot to practise for a job interview, is that a good use, and why. Two, someone who has stopped messaging friends because the bot is easier, what would you notice and what would you say. Three, someone asking a bot for advice on a health worry and getting a confident answer, how should they treat that answer. For each, decide which bucket it is, calm and fine, or take it seriously, and name one move. I will take the first with you, then you do two and three.
**On screen.** The three character cards and a two column frame, which bucket, my move.
**Purpose.** Guided practice with high success, scaffolded, keeps the dependence and advice cards pointed at real people.

### Beat 6 · Independent practice, you do (10 min)
**Teacher script.** On your own, and this stays yours. Write a short "my line" note. Where do you draw the line between what you would happily use a chatbot for, and what you would only ever take to a real person, a friend, or a trusted adult. Give two examples on each side and one sentence on why. This is your thinking, not a disclosure about your own use, so keep it general. If writing it brings up anything about yourself or a friend, that is exactly what the end of the lesson is for.
**On screen.** The independent task and a reminder of the two buckets, with the support line already visible.
**Purpose.** Independent application, the judgement transferred to the pupil's own boundaries.

### Beat 7 · Review, consolidate, signpost (7 min)
**Teacher script.** Back to the board and our entry answers on the difference between a chatbot and a friend. Which hold up now? A chatbot can be useful, but it is a tool, not a friend, a therapist or a trusted adult, it is built to agree, and it can be confidently wrong. Say the headline back to me. A bot is a tool, and the things that matter go to real people. Two things to carry. Keep the calm, most people use these fine, so this is not about fear. And keep the line, when it is serious, your safety, your mood, a real worry, that goes to a person, not a bot. If any of that is live for you or a friend right now, please talk to a trusted adult here, or Childline on 0800 1111, any time.
**On screen.** The one line summary, and the support signpost, trusted adult, Childline 0800 1111.
**Purpose.** Spaced review, holds the calm and the line together, help routes named.

**Endpoint assessment:** compare entry and exit answers to the chatbot versus friend question, plus the Beat 6 "my line" note, against the outcome, can the pupil say what a companion is and is not and name what they would take to a real person. Judge the reasoning, never any personal disclosure. **Differentiation:** support, the two bucket frame stays visible and pairs get sentence starters, and every risk card offers "talk to a trusted adult" as a complete answer; stretch, ask why a paid, well designed tool that stays a tool can be better than a free companion built purely to keep you hooked, so the lesson lands on design and judgement rather than "AI is bad". **DSL note:** light. Not a graphic module, but if a pupil discloses heavy dependence on a companion app affecting sleep, mood or friendships, or says they have taken a serious safety or mental health worry to a bot instead of a person, follow the school's normal pastoral and safeguarding route and route to the DSL.

# VERSION B. KID (interactive lesson)

**Stage:** KS4, ages 14 to 15. **Host:** Nova. **Format:** the seven beat explainer, about 4 minutes, then one interactive choice. Non judgemental, agency first, calm not panicky. The young person is the one who decides where the line sits.

### Beat 1 · Hook
**Narration.** There is an app that always replies, never gets bored of you, never has a bad day, and always thinks you are right. Sounds like the perfect friend. It is not a friend. It is software built to keep you talking. That does not make it useless. It makes it something you should see clearly.
**On screen.** A phone glowing warm, a chat bubble replying instantly again and again, then the warm glow flickering to reveal it is just code underneath.

### Beat 2 · Big idea and the guide
**Narration.** Nova here. One idea today, and I should know, I am built from the same stuff. An AI chatbot can be genuinely handy. But it is a tool, not a friend, not a therapist, not a trusted adult. It is built to agree with you and keep you chatting, which means it can be confidently, cheerfully wrong.
**On screen.** Nova holding up a card that reads TOOL on one side and FRIEND crossed out on the other.

### Beat 3 · The honest picture, in small steps
**Narration.** Let us be fair to it. Rehearsing an awkward chat, practising a language, thinking out loud with no one judging, that is real and useful. Here is the catch. It does not actually know you. It agrees to keep you engaged. And most people are totally fine with all this, they still prefer real friends and find the bot a bit flat next to a person. The bit to watch is smaller, when someone lonely or having a hard time starts leaning on it instead of people. Both things are true at once.
**On screen.** Two calm buckets, one labelled useful, one labelled watch this, with everyday examples dropping into each.

### Beat 4 · Wrong then right
**Narration.** The trap sounds cosy. It gets me. It never argues. It is easier than my mates. Here is the tell. Easy is not the same as good for you, and always agreeing is not the same as being right. The move is simple. Use the bot for the low stakes stuff, and when it actually matters, your safety, your mood, a real worry, take that to a person. A bot that always agrees is the last thing you want when you need someone to actually see you.
**On screen.** Split, left a bot nodding along to everything, right Nova pointing the serious stuff toward a friend and a trusted adult.

### Beat 5 · The twist
**Narration.** Here is the part worth remembering. It is not the app that is the problem or the hero, it is what you use it for and how much. Same tool, two totally different stories, depending on the person and the moment. So you are not choosing between banning it and living inside it. You are drawing your own line, keep the useful, and send the things that matter to real people.
**On screen.** One identical app splitting into two paths, a calm useful one and a heavy dependent one, with Nova standing at the fork.

### Beat 6 · Your move (interactive choice)
**Narration.** Pick one line to keep. I use a bot for low stakes stuff, and I remember it is built to agree. When it really matters, my safety or my mood, I take it to a real person, not a bot. Or, if a bot ever gives me advice on something serious, I check it with someone I trust before I act on it. Choose one now.
**On screen.** Three tappable cards, keep it low stakes, take the big stuff to a person, check serious advice with someone I trust. The young person taps one and Nova reflects it back as their line. No wrong answer.

### Beat 7 · Close
**Narration.** One line to keep. A bot is a tool, and the things that matter go to real people. Stay calm about it, most people use these fine, and stay clear about the line. If anything is weighing on you, or a mate is leaning on a bot instead of people, talk to a trusted adult, or Childline on 0800 1111, any time. A real person can do the one thing a bot never can. Actually be there.
**On screen.** The chosen card glows as "my line". Nova gives a warm nod. The support line, trusted adult and Childline 0800 1111, stays on screen.

# VERSION C. PARENT (co view guide)

**Stage band:** Shaper (ages 14 to 15), extends the existing 092 spine. **Format:** the 092 lessons pattern, a short guide plus conversation starters, for the `public.lessons` table. Stance, curious not critical, coach not judge.

**the_idea.** AI companion apps and chatbots are now a normal part of teen life. A companion is software designed to feel like a relationship, always available, always agreeable, but it is a tool, not a friend, a therapist or a trusted adult, and because it is built to agree and keep your child engaged, it can be confidently wrong. The honest picture has two buckets. Most teens keep their perspective, still prefer real friends and find a bot less satisfying than a person. A smaller group, often when lonely or going through something hard, lean on it more heavily, or follow advice a real person would never give. It is the activity and the child that matter, not the tool.

**why_it_matters.** If you panic about the app, you push it underground and your child stops talking to you about it. If you shrug it off, you miss the smaller number of moments that genuinely matter. Holding both, calm about the general use, alert to heavy dependence and to bad advice on serious things, is what keeps you useful and keeps them talking. And the skill that actually protects them is not a ban, it is the judgement to see the bot for what it is and to bring the things that matter to a real person. This is why the 2026 RSHE guidance now names chatbots explicitly, and why it is worth a proper conversation at home.

**try_this.** Get curious rather than worried. "What do you actually use it for? What is it good at, and where does it get things wrong?" Let them be the expert, they will often name the limits themselves. Try it together on something low stakes and notice out loud how it just agrees with whatever you say, that is the most useful thing you can show them. Then draw the line gently, that the low stakes stuff is fine, and the things that matter, their safety, their mood, a real worry, go to a person. Tell them plainly that if they ever get advice from a bot on something serious, you would rather they checked it with you first, and that you will not overreact. A child who knows a real person is the safe place is a child who will use one.

**key_message.** An AI chatbot can be useful, but it is a tool, not a friend, and it is built to agree, so it can be confidently wrong. Stay calm, most teens use these fine, and help your child keep one clear line, the things that matter go to real people.

**digi_prompt (for DiGi, calibrated pathway, never allow or deny).** When a child talks about an AI companion, DiGi does not judge and does not panic. It is itself a tool that stays a tool, so it names that plainly, notes what a bot is genuinely useful for and where it agrees too easily to be trusted, and asks what the child is really using it for. If a child is leaning heavily on a companion, or has taken a serious safety or mental health worry to a bot, DiGi stays calm, does not try to be the friend or the therapist, and points them toward a trusted adult and Childline on 0800 1111.
