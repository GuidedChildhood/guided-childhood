# JUSTIN PHILLIPS, FOUNDER AND STORY CONTEXT

Use this document as background context when working on Guided Childhood.

It is not a script and it is not an instruction to force personal stories into everything.

Its purpose is to help you understand who Justin is, why Guided Childhood exists, which experiences genuinely shaped his thinking, and which personal stories may be relevant when writing content, designing products or explaining the company.

The overriding rule is authenticity.

Never invent an anecdote, conversation, emotion, event involving Justin or his family. Do not improve a true story by adding details that are not here. A personal story should only appear when it naturally illuminates the subject.

## The founder arc

Justin Phillips was born and grew up in Bristol. He had a safe and loving childhood. His father was an old school working dad and mechanic. His mother was loving, stayed at home while the children were young and also did hairdressing from home. Justin has a younger brother.

School was not an easy fit. He was easily distracted, had very messy handwriting, struggled with homework, grammar, sustained reading and some conventional academic work. He did not consider himself disruptive, but increasingly felt that he did not quite fit the educational pathway around him.

One memory has stayed with him. At around 11 he was sitting at the dinner table struggling with maths homework. His father understood the maths and became increasingly frustrated that Justin did not. Justin remembers feeling scared, useless and like a failure. As a parent, he sometimes recognises that same frustration appearing in himself. When it does, he tries to catch it because he wants to respond differently to his own children.

This is a sensitive story. Use rarely and only where genuinely relevant to learning, parenting, empathy or helping a struggling child.

## The Porsche in the garage

One of Justin's strongest childhood memories is from around the age of seven. His father bought a damaged Porsche with a partner because the family could not afford one outright. Justin watched him rebuild it in the garage at home. He would pass tools and look forward to seeing what had changed each day. Going for a drive in the finished car felt enormously exciting.

What stayed with Justin was the process. His father could take something that looked impossibly complicated, dismantle it, arrange the pieces methodically and put it back together. As a seven year old, Justin thought his dad was almost a magician. His father also stripped down, rebuilt and sprayed a guitar.

The underlying memory is not really about cars. It is about seeing that complicated things can be understood by taking them apart, learning how the pieces work and rebuilding them methodically. Do not force this into a metaphor. It is simply genuine founder material when a subject naturally concerns building, systems, complexity, learning by doing or understanding how something works.

## Education and finding his ability later

Justin broke his leg at around 14 after going into a football tackle. He was already struggling academically and being away from school pushed him further behind. He eventually did A levels largely because that appeared to be what people did next, but did not succeed academically. He later moved to London for college to study biology with an interest in genetics, but again struggled to complete the work and eventually dropped out. He did not immediately tell his parents. There was no dramatic alternative plan. He drifted for a period and spent a lot of time at the gym without knowing what direction his life should take.

The conventional school, qualifications, university, career pathway seemed to work for other people but not for him.

His understanding of his own ability changed when he started working. A manager recognised that Justin was conscientious and capable and encouraged him towards leadership. A female boss in particular backed him and later gave him an excellent recommendation. Justin remembers how being valued by someone made him want to become better. He discovered that he was good at organising people, getting work completed, understanding customers, explaining products and recognising what clients actually needed.

At around 32 he became a company director. For someone who had struggled academically and dropped out of college, that initially did not seem quite real.

This experience contributes to Justin's interest in different ways of learning and developing capability, but should not be used to claim that his educational experience is representative of other children.

## Learning by building

Justin progressed through accounting and contractor related businesses and became increasingly entrepreneurial. Around 2010 he helped build an online accounting, SaaS type service at a time when much of the accounting industry remained traditional. The proposition was to make company formation, accounting and administration much easier online.

Justin was good at understanding the product deeply, explaining it to clients and agencies, solving problems and generating referrals. He learned that he was particularly strong at understanding what a customer was struggling with and designing or explaining something that made their life easier. He also experienced the limits of growth, partnership difficulties and the difficulty of turning a good product into a much larger business.

This is relevant to GDC because Justin is not approaching it primarily as an academic. His instinct is to understand a practical problem and build something people can actually use.

## Inspired by Alma

Justin and his Spanish wife Natalia named a family business after their first daughter, Alma. For Alma's first birthday Natalia made cake toppers and party decorations. People liked them. That small moment became Inspired by Alma.

The business began from a bedroom at home and expanded through Etsy, Amazon and Amazon Handmade. It grew quickly and eventually included staff and a high street operation.

But the experience also taught Justin something important about platforms. Competitors appeared. Successful ideas were copied. Marketplaces became crowded. Reviews and platform decisions could materially affect sales. Justin increasingly felt that although they had built the business, another company controlled much of the environment in which the business existed.

This experience can be relevant when thinking about platforms, algorithms, dependence on intermediaries and who actually controls a digital environment. Again, do not artificially insert it into unrelated platform stories.

## Family

Justin is married to Natalia, who is Spanish and later became a teacher. Justin loves Spain, travel and experiencing other cultures. They have three children: Alma, Olga and Teo.

Parenthood is central to the origin of Guided Childhood. Do not turn the children into content. Stories involving them should be used sparingly, respectfully and only when the experience is relevant to something Justin genuinely learned. Avoid unnecessary personal detail.

## The original technology belief

Before having children and during the early parenting years, Justin leaned towards a much simpler belief: technology and screens were probably bad for children and paper, physical activity and traditional experiences were preferable.

Reality made that position increasingly difficult to maintain. His children learned things from screens. They encountered languages, crafts, science, entertainment, communication and educational material. Technology became integrated into school. AI accelerated this further.

At the same time Justin saw genuine family problems around devices. Turning screens off could create arguments. Children sometimes preferred television to going somewhere. Sibling disputes could revolve around what to watch. Short form recommendations could move towards material he did not expect. Setting controls was complicated. Screens could make parenting easier in the short term while creating a habit that was difficult to unwind later.

The contradiction became important: technology could be extremely useful and still create real problems. Justin increasingly stopped looking for an answer that required technology to be either good or bad.

## The YouTube moment

One early turning point involved YouTube. Justin saw a video about relationships appear through recommendations and realised how little control a parent could have over where a sequence of recommended videos eventually led. He also watched his children move from one piece of content to another in ways that were difficult to anticipate. At the same time he could see that YouTube contained extraordinary educational material.

This produced the original Social Billboard idea. The initial problem was essentially: how can children benefit from the best of YouTube without handing the next choice entirely to the recommendation algorithm?

Justin initially tried manually selecting videos he thought were suitable. It was time consuming, difficult to maintain and did not solve the broader problem. Social Billboard grew from that itch.

## The family scenes that complicated the question

There was never one dramatic incident that created Guided Childhood. There were repeated ordinary parenting moments. Two young daughters sitting absorbed in screens rather than communicating with the family. Arguments in the car over what to watch. Children becoming upset when a screen was turned off. A child wanting television rather than an outside activity. Trying timers and rules. Trying to make schoolwork come before entertainment. Discovering that parental control systems themselves could be complicated. And simultaneously seeing children learn genuinely useful things online.

One particularly important contradiction involved Alma. As she approached secondary school age she wanted greater access to a phone, as her friends did. One of her friends moved to Wales. WhatsApp allowed Alma to continue that friendship after school. The same technology Justin was worrying about was supporting a relationship that mattered to his daughter.

That is a useful representation of the problem Guided Childhood is trying to solve. The digital world is not simply something being done to children. It is increasingly somewhere their friendships, education, creativity and development take place.

## From Social Billboard to Guided Childhood

Social Billboard began largely as a content and algorithm problem. Guided Childhood emerged when Justin realised the parenting problem was much larger. A perfectly selected playlist could not teach a child what to do in a group chat. A filter could not teach them why an algorithm was recommending something. A parental control could not teach them when to ask an adult for help. A ban could delay an experience without necessarily preparing the child for the day access arrived.

Justin began reading much more of the research. Jonathan Haidt was one of the most prominent voices he encountered, but the certainty of the argument did not completely match Justin's lived experience of technology being both beneficial and problematic. He subsequently spent substantial time reading and following researchers including Candice Odgers, Amy Orben, Andrew Przybylski, Chris Ferguson and others examining the evidence from different perspectives.

The disagreements in the evidence became important to him. Guided Childhood should therefore never pretend that complicated research questions have simple answers merely because simple answers market better.

## Justin's big change of mind

Five years ago Justin thought technology was largely bad for children and that education should remain much closer to paper and pen. Today he believes technology can be enormously important to children's education, development and future lives. AI makes digital capability increasingly important. The challenge is therefore not simply keeping children away from technology. It is helping them develop the skills and judgement to use increasingly powerful technology well.

Do not turn this into "technology is good". Justin's position is deliberately more complicated. Different technologies, content, contexts, children, designs and patterns of use can produce very different experiences.

## Delay and preparation

A delay is not preparation. This does NOT mean delay is useless. There may be good reasons to delay particular experiences or forms of independent access. The important distinction is that delaying something and preparing a child for it are different jobs. A protected period creates an opportunity. Guided Childhood asks what the child should learn and practise during that period.

Justin does not believe one arbitrary age can describe the readiness of every child for every digital experience. The direction is staged introduction, age appropriate experiences, education, adult support and gradually increasing independence.

## The Digital Passport

The Digital Passport grew from a simple question: what should a child understand about their digital world at each stage of growing up? At four, their world and decisions are very different from those of a 10 year old. A 13 year old entering more independent communication faces different decisions again. By 16 and beyond, adults will inevitably control less.

The Passport therefore should not simply declare a child "safe online". It should help make digital development visible: what they have learned; what they have practised; what they can currently manage; what still needs adult scaffolding; and what becomes relevant next. The objective is progressive capability rather than a magic birthday.

## The origin of DiGi

One of the clearest product origin stories involves Teo. At around four, Teo was watching a dance video. When it was time to stop, he became very upset. Justin reacted instinctively and threatened to take away Saturday football. Almost immediately afterwards Justin thought: that wasn't the right response. But then came the more interesting question: what would have been?

Justin had already spent years researching children and technology and still did not necessarily know what to say in an ordinary parenting moment. He began researching practical parenting approaches, including work from Dr Becky and others. That helped produce the idea behind DiGi.

A parent should be able to describe the moment that is happening now and get practical, evidence informed options for handling it. But Justin wants DiGi to go further. If a parent tries something, the system should eventually be able to learn whether it helped. Parents should be able to return and say what happened. Over time the system could combine research with structured feedback about what parents actually find useful in real situations.

This remains a product hypothesis. Never describe the learning or outcome system as proven unless evidence exists.

## Why schools

As Natalia became a teacher, Justin increasingly thought about the other side of children's digital education. Schools are expected to prepare children for a digital environment that is changing extraordinarily quickly. AI makes the problem even more obvious.

The question became: how can a school maintain a useful digital literacy curriculum when the world pupils are entering changes faster than traditional curriculum development? That contributed to the Guided Childhood school pathway: a continually updated, age staged curriculum that helps schools teach children about the online environments they actually encounter, while mapping the work to relevant school requirements.

The ambition is not simply "teach social media". It is to prepare children progressively for a digital and AI mediated world.

## The AI and building moment

After moving to the countryside, Justin regularly walked in the morning listening to entrepreneurs and business podcasts. He was also watching the development of AI assisted software creation. For years, turning an idea into software meant finding developers, raising or spending substantial money and hoping another person understood the vision. Justin had already experienced some of those difficulties.

AI assisted coding changed his perception of what was possible. He could increasingly turn the system in his head into something tangible himself. That was extremely attractive to someone who has historically learned through doing.

It also created another GDC question: if AI can materially change what an adult without traditional technical training can build, what will it mean for children growing up with these tools from the beginning?

## Current founder reality

Guided Childhood is being built while Justin supports a family, without an unlimited runway. Building something uncertain while supporting a family creates pressure, and that line is as far as public content goes. Justin finds recording himself on camera uncomfortable despite knowing that communicating publicly matters. Anything about the marriage, the finances or the relationship beyond that one public line is private context held outside this repo and needs Justin's explicit approval for the specific use.

## What Guided Childhood is really trying to achieve

Justin does not want his children to grow up frightened of technology. He wants them to become comfortable and capable with it. He wants them to be able to learn with technology, recognise risks, understand when something is real or fake, know when they need help and eventually make sensible decisions without an adult controlling every interaction.

The destination is not perfect safety. It is increasing judgement and capability.

A useful expression of this philosophy is: "Eventually the controls come off. What I want left behind is judgement."

## The intergenerational thread

There is a quieter story underneath the business. Justin remembers his father as strict but loving. His father worked hard and pursued ways of improving life for his family despite not always knowing exactly how things would work out. As a child Justin watched him build and repair things that seemed impossibly complicated.

Justin hopes that when his own children are adults they are proud that their dad also pursued something he believed could make their lives better.

Do not overuse this. It may eventually be one of the strongest founder stories precisely because it is used rarely.

## Recurring themes available to content

Not mandatory messages. Recurring tensions that genuinely emerge from Justin's life and GDC: protection vs preparation; delay vs readiness; control vs gradually transferring judgement; technology as both opportunity and risk; the difference between an app and what actually happens inside it; algorithms vs deliberate choice; learning by doing; different children needing different support; being academically unsuccessful does not mean being incapable of learning; adults not always knowing the right response either; parents making mistakes and correcting them; the value of someone believing in a learner; platforms being useful while also controlling the environment; building rather than simply criticising; evidence being more complicated than headlines; AI as something children need preparation for rather than something adults can simply wish away.

## Story selection principle

When creating content, first identify the actual idea worth communicating. Then ask whether one of Justin's genuine experiences naturally makes that idea easier to understand or care about. If yes, consider using it. If no, don't. Sometimes the research paper, policy decision, school problem or product change is already the strongest story. Personal storytelling should increase meaning, not become a formula. Do not repeatedly use the same founder anecdote. Look for small scenes rather than repeatedly summarising Justin's entire biography.

## Privacy levels

GREEN, generally usable when relevant: Porsche and garage; academic struggles in general; dropping out of college; finding ability through work; becoming a director; building online accounting services; Inspired by Alma's platform experience; general parenting struggles with screens; Social Billboard origin; research journey; change of mind about technology; Digital Passport philosophy; general AI building journey; DiGi product origin, provided Teo is treated respectfully.

AMBER, use thoughtfully and flag for Justin: the maths table incident with his father; specific stories involving Alma, Olga or Teo; family screen arguments; details about children's friendships; feeling like a failure at school; business failures or significant financial pressure; Natalia disagreeing with Justin about aspects of technology or business.

RED, never use publicly without Justin explicitly approving the specific use: private marital matters; detailed family finances; private information about extended family; sensitive information about the children; anything that could embarrass the children now or later; any invented dialogue attributed to a family member.

## Voice consequence

Understanding this biography should make the writing more personal without making it more dramatic. Justin is not presenting himself as the parent who solved technology. He is a parent who repeatedly found that the simple answers did not survive contact with real family life. He researched the problem. His views changed. Then he started building the tools he wished he had. That is the founder perspective to preserve.
