THE STORY
  proposed title: A new meta-analysis says the effect is much bigger. Here is how I decided not to use it.
  angle: A paper landed this week that would help the argument I make for a living, and I am not going to use it. Walking a parent through why is more useful than the number itself, because it is the same test they can apply to the next frightening headline.
  ONE STORY: A meta-analysis published on 6 November reports a correlation between social media use and worse mental health that is far larger than the best powered studies have found, and it would be very convenient for anybody selling a solution.
  ONE TENSION: The number is real and peer reviewed, and it still should not change what you believe, because twelve studies pooled with ninety four percent heterogeneity is not stronger evidence than a representative cohort of twenty five thousand.
  ONE GDC BELIEF: We judge a finding by its design and its spread, not by whether it points the way we already lean. We publish the ones that are awkward for us, which means we also have to decline the ones that flatter us.
  ONE TAKEAWAY: When a study says the effect is bigger than you were told, look for two things before you believe it: how many studies went in, and whether they agreed with each other.

FIGURES CLEARED FOR USE
  r = 0.308, 95% CI 0.239 to 0.374, p < 0.001, the pooled correlation
  I squared = 93.85 percent, the heterogeneity
  12 studies contributing 15 effect sizes
  subgroup difference Q = 9.72, p = 0.008
  stage estimates as reported: primary r = 0.367, high school r = 0.334, middle school r = 0.201

DO NOT QUOTE
  the sample size, which could not be extracted from the available text
  any total number of participants: the abstract gives the number of studies and effect sizes but no pooled N, and the dossier could not extract one
  'primary school children are most at risk', which the subgroup analysis cannot support
  any multiple comparing this to earlier estimates, because r and beta are different metrics
  any UK prevalence, since no UK breakdown is given and the search included CNKI

WHAT THIS DOES NOT PROVE
  That social media causes mental health problems in children. Every input is a correlation.
  That primary school aged children are the group most at risk.
  That the association is large in the general population, or in the UK.
  That the earlier small effect estimates were wrong. A twelve study pool with this heterogeneity does not overturn larger, better powered work.
  Anything about which children are affected, which is the question that actually matters to a parent.

LIMITATIONS
  I squared = 93.85 percent. The included studies disagree so much that one pooled correlation is close to meaningless as a summary.
  Twelve studies is a small base for any meta-analysis and far too small for a three way subgroup split.
  Correlation coefficients only. Any study design that reported something else is excluded, which biases the pool towards cross sectional work.
  No country breakdown in the abstract, with CNKI in the search, so the geographic mix is unknown and may be heavily Chinese.
  Exposure is 'social media use' as each original study defined it, which is the exact undifferentiated measure the protocol says not to accept. Duration, content, time of day and interaction are all collapsed.
  Mental health is likewise whatever each original study measured.
  Full text not read, so the study table, the weighting and the actual countries have not been checked.

CONTRADICTORY OR NULL EVIDENCE
  The Greater Manchester #BeeWell cohort, 25,629 teenagers, found no evidence that time on social media predicted later emotional difficulties. That is a single large representative longitudinal cohort against twelve pooled correlational studies, and on design it is the stronger evidence. It is already the basis of GDC-049.
  The 46 study, 79 effect size meta-analysis pooling to roughly beta = 0.061, which Przybylski has described as smaller than the effect of wearing glasses. Four independent groups reached broadly the same small estimate. That is the body of work this new paper sits against.
  Orben and Przybylski's specification curve analyses, which show how much an estimate in this field moves with analytic choices. With I squared at 93.85 percent, that critique applies to this pool with unusual force.

CLAIM BY CLAIM
  [VERIFIED] The pooled correlation between social media use and worse adolescent mental health was r = 0.308, 95% CI 0.239 to 0.374, p < 0.001.
      basis: Stated verbatim in the published abstract. The DOI resolves through Crossref and the online publication date, 6 November 2026, agrees with the index. This verifies what the paper reports, not that the figure is a good estimate of the real world association.
  [UNSUPPORTED] The association varies by educational stage, and primary school students are the most vulnerable group.
      basis: The subgroup test is Q = 9.72, p = 0.008, but it is splitting twelve studies and fifteen effect sizes across three school stages. A subgroup analysis on that few studies is badly underpowered and highly sensitive to which study sits in which bin. 'Most vulnerable' is also a clinical claim about children, and the underlying studies are correlational, so the design cannot carry it.
  [QUALIFIED] r = 0.308 is a reasonable estimate of the association between adolescent social media use and mental health.
      basis: Two reasons for caution, both from the abstract itself. The heterogeneity is I squared = 93.85 percent, which means the twelve studies disagree with each other enormously and a single pooled number describes none of them well. And the search included CNKI alongside the English language databases, so the sample is likely weighted towards Chinese cohorts, which the abstract does not break down. The funder is the National Social Science Foundation of China. None of that makes the figure wrong, and all of it has to be said before it is repeated.
  [QUALIFIED] This finding is larger than the association reported by the best powered work in this field.
      basis: It is substantially larger than the pooled estimates we have already verified, which sit near beta = 0.061 across 46 studies and 79 effect sizes. But r and a standardised beta are not the same quantity, so the comparison is directional rather than a clean ratio. Do NOT write that it is five or six times bigger. Write that it is much larger and that the metrics differ.
  [UNSUPPORTED] Social media use has mental health effects on adolescents.
      basis: The paper's own title says 'Effects' while its methods pool correlation coefficients. The abstract body correctly says 'associated with' throughout. The title overclaims relative to the design, which is worth noting precisely because the title is what gets quoted.

EVIDENCE CONFIDENCE
  LOW to MODERATE for the pooled figure as a description of these twelve studies. LOW as a guide to what is true of UK children. The design is sound for what it is, a random effects meta-analysis with publication bias assessment and leave one out sensitivity testing, and the reported stability is in its favour. The heterogeneity, the small number of studies and the absent country breakdown are what hold it down.

UNRESOLVED VERIFICATION QUESTIONS
  Which countries the twelve studies are from, and how much of the pooled estimate is Chinese cohort data. The abstract does not say and the full text was not read.
  The pooled N. Not in the abstract.
  Whether the three stage bins each contain enough studies to be worth reporting. Needs the study table.
  Whether any of the twelve overlap in sample with each other, which the protocol specifically warns about for pooled work.
