THE STORY
  proposed title: Researchers asked parents how they would feel about an AI leading their child's therapy session. Three in four were not comfortable.
  angle: Open on the journal and the finding with its numbers. Then be exact about what was asked: one hypothetical question about AI led behavioural therapy, in an opt in sample of parents in the United States. It is not a verdict on AI and children in general. I build a parent tool with an AI guide in it, so say that plainly, and say where the line is: guidance for a parent is not therapy for a child, and nothing we make diagnoses or treats. Then give parents what the survey says they would look for.
  ONE STORY: A survey published in JAMA Pediatrics asked parents in the United States how comfortable they would be with a therapy session for their child led by AI. Of 572 who answered, 47.2 percent were not comfortable at all and 28.8 percent were not too comfortable.
  ONE TENSION: The parents most at ease were the ones who would accept AI therapy for themselves, and using AI day to day made no difference. So this looks less like fear of something unfamiliar and more like a judgement about where AI belongs.
  ONE GDC BELIEF: A parent should be able to tell what a tool is for and where it stops. An AI that helps a parent think is a different thing from an AI treating a child, and a product should say which it is.
  ONE TAKEAWAY: Before your child uses any AI tool for something that matters, ask what the survey's parents said they would ask: is it accurate, who is overseeing it, and where does what my child says go.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT PROVE
  That AI led therapy is unsafe or does not work for children.
  That parents distrust AI with their children in general.
  That UK parents feel the same way.
  That children share their parents' view. Children were not asked.
  That anything we build is trusted, safe or better. It was not studied.

LIMITATIONS
  Nonprobability, opt in sample. Weighting does not make it a random one.
  Modest size, 572, with wide intervals and no reliable subgroup results.
  Stated comfort with a hypothetical. It may not reflect what parents would do, or what is appropriate or safe. The authors say this themselves.
  Cross sectional, so nothing causal.
  United States only.
  Familiarity with AI in health care was not measured.
  A Research Letter, so short, with limited detail on the questions asked.

CONTRADICTORY OR NULL EVIDENCE
  From the paper: about a quarter of parents, 16.2 plus 7.8 percent, were somewhat or very comfortable. Acceptance is not negligible.
  From the paper: comfort for the child rose sharply among parents comfortable with AI led therapy for themselves and among those who expect AI to do better than professionals. Attitudes may shift as use spreads.
  From the paper: everyday AI use by parents and children made no difference, which cuts against the easy story that people fear what they do not know.
  From the paper: the authors concede that comfort for self and comfort for child may be one attitude measured twice.

MEDIA COVERAGE
  Coverage was not searched in this run. The predictable errors are 'three in four parents reject AI for their kids' and 'parents do not trust AI with children'. The question was about AI leading a behavioural therapy session, and nothing wider.

CLAIM BY CLAIM
  [VERIFIED] Of 3,102 respondents, 584 had at least one child aged 5 to 17, and 572 answered the main question about comfort.
      basis: Results, full text: among 3102 respondents, 584 had at least 1 child aged 5 to 17 years, and 572 responded to the primary outcome.
  [VERIFIED] Weighted, 47.2 percent were not comfortable at all, 28.8 percent not too comfortable, 16.2 percent somewhat comfortable and 7.8 percent very comfortable with a therapy session for their child led by AI.
      basis: Results, full text, with 95 percent intervals of 42.1 to 52.3, 24.5 to 33.6, 12.7 to 20.5 and 5.3 to 11.3. The intervals are wide and should be mentioned in words.
  [QUALIFIED] Three in four parents were not comfortable.
      basis: 47.2 plus 28.8 is 76.0. The paper does not print the sum, so it is ours and is written as about three in four, never as a figure the study reported.
  [QUALIFIED] Parents most often chose accuracy of diagnosis (58.5 percent), level of empathy (49.4 percent), recommendation from a medical professional (46.8 percent) and confidentiality and privacy (43.7 percent) as what would matter most.
      basis: Verified in the full text, with a correction to the candidate: this item was answered by 546 parents, not 572, and parents could choose more than one.
  [VERIFIED] It was a cross sectional survey of adults in the United States in November 2025, using a nonprobability sample.
      basis: Methods: a cross sectional survey of US adults in November 2025 through a participatory surveillance platform, a nonprobability river sample, weighted towards census targets.
  [VERIFIED] Everyday use of AI by parents and children was not associated with comfort.
      basis: Stated in the results. Comfort tracked the parent's comfort with AI led therapy for themselves, which the authors concede may be the same underlying attitude measured twice.
  [UNSUPPORTED] Parents do not trust AI with their children.
      basis: Too wide. The question was about AI led behavioural therapy only. Nothing was asked about chatbots, companions, schoolwork or tools for parents.
  [UNSUPPORTED] AI led therapy is unsafe or does not work for children.
      basis: The survey measured stated comfort with a hypothetical. It did not measure safety, effectiveness, use or any outcome for a child.
