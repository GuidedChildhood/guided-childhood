THE STORY
  proposed title: Ask 13 and 14 year olds how they protect themselves online and about a third say they talk to an adult. By 17 and 18 it is fewer than one in five.
  angle: The source is the European Commission's own survey, Flash Eurobarometer 579, the youth tables (volume B, question 11), which a preprint from a German university hospital then analysed. Say it is a survey of young people in the 27 EU countries in spring 2026, not the UK. The question asked young people who use social media on a school day what they do to protect their mental wellbeing there. Talking to a parent, teacher or another adult was chosen by 32 percent of 13 and 14 year olds, 28 percent of 15 and 16 year olds and 18 percent of 17 and 18 year olds, while reporting content and taking breaks went up with age. Be exact that these are three different groups of teenagers at one moment, not the same children followed over time, and that the answer bundles parents with teachers and other adults. The tension: older teenagers see more of the hard stuff, AI fakes and unwanted sexual content among it, and are less likely to name an adult as part of how they cope. Our belief: the open door is built before it is needed, when they are younger and still say yes to it, not at 17. Say once that I build a parent tool and that one of its first parent lessons gives the line for that. Close on the takeaway for parents.
  ONE STORY: The European Commission asked 26,297 young people aged 13 to 18 across the EU about life on social media in spring 2026. Asked what they do to protect their wellbeing there, 32 percent of 13 and 14 year olds said they talk to a parent, teacher or another adult. Among 17 and 18 year olds it was 18 percent.
  ONE TENSION: The older groups reported more of the difficult encounters, such as AI content that was hard to recognise (47 percent at 17 and 18 against 34 percent at 13 and 14) and unwanted sexualised content (23 against 14), and were the least likely to name an adult as one of the ways they protect themselves.
  ONE GDC BELIEF: The open door is something a parent builds early, in calm moments, before it is needed. Connection is the protection.
  ONE TAKEAWAY: Tonight, while they are still young enough to say yes to it, tell your child: if a screen ever gives you that uh oh feeling, you can always come to me, and you will never be in trouble for telling me.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT PROVE
  That teenagers stop confiding in their parents as they grow.
  That talking to an adult protects a teenager better than reporting or taking breaks.
  That an open door conversation at 10 changes what a teenager does at 17.
  Anything about UK teenagers or about children under 13.
  That Guided Childhood changes any of this.

LIMITATIONS
  Cross sectional. Age group differences are not change within the same young people and may partly reflect cohort.
  Question 11 was asked only of young people who use social media on a typical school day.
  The option is talk to a parent, teacher or another adult, so parents cannot be separated out.
  Multiple answers were allowed and the question does not measure how often, when, or whether it helped.
  EU 27 only. The UK was not surveyed.
  The analysis paper is a preprint and not peer reviewed, though the figures used here come from the Commission's own tables.
  A finding that flatters our thesis, connection is the protection, so it was checked against the primary tables rather than taken from the preprint.

CONTRADICTORY OR NULL EVIDENCE
  In the same survey 75 percent of young people said parents or relatives do enough to protect young people's mental wellbeing online, against 52 percent for social media platforms (question 12a and 12c, EU27), so the picture is not one of teenagers giving up on parents.
  Older teenagers also more often chose self directed steps such as reporting content and taking breaks, which the authors read as growing independence, a normal and healthy part of adolescence rather than a failure.
  Seeking professional help barely changed with age (12 to 15 percent), so the fall is specific to the adult option, not to help seeking in general.

MEDIA COVERAGE
  Coverage was not searched in this run; the preprint was posted yesterday. The likely misreading is 'teenagers stop talking to their parents', which turns three age groups at one moment into a trajectory and drops teachers and other adults from the option. Avoid it.

CLAIM BY CLAIM
  [VERIFIED] The youth survey covered 26,297 young people aged 13 to 18 in the 27 EU member states, fieldwork 30 March to 16 April 2026.
      basis: Official tables, volume AA and B: base EU27 (All) 26,297 unweighted and weighted; fieldwork dates printed on every sheet.
  [QUALIFIED] Talking to a parent, teacher or another adult, as a way to protect wellbeing on social media, was chosen by 32 percent of 13 to 14 year olds, 28 percent of 15 to 16 year olds and 18 percent of 17 to 18 year olds.
      basis: Official volume B, question 11. Correct, but the base is young people who use social media on a typical school day (23,595 unweighted), not all 26,297; the answer groups parents with teachers and other adults; and it is one of several options that could all be chosen. The EU27 figure is 26 percent.
  [VERIFIED] Reporting harmful content or accounts rose from 21 percent at 13 to 14 to 30 percent at 17 to 18, and taking breaks from 28 to 35 percent.
      basis: Official volume B, question 11, same base.
  [VERIFIED] In the past three months, AI generated content that was hard to recognise was reported by 34 percent of 13 to 14 year olds and 47 percent of 17 to 18 year olds, and unwanted sexualised content by 14 and 23 percent.
      basis: Official volume B, question 10, same base. The question covers content seen or experienced, so it is exposure, not victimisation.
  [UNSUPPORTED] Teenagers stop talking to their parents as they get older.
      basis: Cross sectional: three different age groups at one moment, not the same young people followed. The authors say the pattern may reflect development, cumulative experience or cohort, and cannot show change within a person. The item also bundles parents with teachers and other adults.
  [UNSUPPORTED] Older teenagers who see more risk are the ones who do not seek adult help.
      basis: The tables are aggregates and cannot link answers within the same person. The authors call this a hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.

EVIDENCE CONFIDENCE
  HIGH that the European Commission's tables say what is written here: every figure was read in the official weighted tables. MODERATE as evidence for the point being made: a large official survey, but cross sectional, self reported, multiple choice, EU only, and the adult option bundles parents with teachers and others.
