\=== LINKEDIN POST ===

A new study landed that would make my argument easier to sell.

  

And I am not going to use its headline number to frighten parents.

  

The paper is a peer reviewed meta analysis of social media use and adolescent mental health. It reports a pooled correlation of r = 0.308.

  

If you already believe social media is harming children, that might sound like the confirmation you have been waiting for.

  

I work on helping families navigate childhood technology. A finding like this could be convenient for me.

  

That is precisely why I want to examine it more carefully.

  

The analysis combines 12 studies contributing 15 effect sizes. More importantly, it reports heterogeneity of I squared = 93.85 percent.

  

In plain English, the findings vary enormously between the included studies. Putting them together produces a number, but it does not make the underlying disagreement disappear.

  

There is another distinction worth making.

  

A correlation is not proof that social media caused a mental health difficulty. And a pooled measure of social media use cannot tell us what a particular child watched, who they spoke to, what happened at bedtime, or what else was going on in their life.

  

The authors also report differences across educational stages. That is interesting to investigate, but it is not a sound basis for declaring younger children the most vulnerable.

  

I am not saying the study should be dismissed. It deserves to be read, questioned and compared with other evidence.

  

I am saying that a result pointing in the direction we prefer deserves the same scrutiny as a result that challenges us.

  

Parents do not need another frightening statistic stripped of context. Schools do not need an apparently definitive answer built on studies that disagree.

  

We need better questions about content, relationships, timing and individual circumstances.

  

The next time a headline announces a bigger effect, I would look beyond the headline and ask how many studies were combined and how consistently they agreed.

  

If a finding supports what we already believe, are we checking it as carefully as the findings that do not?

The strongest objection is that a peer reviewed meta analysis can still provide useful evidence even when studies differ. Agreed. This is not a rejection of the paper or its reported correlation. The caution is about treating a highly heterogeneous pool of correlational findings as a settled causal answer for individual children.
